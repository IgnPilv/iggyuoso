# Ear training and pitch training for adult learners (zero to high-intermediate), browser piano app with MIDI input

Scope note: the research ran to about 16 tool calls. Several primary sources (full ABRSM/RCM syllabi, app documentation pages for Teoria, Perfect Ear, Complete Ear Trainer and Earpeggio, and the full text of some studies) were not retrieved. Claims that rest only on general pedagogical knowledge are listed under Inferences or Gaps, not as cited findings.

## 1. Which relative-pitch skills to teach, and in what order

### Takeaway
Graded exams and established curricula agree on one order. Start with pulse/metre and same-or-different and higher-or-lower judgments. Then move to short echo melodies (stepwise, narrow range), major vs minor, and intervals (melodic before harmonic). Next come chord qualities, then cadences (perfect/imperfect, then plagal and interrupted/deceptive), then seventh chords and progressions. Melodic and rhythmic dictation and playback by ear grow alongside these, starting with 2-bar phrases and reaching 4-bar question-and-answer phrases at the intermediate level. Functional (scale-degree-in-key) hearing is the strongest single backbone and can start early.

### Cited findings
- ABRSM Grade 1 aural has four tests. (1) Clap the beat of a piece in 2 or 3 time, stressing strong beats, and say whether it is in 2 or 3. (2) Echo-sing three two-bar major-key phrases with a range from tonic to mediant (3 notes). (3) Spot a pitch change in a two-bar major melody (at the beginning or the end). (4) Musical features: dynamics, articulation. — [Jade Bultitude, ABRSM Grade 1 guide](https://jadebultitude.com/abrsm-aural-tests-grade-1/)
- ABRSM Grade 3 keeps the same four test types, extended. Beat in 2, 3 or 4 time. Echo melodies within an octave, mostly stepwise, intervals no larger than a third. Spot a change of either pitch or rhythm in a melody of up to 4 bars. Features now include tempo and major vs minor. — [Jade Bultitude, ABRSM Grade 3 guide](https://jadebultitude.com/abrsm-aural-test-grade-3/)
- ABRSM Grade 6 has four tests. 6A: repeat (sing or play back) the upper part of a phrase after a key chord and a count-in; it is played twice. 6B: sight-sing with accompaniment. 6C: cadences, perfect (V–I, final) vs imperfect (ends on V, unfinished). 6D: structure/texture, style/period, character, major/minor, tempo, dynamics; then clap a rhythm and name the time as 2, 3 or 4. The guide notes that the format changes at Grade 6. — [e-musicmaestro ABRSM Grade 6 guide](https://www.e-musicmaestro.com/auraltests/guides/abrsm-grade-6-guide)
- A US state graded syllabus (UMTA AIM) at Level 8 is a useful high-intermediate benchmark. Scales: major, Mixolydian, pentatonic, whole-tone. Intervals: P4, P5, tritone, both broken and blocked. Seventh-chord qualities: MM7, Mm7 (dominant), mm7, dim7. Cadences: plagal I–IV–I, authentic I–V7–I, deceptive I–V7–vi, half I–IV–V. Rhythmic dictation with sixteenths and dotted notes, played 4 times. Two-voice melodic dictation (write the soprano over a given alto), played 4 times at MM 54. Scales, intervals, chords and cadences are each played twice. — [UMTA AIM Ear Training Level 8 worksheet](https://www.usu.edu/ycpiano/files/aim/ETlevel8.pdf)
- In RCM playback/melodic improvisation (Levels 5–10), the examiner names key and time signature, plays the tonic chord once and plays a 4-bar question phrase three times. The student answers to make an 8-bar parallel period (Levels 5–6) or contrasting period (7–8). At Levels 9–10 the student is given only a 2-bar opening and must complete the question phrase too. — [RCM Piano Syllabus Supplement](https://teacherportal.rcmusic.com/getattachment/0b9e372d-f0cf-49e1-9e94-be574cce6563/piano_syllabus_supplement.pdf)
- Karpinski's model of melodic dictation (as summarized by Chenette 2018) has four components in order: hearing (attention, perception); short-term melodic memory (limited, extended through extractive listening and chunking); musical understanding (labelling what is remembered); notation. Chenette argues that working memory underlies all four, and that a weakness at each stage needs its own remedy. — [Chenette 2018, JMTP vol. 32](https://journals.ou.edu/jmtp/article/download/543/1229/1221)
- The Functional Ear Trainer (Alain Benbassat's method) trains each tone's function within a key rather than intervals. The developer argues that this function is very similar across keys, so the skill transfers. The app is aimed at absolute beginners through professionals. — [Functional Ear Trainer, App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/id1088761926)
- ToneDear's functional exercises share one template: a short chord progression establishes the key, then a note (scale degrees), two notes (intervals in context) or a short melody (melodic dictation as scale degrees) is identified. It also offers isolated intervals, chords, scales, progressions and "perfect pitch". — [ToneDear](https://tonedear.com/)

### Inferences
- Proposed order for a 5-year track. It follows the exam benchmarks above, with gaps filled from standard practice.
  - Year 1: high/low and same/different, melodic direction (up/down/same), spotting a change, beat and metre (2/3/4). Play back 2–3-note stepwise echoes within do–mi, widening to do–sol, then to an octave. Major vs minor (tonic triad). First scale degrees in a major key with a cadence first: do, sol, then mi, then the rest.
  - Year 2: all diatonic scale degrees in major; melodic intervals within the octave, ascending before descending; triad quality major/minor; perfect vs imperfect cadence. Echo/playback of 2-bar, then 4-bar melodies. Simple rhythmic dictation.
  - Year 3: harmonic intervals; diminished and augmented triads; minor-key scale degrees; plagal and interrupted cadences; I–IV–V–I progressions; chromatic scale degrees; melodic dictation of 4 bars.
  - Years 4–5: seventh chords (dominant 7, major 7, minor 7, half-diminished, diminished 7); triad inversions; progressions with ii, vi, V7, secondary dominants; modulation to closely related keys; modes/pentatonic/whole-tone. Two-voice dictation. Playback and question-answer improvisation matching RCM Levels 7–8.
- The "high-intermediate" target lines up roughly with ABRSM Grade 6–7 aural, RCM Level 8 ear tests and UMTA AIM Level 8: seventh-chord quality, four cadence types, 4-bar playback, and two-voice dictation.
- Functional scale-degree hearing (cadence, then note) fits a keyboard app especially well because the response is "play the note". It should be the backbone. Isolated interval naming can be a supporting drill.
- Movable do vs numbers: offer scale-degree numbers (1–7) by default, with movable-do solfege as an optional label. EarMaster supports both, which shows there is demand (see section 3).

### Gaps
- Full ABRSM Grade 2, 4, 5, 7 and 8 test texts, and full RCM interval/chord/progression lists per level, were not retrieved. The RCM supplement retrieved covered only playback for Levels 5–10.
- No peer-reviewed source was found that empirically sets an optimal order for interval introduction (for example P5/P8 first vs M3/m3 first). The common practice of starting with widely contrasting intervals (octave vs P5 vs M3, then filling in) is uncited.
- Gordon's audiation "sequential learning theory" and Kodály sequencing (sol–mi, then la, then do, the pentatonic before the diatonic) were not retrieved from primary sources in this pass.

## 2. Evidence on absolute pitch (AP) training in adults, and on relative-pitch training

### Takeaway
A minority of adults can reach AP-level note naming after intensive, adaptive, feedback-rich training: about 2 of 6 in Van Hedger et al. 2019 (about 32 h) and 2 of 12 for all 12 notes in Wong et al. 2025 (about 21 h). Most participants improve substantially but stop short of AP. All participants were already musicians, and in one study they were selected for strong auditory memory. For an adult beginner, AP should be an optional, gamified side track ("note-colour" naming). Relative pitch should be the core, because it is what every exam and every musical task needs.

### Cited findings
- Van Hedger, Heald and Nusbaum (2019, PLoS ONE 14(9): e0223047): 6 adults aged 18–26 with some musical training, selected for superior auditory working memory. Training lasted 8 weeks (2 four-week phases), 4 days a week, about 4 h a week (about 32 h in all). Tasks were speeded note identification, untimed accuracy training and key identification; stimuli were 160 notes in 7 timbres; weekly tests had no feedback. 2 of 6 reached genuine-AP thresholds (for example 94% and 97% on the UCSD test); the other four improved modestly, the best of them to about 52%. The two successful learners largely kept their performance about 4 months later. The authors explicitly decline to estimate what proportion of adults could succeed. — [Van Hedger et al. 2019, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6759182)
- Wong, Cheung, Ngan and Wong (2025, Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, "Learning fast and accurate absolute pitch judgment in adulthood"): 12 adult musicians, an online 8-week programme, about 21 h each, more than 15,000 trials of naming notes with no reference. Difficulty adapted by adding notes and shortening the response window. On average participants named more than 7 notes almost perfectly within about 1–2 s; errors became closer to the target. 2 of 12 could instantly name all 12 notes with near-perfect accuracy. The authors stress that the design was engaging and adaptive and forced learning of note identity rather than pattern-guessing. — [Psychology Today, blog by Yetta & Alan Wong](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/surrey-minds/202506/perfect-pitch-isnt-just-for-kids-adults-can-develop-it-too); also covered by [HealthDay](https://www.healthday.com/health-news/neurology/adults-can-learn-perfect-pitch-study-suggests)
- Caveats on Wong 2025 as reported: small sample, no control group described in the summary, retention and transfer to other timbres not addressed in that article. — [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/surrey-minds/202506/perfect-pitch-isnt-just-for-kids-adults-can-develop-it-too)
- Interleaving vs blocking for melodic intervals (Wong, Chen & Lim, Psychology of Music 2021, 49(4)): novices learned 6 ascending melodic intervals. Without reference songs or singing, interleaved practice gave clearly better identification of new instances than blocked practice. With reference songs and singing as aids, the two schedules were comparable. Most learners wrongly believed blocking worked better. — [Wong, Chen & Lim 2021, SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0305735620922595)
- Chenette (2018) frames aural-skills difficulty largely as a working-memory bottleneck. This supports chunking (hearing groups rather than single notes) and extractive listening as trained skills. — [Chenette 2018](https://journals.ou.edu/jmtp/article/download/543/1229/1221)

### Inferences
- The adult AP data imply roughly 20–30+ hours of focused, speeded, adaptive training to see large gains, with full 12-note AP for only about 15–35% of already-musical, selected adults. For a "not musical" beginner, promise "better note recognition", not perfect pitch.
- Design lessons that carry over from the AP studies even to relative-pitch drills: adaptive set size (start with 2–3 categories, add one at a time), a response-time window that shrinks as accuracy rises, many short trials, multiple timbres to prevent memorizing one sound, and avoiding cues that allow relative guessing. For an AP drill, insert random noise or a random-pitch sequence between trials so the previous note cannot serve as a reference. The Wong team's stress on preventing "patterns or guessing" points the same way.
- AP-style drills are cheap to build (one note, play/name it) and give a game-like "streak" mechanic. They are worth including as an optional side quest that does not gate progression.

### Gaps
- The Wong et al. 2020 study (an earlier AP training study by the same group) was not retrieved, so its numbers are not confirmed here.
- No retrieved controlled study measured whether relative-pitch training transfers to performance skills such as sight-reading or playing by ear in adult beginners. The claim of "transfer" in the Functional Ear Trainer comes from the developer, not from an independent study.
- No 2015–2026 randomized trial was found comparing functional (scale-degree) training with isolated interval training.

## 3. How existing tools structure ear training

### Takeaway
Tools fall into two families. Interval/chord/scale identification trainers (ToneDear, musictheory.net-style, EarMaster's custom mode) quiz single elements. Functional trainers (Functional Ear Trainer, ToneDear's functional modes) play a cadence first, then ask for scale degrees. EarMaster is the most complete: progressive "workshops" covering every skill type, multiple answer modes including MIDI and microphone input, and a choice of naming systems. Adaptive difficulty is rarely documented publicly.

### Cited findings
- EarMaster has progressive "Workshops" (general ear training and theory, plus jazz workshops), a beginner's course, and a Customize mode. Its exercises cover: interval comparison, identification and singing; scale, chord, inversion and progression identification; rhythm dictation, reading, clap-back and error detection; melodic dictation, sing-back and sight-singing. Answers can be given on an on-screen staff, piano, guitar or other instruments, a functional scale-degree/solfege keyboard, multiple-choice buttons, a **MIDI instrument**, or a **microphone** with real-time pitch detection. Naming systems are letters, scale degrees, fixed-do or movable-do (Kodály-compatible). Results go to a statistics window and can sync to teachers via EarMaster Cloud. — [Wikipedia: EarMaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EarMaster)
- ToneDear exercise types: intervals, chords, scales, chord progressions, "perfect pitch", scale degrees (functional), intervals in context, and melodic dictation (as scale degrees after a progression). Its advice is to practise "a little bit every day". — [ToneDear](https://tonedear.com/)
- Functional Ear Trainer recommends about 10 minutes a day and is based on tone function within a key. — [Functional Ear Trainer, App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/id1088761926)

### Inferences
- The app has a MIDI keyboard, so its natural differentiator is the answer mode "play it on the real keyboard", which EarMaster also supports. Every drill type (scale degree, interval, chord, melody) can be answered by playing. Naming via buttons is then a secondary mode for theory-label learning.
- The common functional template (cadence, then target, then answer, then hear the resolution to the tonic) is cheap to implement with Web Audio. It should be the default drill frame.

### Gaps
- No detailed level structures were retrieved for Teoria, Perfect Ear, Complete Ear Trainer (Binary Guilds), Earpeggio or musictheory.net/Tenuto. Their documentation and their adaptive and scoring rules were not checked in this pass. Complete Ear Trainer is widely described as having about 150 levels with a star system, but this was not verified here.
- The internal adaptive algorithms (if any) of EarMaster and Functional Ear Trainer are not publicly documented in the sources found.

## 4. Graded exam aural tests as level benchmarks

### Takeaway
Exam aural tests give concrete, checkable milestones. ABRSM Grades 1–3 cover pulse/metre, echo-singing short melodies, spotting changes and musical features (with major/minor at Grade 3). By Grade 6 they add playback of a melody and perfect vs imperfect cadences. RCM/AIM upper-intermediate levels (around Level 8) expect seventh-chord quality, four cadence types, 4-bar playback/answer phrases, and rhythmic and two-voice melodic dictation.

### Cited findings
- ABRSM Grade 1 and Grade 3 test contents: see section 1. — [Jade Bultitude G1](https://jadebultitude.com/abrsm-aural-tests-grade-1/); [Jade Bultitude G3](https://jadebultitude.com/abrsm-aural-test-grade-3/)
- ABRSM Grade 6 tests 6A–6D, including playback of a melody on an instrument (allowed as an alternative to singing) and perfect/imperfect cadences. — [e-musicmaestro G6](https://www.e-musicmaestro.com/auraltests/guides/abrsm-grade-6-guide)
- RCM playback Levels 5–10 (question/answer periods, phrase played three times). — [RCM Piano Syllabus Supplement](https://teacherportal.rcmusic.com/getattachment/0b9e372d-f0cf-49e1-9e94-be574cce6563/piano_syllabus_supplement.pdf)
- UMTA AIM Level 8 detailed list (scales, P4/P5/tritone, four seventh chords, four cadences, rhythm and two-voice dictation). — [AIM Level 8](https://www.usu.edu/ycpiano/files/aim/ETlevel8.pdf)

### Inferences
- A practical mapping for a 5-year app. Year 1 is about ABRSM G1–2. Year 2 is about G3 (major/minor, 4-bar change detection). Year 3 is about G4–5 (cadences start, longer echoes). Year 4 is about G6 / RCM 6 (perfect/imperfect, 4-bar playback). Year 5 is about G7 / RCM–AIM 8 (seventh chords, deceptive/plagal cadences, two-voice dictation).
- Exam test formats can be turned directly into "boss" or checkpoint challenges at the end of each tier. Examples: "spot the change: beginning or end?", "play back the top line after two hearings", "perfect or imperfect?".
- Where ABRSM requires singing, the app substitutes playing back on the keyboard. ABRSM 6A itself allows playing instead of singing, which supports the substitution.

### Gaps
- Exact ABRSM Grade 2, 4, 5, 7, 8 and RCM per-level interval/chord lists were not retrieved, so the mapping above is approximate.

## 5. Response modes with a MIDI keyboard

### Takeaway
"Play it back" on the keyboard is the best default. It is a legitimate exam mode (ABRSM 6A allows playing back, and RCM playback is played) and it forces audiation of the actual pitch rather than a label. Label identification (buttons: interval names, chord qualities, cadence types) is needed to learn theory vocabulary. Singing is not available here, which loses the strongest internalisation aid noted in the interleaving study.

### Cited findings
- ABRSM Grade 6 melody repeat may be sung, hummed, whistled or played on the instrument. — [e-musicmaestro G6](https://www.e-musicmaestro.com/auraltests/guides/abrsm-grade-6-guide)
- EarMaster accepts answers from a MIDI instrument, an on-screen keyboard or a functional scale-degree keyboard, and via microphone. — [Wikipedia: EarMaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EarMaster)
- Reference songs and singing changed the learning outcome. With them, blocked and interleaved schedules performed alike; without them, interleaving was clearly better. Singing/reference melodies therefore act as a strong scaffold. — [Wong, Chen & Lim 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0305735620922595)

### Inferences
- Suggested response modes by drill:
  - Pitch match: hear a note, find it on the keys. Octave-tolerant at first, octave-exact later.
  - Scale degree: hear the cadence and a note, then play the note. Labels show after the answer.
  - Interval: hear it and play the second note from a given first note. Optionally name it.
  - Chord quality: name it (buttons), later play it.
  - Cadence or progression: name it, or play the bass line.
  - Melody: play back the whole phrase.
  - Rhythm: tap it back on any key, reusing the existing timing engine and the 60 ms chord window.
- Because the app already scores timing per hand, rhythmic dictation and clap-back can use the same scoring with no new input type.
- To make up for no singing, add "think it first" prompts: hear it, wait 2 s silently, then play. Also add optional reference-song hints for intervals. Neither has been tested in retrieved studies.
- Playing on the keyboard has a trial-and-error risk, since a learner can hunt for the note. Score the first note played, or limit attempts, so that hunting is not rewarded.

### Gaps
- No retrieved study compared play-back with name-identification response modes for learning outcomes.

## 6. Session length, interleaving, spacing, feedback

### Takeaway
Short daily sessions (about 10 minutes) are the industry norm. The adult AP studies used about 20–32 hours spread over 8 weeks, at about 4 h a week. Interleaving categories beats blocking when there are no song or singing crutches, although learners believe the opposite, so the app should interleave by default and explain why. Immediate feedback, adaptive set size and speeded response windows were features of the successful AP programmes.

### Cited findings
- Functional Ear Trainer recommends 10 minutes a day; ToneDear recommends "a little bit every day". — [App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/id1088761926); [ToneDear](https://tonedear.com/)
- The Van Hedger 2019 schedule was 4 days a week, about 4 h a week for 8 weeks, with weekly no-feedback tests. — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6759182)
- In Wong 2025, about 21 h over 8 weeks with more than 15,000 trials gave about 70 trials per minute of practice time. The programme was adaptive (more notes, shorter response time). — [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/surrey-minds/202506/perfect-pitch-isnt-just-for-kids-adults-can-develop-it-too)
- Interleaving outperformed blocking for melodic interval learning without aids, and learners misjudged which schedule worked better. — [Wong, Chen & Lim 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0305735620922595)

### Inferences
- Session design: a 5–10 minute ear block inside each daily practice, made of many fast trials (aim for dozens per session). Interleave the current set of 3–6 categories. Introduce a new category briefly in a block, then mix it into the interleaved set at once.
- Adaptive rule sketch, modelled on the AP studies. Add a category when rolling accuracy is at least about 85–90% over the last 20–30 trials. Shrink the response window once accuracy is stable. Drop back on repeated failures. Use spaced review of mastered categories, mixing old items into new sets.
- Use weekly no-feedback checkpoint tests (as in Van Hedger 2019) as mastery gates in the gamified tree.
- Feedback: play the correct answer immediately, and for scale degrees play the resolution to the tonic. Show the label after the response. Track per-category confusion (for example 4 vs 5, m3 vs M3) and target the confused pairs.

### Gaps
- No ear-training-specific study on optimal spacing intervals (days between reviews) was retrieved. Spacing recommendations are extrapolated from general learning science.
- No study was found that sets a specific accuracy threshold for advancing a level in ear training. The 85–90% figure is a design inference, loosely anchored in the 85% AP criterion used by Van Hedger et al.
