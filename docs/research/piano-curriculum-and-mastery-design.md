# Five years lands an adult at Grade 6

Every serious tradition examined here (ABRSM, RCM and Trinity graded exams, the Soviet-model children's music school, adult method books, and the Kodály and Gordon pedagogies) teaches piano, theory and ear in the same broad order. Playing starts in five-finger positions, hands separately, and moves to one-octave and then two-octave scales hands together, then arpeggios and seventh chords. Reading builds up one parameter at a time: key, metre, hand position, note values, articulation. Theory runs from notation through a widening circle of keys, interval number and then quality, the tonic triad and then V and IV, inversions, V7, cadences, function, modulation and form. Ear training begins with pulse and high/low judgments and moves through echo playback, major/minor, intervals, chord qualities and cadences to seventh chords and progressions. On ABRSM's own preparation-hour estimates, an adult practising 20–40 minutes a day accumulates roughly 600–1,200 hours in five years. That is enough for **ABRSM Grade 5 at the low end, Grade 6 as the realistic core target, and Grade 7 only as a stretch for daily 40-minute practisers**. "High intermediate" (Grade 6–7 / RCM Level 6–7) is achievable, but only if the app supplies what a teacher normally does: diagnosis, hard-spot isolation, spaced review and a balanced repertoire diet. The learning-science and product evidence points to one structure. Use a **single guided path laid over a visible prerequisite tree**. Give skills mastery states that decay and that reach "Mastered" only on a later-day mixed retest. Award XP for clean, accurate repetitions, not minutes. Keep streaks low-bar and leave out leagues. Use a functional, play-it-back ear-training spine with absolute pitch as an optional side quest. The report closes with a year-by-year skill map across seven strands, exit benchmarks, prerequisite chains and the mechanics to build.

## The exam boards and the Soviet school share one spine, at different speeds

The graded exam boards give the most precise milestones. ABRSM's piano syllabus lists, for every grade, which scales are played hands separately or together, over how many octaves, and at what minimum speed. Scales rise from **crotchet 54 at Initial to 100 at Grade 4, then minim 60 at Grade 5 and minim 72 at Grade 6**. Hands-together two-octave major and minor scales arrive at Grades 2–3, and four-octave hands-together scales with dominant and diminished sevenths at Grade 6 ([ABRSM 2025 & 2026 spec](https://www.abrsm.org/sites/default/files/2024-06/Piano%202025%20&%202026%20Prac%20syllabus%2020240524_access.pdf)). All scales are played from memory, legato, evenly and without pedal. That is a ready-made scoring rubric: evenness of inter-onset intervals, legato overlap, and tempo at or above target. The 2027–28 specification leaves the scale, sight-reading and aural requirements unchanged, so this ladder is stable for years ([ABRSM 2027 & 2028 spec](https://www.abrsm.org/sites/default/files/2026-06/Piano%20Practical%20Grades%20Qualification%20Specification%202027%20&%202028.pdf)). One caveat: the syllabus footnote naming the rhythmic grouping behind those speeds did not render in extraction, so the notes-per-beat assumption (quavers at crotchet speeds, semiquavers at minim speeds) still needs a visual check against the PDF.

ABRSM's sight-reading tests are the clearest model of how reading grows one parameter at a time. Initial uses 4 bars, hands separately in a five-finger position, in C major or D minor. Grade 1 adds 3/4, dotted notes and any five-finger position. **Grade 2 adds hands together**, ties and pp. **Grade 3 takes the hands out of five-finger position**, adds 3/8, two-note chords and simple semiquavers, and runs up to 8 bars. Grade 4 adds 6/8, anacrusis and chromatic notes. Grade 5 adds four-part chords and simple syncopation over 8–12 bars. Grade 6 adds 5/8, 5/4, 9/8, triplets, clef changes and sustain pedal over 12–16 bars ([ABRSM 2025 & 2026 spec](https://www.abrsm.org/sites/default/files/2024-06/Piano%202025%20&%202026%20Prac%20syllabus%2020240524_access.pdf)). Each of these features maps onto an unlockable reading node.

RCM's technical tables are more granular and take a harmony-first route. **Triads with inversions appear from Preparatory B, well before arpeggios, which wait until Level 4.** I–V–I cadence patterns arrive at Level 5. Dominant and leading-tone diminished sevenths come at Levels 5–7. Hands-together arpeggios in semiquavers at crotchet 60 are a Level 7 skill. Scale tempos climb from about 60–69 (quavers) at Preparatory B and Level 1 to 92–104 at Levels 4–5, then to semiquavers at 60–76 at Levels 6–7 ([RCM 2022 technical requirements charts](https://colorinmypiano.com/wp-content/files/RCM_2022_Technical_Requirements_Charts_Prep-10.pdf)). The RCM musicianship tests sketch the early ear ladder. Preparatory levels test major/minor triad quality, 4-note playback and 2-bar clapback. Level 1 adds m3 and M3. Level 2 adds P5. Level 3 adds P4, root/3rd/5th identification, 5–6-note playback, and hands-together sight-reading of 4 bars ([RCM Piano Syllabus 2022](https://teacherportal.rcmusic.com/getattachment/57f3734d-97e5-4777-b67e-4b1111ee31a3/piano-syllabus-2022-edition.pdf)). Trinity's 2023 piano syllabus could be confirmed only at the level of format: 12-piece exam books, a 21-piece extended edition, and the option of a candidate's own composition ([Interlude.hk](https://interlude.hk/introducing-trinity-college-londons-new-piano-syllabus/)). Its scale lists and supporting tests were not retrieved.

The Soviet-model children's music school (ДМШ) is technically ahead of the Anglo boards at the same year count, and its structure holds lessons an app can reuse directly. The St Petersburg Rachmaninov school's 8-year programme starts year 1 with **10–15 single-line pieces in non legato, then legato**, followed by 8–10 two-hand pieces including etudes. **Staccato and pedal are held back until year 2.** Four-octave scales arrive in year 3, the D7 in year 4, and scales in thirds and tenths in year 5 ([Rachmaninov school programme](https://rahmaninovschool.spb.ru/f/dpop_2020_8_fortepiano_spec_i_chtenie.pdf)). From year 2 every year's repertoire follows a fixed genre mix: **etudes, polyphony, large form (sonatina movements, variations) and character pieces**, 14–18 works in all. The graduation exam is five works from memory, one from each category. The assessment rhythm is also fixed: an etude competition in the first quarter, a technical exam in the third quarter from year 3, semester tests and a promotion exam. Sight-reading is deliberately set two or three classes below the pupil's current level. Home practice norms are **3 hours a week in years 1–2, rising to 5–6 hours by years 5–8**, which is roughly 25–50 minutes a day and the same range as this app's learner ([Chastoozerskaya DShI framework](https://chshi.uralschool.ru/file/download?id=2296)). The child also gets two hours of weekly teacher contact plus separate solfeggio classes, and that is the gap software has to fill. For Lithuania, only a hobby-level programme was found, which sets totals over the whole course rather than per-year scales ([JMM programme annex](https://jmm.lt/storage/4600/Muzikos-megeju-ugdymo-programos-priedas.pdf)). A formal pre-professional Lithuanian piano programme was not located.

Adult method books trade breadth of reading positions for early chords. Alfred's Adult All-in-One Level 1 introduces **C and G7 chords with chord symbols by about page 32**, then F, G position, the damper pedal, eighth notes, dotted quarters, the C, G and F major scales, primary chords, the blues progression, syncopation, A and D harmonic minor, and finally eighth-note triplets, all in one book ([Alfred's Adult All-in-One Level 1](https://studylib.net/doc/25598956/alfred-s-adult-all-in-one-piano-course)). Faber's adult course likewise pairs a sequenced reading plan with lead sheets, chord symbols and a short daily technique routine ([Faber Adult Piano Adventures](https://pianoadventures.com/piano-books/adult-piano-adventures/level-1/things-to-know/)). An adult's first year in such a book reaches roughly Initial–Grade 1 reading with Grade 2-level harmonic vocabulary. The two models disagree on pedal timing: Alfred brings in pedal in book one, the ДМШ waits until year 2, and ABRSM sight-reading waits until Grade 6. The adult-method choice (early, simple pedal over block chords) fits a motivated adult better. Disciplined legato pedalling can still be held back to year 2.

The hours settle the "five years" question. ABRSM's official total qualification times add up to **about 640 hours from Initial to Grade 5, 860 to Grade 6 and 1,130 to Grade 7** ([ABRSM 2025 & 2026 spec](https://www.abrsm.org/sites/default/files/2024-06/Piano%202025%20&%202026%20Prac%20syllabus%2020240524_access.pdf)). Twenty minutes a day every day comes to about 120 hours a year, or 600 hours over five years. Forty minutes a day comes to about 1,200. ABRSM's figures assume a taught pupil preparing only for exams, so they are a floor rather than a ceiling. One London teacher's timeline (opinion, not data) has adults reaching Grade 5 in 2.5–4 years with weekly lessons and 30–60 minutes of daily practice ([piano-composer-teacher-london.co.uk](https://www.piano-composer-teacher-london.co.uk/how-long-does-it-take-to-learn-piano/)). No peer-reviewed data on adult progression rates was found. The defensible promise is **Grade 6 by the end of year 5 for a consistent 30-minute practiser, Grade 5 at minimum, and Grade 7 for those who reliably hit 40 minutes**. Theory and ear must keep pace: ABRSM will not admit a candidate to Grade 6 without a Grade 5 theory or musicianship pass ([ABRSM 2025 & 2026 spec](https://www.abrsm.org/sites/default/files/2024-06/Piano%202025%20&%202026%20Prac%20syllabus%2020240524_access.pdf)).

## Theory follows a fixed dependency chain, and sound comes before the symbol

All three Anglo theory syllabi follow the same logic, adding about one or two new key signatures per grade. Trinity's syllabus is the most explicit. Grade 1 covers C, F and G major, tones and semitones, scale degrees, interval number to the 5th, simple time, and tonic triads labelled both C and I. Grade 2 adds relative minors, **interval quality**, semiquavers, simple syncopation and first-inversion tonic triads. Grade 3 brings compound time, anacrusis, second inversions, V and perfect cadences. Grade 4 brings IV, the plagal cadence, V7, the tritone and the chromatic scale. Grade 5 brings ii, the imperfect cadence, **modulation to dominant or relative**, binary form and pentatonic scales. Grade 6 adds diminished sevenths, figured bass, interrupted cadences and ternary form. Grade 7 adds secondary sevenths, pivot-chord modulation and sonata form ([Trinity Theory of Music syllabus](https://www.newschool.ie/wp-content/uploads/2018/01/Theory-of-Music-Syllabus-2009.pdf)). ABRSM's grade topics follow the same arc: interval numbers at Grade 1, quality at Grade 3, keys to 5 sharps or flats and technical degree names at Grade 4, inversions, cadences and chord II at Grade 5 ([MyMusicTheory](https://www.mymusictheory.com)). RCM likewise moves from tonic triads at Preparatory to V7 at Level 5, authentic and half cadences at Level 6, diminished sevenths and exotic scales at Level 7, and modes at Level 8 ([RCM Theory Syllabus 2016 Highlights](https://admin.rcmusic.com/sites/default/files/files/RCM-Theory-Syllabus-2016-Highlights.pdf)). Both Trinity and RCM **teach chord symbols and Roman numerals together from the first level**, which suits a piano app where lead sheets are useful early.

Soviet-tradition solfeggio covers the same ground but makes every concept audible. A St Petersburg second-year programme pairs keys up to D major with stable and unstable (leading) degrees, intervals to the 7th, dotted rhythm, and oral, visual and melodic dictation ([SPb solfeggio 2nd year](https://ddt-grad-tchudes.akrns.gov.spb.ru/images/doc/doki/23-24/RP/Karimova/RP_Solfedzhio_2go.pdf)). The Belarusian national programme, keyed to the standard Kalmykov–Fridkin textbook, puts tritones resolving in key, the cadential 6/4, D7 inversions, the four triad types and **8-bar melodic dictation in 30 minutes and 10 playings** at its fifth grade. It moves on to harmonic major and VII7, then II7 and all sevenths, then modulation to closely related keys ([ADU.by solfeggio programme](https://www.adu.by/images/2024/02/muz.-instrum._iskustv_V-IX_solfedgio.pdf)). The Lithuanian encyclopedia's definition of solfeggio (singing with syllables, scales, intervals, chords and dictation, under Kodály, Ostrovsky and Sposobin influences) suggests Lithuanian schools inherit this model ([VLE: solfedžio](https://www.vle.lt/straipsnis/solfedzio/)), though no programme confirmed it. Three Soviet concepts are absent from the Anglo boards and worth adopting: **stable versus unstable degrees and their resolution**, characteristic intervals resolving within the key, and T–S–D chord chains played and heard as one sequence.

The child-centred pedagogies add a rule about order rather than content: **experience the sound, then name it, then read it, then create with it**. Kodály's sequence orders pitch as sol–mi, la, do and re (do-pentatonic), then the extended pentatonic, and only then fa and ti, the diatonic scale and minor. Rhythm goes from beat to ta/ti-ti, rests, half notes, sixteenths, dotted rhythms and syncopation, then 6/8 ([Wichita State Kodály sequence](https://wichita.edu/academics/fine_arts/music/kodaly/documents/G_2009_Wichita_A_KODALY_SEQUENCE_FINAL.pdf)). Gordon's Music Learning Theory frames learning as aural/oral imitation, verbal association (syllables, names such as tonic and dominant), symbolic association (notation), composite synthesis, and finally inference: improvisation and theory ([Gordon music learning theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gordon_music_learning_theory)). For this app, every theory node should therefore run **hear → name → see → play → create**. One example is hearing major against minor triads, learning the labels, seeing them on the staff, building them on the keyboard, then improvising with them. Another follows from the same rule: tonic and dominant function should be drilled by ear in year 1, long before written cadence theory in year 3.

Some theory needs sound and some does not. Pitch matching, scale-degree hearing, interval and chord-quality identification, cadence and progression recognition, and rhythmic and melodic dictation all need audio. Building intervals, chords and scales from a name, transposing, and harmonising with I–IV–V are better with audio as a check. Key signatures, terms, beaming, enharmonics and Roman-numeral analysis on the page work as visual drills. This matches how ear-training tools and university musicianship guides split their exercises ([ToneDear](https://tonedear.com/); [McGill musicianship guide](https://libraryguides.mcgill.ca/musicianship)). Because the adult learns key signatures conceptually, the theory strand can run ahead of the boards' slow drip of keys. What actually sets the pace is playing in those keys.

## Functional relative pitch is the ear spine, and absolute pitch is an optional side quest

The exam aural tests give checkable milestones. ABRSM Grade 1 asks for clapping the beat in 2 or 3 time, echoing three-note two-bar phrases, spotting a pitch change at the start or end of a phrase, and naming dynamics and articulation ([ABRSM G1 aural guide](https://jadebultitude.com/abrsm-aural-tests-grade-1/)). Grade 3 extends this to 4-bar change detection of pitch or rhythm, echo melodies within an octave, and major against minor ([ABRSM G3 aural guide](https://jadebultitude.com/abrsm-aural-test-grade-3/)). The format changes at Grade 6: playback of the upper part of a phrase after two hearings, which **may be played on the instrument instead of sung**, plus perfect against imperfect cadences and questions on style and texture ([ABRSM G6 guide](https://www.e-musicmaestro.com/auraltests/guides/abrsm-grade-6-guide)). A high-intermediate benchmark is Utah's AIM Level 8. It asks for P4, P5 and tritone both broken and blocked, **four seventh-chord qualities (MM7, Mm7, mm7, dim7)**, four cadences (authentic, plagal, half, deceptive), rhythmic dictation with sixteenths, and two-voice melodic dictation ([UMTA AIM Level 8](https://www.usu.edu/ycpiano/files/aim/ETlevel8.pdf)). RCM's playback-improvisation test at Levels 5–8 has the student answer a 4-bar question phrase, heard three times, to complete an 8-bar parallel and then contrasting period ([RCM Piano Syllabus Supplement](https://teacherportal.rcmusic.com/getattachment/0b9e372d-f0cf-49e1-9e94-be574cce6563/piano_syllabus_supplement.pdf)). That is an ideal year-4–5 "creativity boss".

Two tool families exist. Isolated-element quizzes (interval, chord, scale) form one. Functional trainers form the other: they play a cadence to set the key and then ask for a scale degree, an interval in context or a short melody ([ToneDear](https://tonedear.com/); [Functional Ear Trainer](https://apps.apple.com/app/id1088761926)). EarMaster is the most complete reference product. It covers every skill type in progressive workshops and accepts answers from **a MIDI instrument**, an on-screen keyboard, a scale-degree keypad or a microphone, in letters, scale degrees, or fixed or movable do ([EarMaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EarMaster)). No controlled study shows functional training beating isolated interval drills. Still, functional hearing is what exams test and what playing by ear needs, and on a MIDI keyboard the answer is simply to play the note. **The default ear drill frame should be cadence → target → play it back → hear the resolution to the tonic.** Named-label drills support it so the learner picks up the theory vocabulary.

Melodic dictation is mainly limited by working memory. Karpinski's model, as Chenette summarises it, runs hearing → short-term melodic memory → understanding → notation. Each stage fails in its own way, and chunking and extractive listening are trainable skills in their own right ([Chenette 2018](https://journals.ou.edu/jmtp/article/download/543/1229/1221)). Playback length should therefore grow slowly: 3 notes, then 4–6, then 2 bars, then 4 bars, then two voices. The app should also teach chunking explicitly ("hear it as two groups of three").

Absolute pitch is learnable by a minority of adults, never by most. In Van Hedger and colleagues' study, **2 of 6 adult musicians**, selected for strong auditory memory, reached genuine absolute-pitch thresholds after about 32 hours over 8 weeks, and largely kept the gain four months later. The other four improved only modestly ([Van Hedger et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6759182)). In Wong and colleagues' 2025 online programme, 12 musicians did about 21 hours and more than 15,000 adaptive trials. On average they named more than 7 notes almost perfectly within 1–2 seconds, but **only 2 of 12 mastered all twelve** ([Psychology Today summary](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/surrey-minds/202506/perfect-pitch-isnt-just-for-kids-adults-can-develop-it-too)). For a self-described "not musical" beginner, the honest framing is "note recognition" as an optional, non-gating side quest. The mechanics of those studies still carry over to all ear drills: start with 2–3 categories and add one at a time, shrink the response window as accuracy rises, run many fast trials, vary timbre, and give weekly no-feedback tests. Interleaving matters too. In a study of novices learning melodic intervals without reference songs or singing, **interleaved practice clearly beat blocked practice, though learners believed the opposite** ([Wong, Chen & Lim 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0305735620922595)). This app has no singing input, so it loses that crutch, and interleaving the ear sets should be the default.

## Learning science rewards clean repetitions, later-day retests and low-bar habits

The strongest piano-specific finding is that practice quality beats quantity. In Duke, Simmons and Cash's study of advanced pianists, total practice time, repetitions and run-throughs did not predict next-day performance. Accuracy during practice did: **the share of error-free complete run-throughs was the strongest predictor of next-day performance (|r| ≈ 0.71)**. The better practisers pinpointed the hard passage and repeated it until the error was gone ([Structural Learning briefing on Duke et al. 2009](https://www.structural-learning.com/research-briefings/practise-the-hard-bit)). The sample was small and the study correlational, but its message lines up with the rest. Motor sequences consolidate offline: after learning a piano-like finger sequence, people who slept improved where a daytime-rest group did not ([Debas et al. 2014](https://www.lib.upmc.fr/~marrelec/Publis/Debas-2014.pdf)). Interleaving pieces in 3-minute blocks gave clarinettists modestly better day-2 retention than 12-minute blocks. Most of them found it more useful, yet still preferred blocking, because **blocked practice feels more fluent than it is** ([Carter & Grahn 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01251/pdf)). Taken together, the trainer should score accuracy during practice rather than minutes, loop the bars where errors cluster, mix review into every session, and never mark a skill as mastered in the session it was first passed.

Existing products show how to turn this into a progression system. Khan Academy's states run Attempted (<70%), Familiar, Proficient and Mastered. **Mastered is reachable only by getting the skill right on a mixed-skill assessment**, and a miss demotes the skill one level ([Khan Academy help](https://support.khanacademy.org/hc/uz/articles/5548760867853)). Duolingo replaced its free-choice tree with a linear path in 2022 because learners did not know whether they were using it "the best way". The path interleaves skills, revisits concepts at growing intervals and carries unit guidebooks ([Duolingo, new home screen](https://blog.duolingo.com/new-duolingo-home-screen-design)). Its half-life-regression scheduler predicted recall with about half the error of a Leitner system and **raised practice sessions by 9.5%** in A/B tests ([Duolingo, How we learn how you learn](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-we-learn-how-you-learn)). FSRS shows that the trade-off between review workload and retention follows a U-shaped curve with an optimum ([FSRS4Anki wiki](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs4anki/wiki/The-optimal-retention)). Melodics, the closest analogue (a MIDI trainer), uses **15 sequential Stage exams per instrument**. Readiness dials fill from same-grade practice, Weekly Goals are curated to the current Stage, and timing-precision scoring can exceed 100. It retired its Trophies and Records ([Melodics progress guide](https://support.melodics.com/en/articles/13876438-your-guide-to-progress-in-melodics)), a quiet signal that collectible clutter did not earn its keep. Piano Marvel's recurring adaptive sight-reading test gives learners a single longitudinal reading score ([Piano Marvel SASR](https://pianomarvel.com/en/info/get-your-score)).

Consumer piano apps fail in predictable places, and those are this app's openings. A detailed review of Simply Piano found **treble clef only in the basic lessons, rhythm that "isn't really judged", theory marked optional**, no way to isolate small sections, only correct/incorrect feedback, and a structure that dissolves after the beginner tier ([Pianist's Compass review](https://pianistscompass.org/reviews/apps/simply-piano/)). The same review praised features worth copying: gated progression before the song library, slower practice tempos, "wait for me" mode and a lead-sheet course. The iggyuoso spec already scores each hand and its timing separately. Making theory and both clefs required, and adding looping and tempo ladders, covers most of the gap.

Gamification helps, but less than it seems, and it carries risks. Sailer and Homner's meta-analysis found a **cognitive effect of g = 0.49 that survives in high-rigour studies (g = 0.42)**. Motivational and behavioural effects (g = 0.36, 0.25) did not survive the rigour filter ([Sailer & Homner 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w)). A qualitative study of Duolingo users documented XP farming on easy content, streak obsession "at the cost of learning", binge lessons, league anxiety and even **wrist pain**. Its authors recommend weighting rewards by difficulty and rewarding learning rather than engagement ([Mogavi et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.16175)). Streaks themselves work when the bar is low. Duolingo found 7-day-streak learners 2.4× likelier to return. When it let a single lesson extend the streak, separate from the daily goal, **7+ day streaks rose by more than 40%** and D14 retention by 3.3% ([Duolingo, Improving the streak](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak)). Wrist strain is a real physical risk for a pianist, so the app must not reward marathon sessions.

## The design basis: a five-year map, its chains and its mechanics

What follows is a synthesis, not a published adult curriculum. It aligns each year to the exam milestones above, uses the ДМШ genre mix for repertoire, and takes ear content from ABRSM, RCM and AIM. Tempos are minimums in the ABRSM/RCM sense. "Exit benchmark" means a later-day checkpoint passed at ★★ (80) or better at full tempo, using the app's existing ★ 65 / ★★ 80 / ★★★ 92 scale.

### Year 1, "Hands apart, then together" (≈ ABRSM Initial–G1, RCM Prep–L1)

| Strand | Content | Exit benchmark |
|---|---|---|
| Technique | Five-finger positions in C, G, F, A minor, D minor; non legato, then legato; pentascales ending on a blocked tonic triad; 1-octave scales hands separately; C major 1 octave hands together; C contrary motion; blocked and broken tonic triads | C major 1 octave HT at q=60; G, F, A minor, D minor HS; even and legato from memory |
| Reading | Grand staff with **both clefs from week one**; landmark notes; five-finger reading; melodies split between hands; ledger lines around middle C | Sight-read 4–8 bars in 3/4 or 4/4, any five-finger position, HS, with dotted notes and mp/mf |
| Rhythm | Pulse; q, h, dotted h, w, then eighths and dotted quarter; rests; ties; 2/4, 3/4, 4/4; pickups | Tap back a 2–3-bar rhythm; play steadily against a click with per-hand drift under a set limit |
| Theory | Keyboard geography and letter names; tones and semitones; interval numbers 2nd–8ve; major-scale construction (C, G, F); one sharp or flat; tonic triad; chord symbols and numerals for C, F, G, G7 (I, IV, V, V7); basic dynamics, articulation and tempo terms | Build any interval by number, and the C, G or F scale and triad, on the keys from a name |
| Ear | High/low, same/different, direction; beat in 2 or 3; 3-note echoes within do–mi, widening to do–sol; major vs minor triad; scale degrees 1 and 5 after a cadence, then 3 | 4–5-note playback; m3 vs M3; spot a change at beginning or end of a 2-bar phrase |
| Repertoire | About 10–15 single-line pieces, then 8–10 two-hand pieces including short etudes; lead-sheet melody with LH C/F/G7 | Three contrasting pieces at full tempo, ★★ or better, with guide track off |
| Creativity | Black-key pentatonic improvisation; 2-bar question and answer | Improvise 4 bars over a held C–G drone with a steady pulse |

### Year 2, "Two octaves, two hands" (≈ ABRSM G2–3, RCM L2–3)

| Strand | Content | Exit benchmark |
|---|---|---|
| Technique | 2-octave HT scales in G, F, A minor, D minor, then D, A, E minor, G minor; chromatic from D, 1 octave; triads with inversions; staccato; simple then legato (overlapping) pedal | 2-octave HT scales in keys to 2 sharps or flats at q=80; inversions HS |
| Reading | **Hands together**; leaving five-finger position; two-note chords; up to 8 bars; keys to 2 sharps or flats | Sight-read 8 bars HT, outside five-finger position, with ties and two-note chords |
| Rhythm | Sixteenths; dotted quarter–eighth; triplets; simple syncopation; 3/8 | Tap back a 4-bar rhythm with rests and dotted figures |
| Theory | Interval quality (M/m 2nd and 3rd, P4, P5, P8, then 6ths and 7ths); natural, harmonic and melodic minor; relative keys; keys to 2–3 signs; primary triads; first inversion; perfect cadence; **stable and unstable degrees**; harmonising with I–V, then I–IV–V | Name and build any M, m or P interval; harmonise an 8-bar melody with I–IV–V |
| Ear | All major scale degrees in context; melodic m3, M3, P5, then P4; root, 3rd or 5th of a triad; perfect vs imperfect cadence; 2-bar playback | 4-bar change detection (pitch or rhythm); 5–6-note playback |
| Repertoire | Genre mix starts: etudes, first two-voice/imitative pieces, short variations or sonatina-style pieces, character pieces | One item from each of the four genres, ★★ at full tempo |
| Creativity | Transposing five-finger melodies by degree; I–IV–V accompaniment patterns; question-and-answer phrases | Transpose an 8-bar melody to a new key and add I–IV–V accompaniment |

### Year 3, "Keys widen, arpeggios arrive" (≈ ABRSM G3–4, RCM L4–5)

| Strand | Content | Exit benchmark |
|---|---|---|

### The prerequisite chains that cannot be reordered

Five chains carry the tree, and each is drawn from the order that Trinity, RCM, ABRSM and the ДМШ share. The **harmony chain** is interval number → interval quality → triad quality → inversions → V7 → cadences → T–S–D function → harmonisation → modulation → form, with every board putting inversions before cadences and V7 before diminished and secondary sevenths ([Trinity syllabus](https://www.newschool.ie/wp-content/uploads/2018/01/Theory-of-Music-Syllabus-2009.pdf); [RCM 2016 Highlights](https://admin.rcmusic.com/sites/default/files/files/RCM-Theory-Syllabus-2016-Highlights.pdf)). The **key chain** is semitone → major scale → key signatures → three minor forms → circle of fifths → transposition and modulation. The **metre chain** is pulse → simple metre → dotted and syncopated figures → compound metre → irregular and mixed metres. The **technique chain** is five-finger HS → 1-octave HS → 1-octave HT → 2-octave HT → triads with inversions → arpeggios → seventh-chord arpeggios → 4-octave HT, following RCM's inversions-before-arpeggios order and ABRSM's tempo ladder. The **ear chain** is major/minor → scale degrees in context → melodic intervals → harmonic intervals → cadences → progressions → seventh chords → modulation, with playback length growing alongside.

There are also cross-strand gates. A key does not unlock in the technique strand until its key-signature node is Practised in theory and its tonic-degree drill is Practised in ear. A chord unlocks in repertoire and lead sheets only once it is built and heard. Each node passes through hear → name → see → play → create, after Gordon. Year exams gate on all seven strands at once, the way ABRSM gates Grade 6 on Grade 5 theory. That keeps the playing strand from outrunning understanding, which is the failure the reviews pin on consumer apps.

### Mastery and gamification mechanics to build

**Skill states with decay.** Locked → Introduced → Practised (★, ≥65) → Solid (★★★, ≥92 at ≥90% tempo) → Mastered. Mastered is reached only by passing again **on a later day inside a mixed review or checkpoint**, after Khan's mixed-assessment rule and the sleep-consolidation evidence. A failed review demotes one state. Time without review drains a freshness meter, and a skill falls due for review when the meter reaches half, as in Duolingo's strength meters. Start with Leitner-style boxes in localStorage. Move to a per-skill half-life or FSRS-like stability model once there is data.

**Path over tree.** Show one "Start today's session" button driven by the path, and keep the full prerequisite tree visible for orientation and electives. Electives include the absolute-pitch side quest, extra repertoire, blues and lead-sheet branches. This follows Duolingo's lesson that free choice paralyses learners, while still leaving room for autonomy.

**Fifteen grade checkpoints.** Three per year (two interim, one year exit), each gated by readiness dials that fill as prerequisite skills reach Solid, after Melodics' 15 Stages. Checkpoints use exam-style formats: "beginning or end?", "play back the top line after two hearings", "perfect or imperfect?". Ear checkpoints give no feedback, as in Van Hedger's weekly tests, and run as unlimited-retry boss challenges. Passing one promotes the skills it samples toward Mastered.

**XP for learning only.** Award XP for clean reps, for stars gained, for reviews done while due and for state promotions. Give **no XP for replaying mastered easy material and none for minutes alone**. Cap the daily bonus and prompt breaks to protect wrists. A clean-rep ratio per exercise is the core statistic.

**Tempo ladder with accuracy gates.** Raise the tempo after three consecutive takes at ≥92% at the current rung, and drop back a rung after repeated misses. When errors cluster, offer a loop drill on the one or two bars where they fall, then put the passage back into the full piece. "Wait for me" mode is allowed as a scaffold but never counts toward Solid.

**Gentle streaks and no leagues.** One short review set extends the streak. Weekly targets (for example five of seven days) and rest days prevent streak anxiety. Competition is only against your past self: personal bests, a monthly level chart, and recurring adaptive reading and ear benchmarks in the SASR style. These give one honest level number per strand, separate from XP.

**Ear drills on the real keyboard.** The default frame is cadence → target → play it back → resolution. Score the first note played so hunting earns nothing. Interleave 3–6 categories at once. Add a category at about 85–90% rolling accuracy over 20–30 trials, shrink the response window as accuracy holds, rotate timbres, and target confused pairs. This is a design inference from the absolute-pitch and interleaving studies, not a validated threshold. Rhythm dictation reuses the existing per-hand timing engine and the 60 ms chord window.

**A guide with an informational voice.** The guide introduces each unit as a short guidebook. It explains why mixing and slow practice work, to head off the "blocked feels better" bias, and turns score data into diagnoses ("left hand late by 40 ms on beat 3"). It never says "you'll lose your streak". Offer an optional "I already know some" placement test per strand that marks earlier nodes Practised but not Mastered, so they still enter review.

**A daily session template for 20–40 minutes.** Roughly a 2–3 minute technique warm-up, ~30% due reviews, ~40% the new path lesson, ~15% ear drill and ~15% free or fun piece, each block swappable. Every block carries an off-app transfer task in rotation: play from printed notation, play with the guide track off, or improvise over a progression. That builds independence from the screen.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question from "what to teach" to "what a teacher does that software must replace". The content order is settled: the boards, the ДМШ and the method books disagree only on pace and on small choices such as when pedal arrives. What separates a Grade 6 adult from a plateaued app user is the teacher's other work, and that work is mechanical enough to build. It means scoring accuracy during practice, isolating the hard bar, retesting on a later day, insisting on all seven strands before promotion, and balancing the repertoire across genres. The ДМШ adds two structural ideas: a fixed four-genre repertoire mix and sight-reading set below the pupil's current level. The exam boards add the parameter-by-parameter reading ladder. Neither costs much to encode, and both are missing from consumer apps.

Three uncertainties should shape the roadmap rather than stall it. No study gives the right advancement thresholds or spacing intervals for music skills, so the 85–92% gates and review schedule should be treated as tunable parameters and logged from day one. Grade 7 in five years depends on practice dose more than design, so the app should report honestly where a learner's pace projects them. The AP results show that a minority of adults can learn near-perfect note naming, but the "not musical" learner is the one most likely to be in the majority who do not. Pitch training should therefore earn its place through functional hearing that pays off in every piece played, and note naming should remain a fun optional streak, never a gate.
