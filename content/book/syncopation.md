## Surprise in rhythm

You have learned that in every meter some beats are strong and some weak: in 4/4, beat 1 is strongest, beat 3 a little strong, beats 2 and 4 weak. Most simple music puts its long notes and accents on the strong beats, and our ears come to expect it. **Syncopation** is when the music deliberately puts an accent or a long note somewhere **unexpected**: on a weak beat, or between the beats, and often holds it over where the strong beat should be.

The effect is a little jolt, a push or a lift, as if the music is leaning forward or dancing around the beat. That jolt is the heart of ragtime, jazz, funk, Latin music, reggae and most pop music. Classical composers used it too, for energy and surprise.

## Three common kinds

### A long note on a weak part of the beat

The most typical syncopation in eighth notes is **short – long – short**: an eighth, a quarter, an eighth. Counted "1 + 2 +": the quarter note starts on the "+" of 1 and lasts through beat 2, so beat 2 itself has no new note. The accent moves to the off-beat.

### A tie across the beat or bar line

A note started just before a strong beat and **tied** over it is a strong syncopation: the strong beat arrives in silence (well, in a held note), and the note has "jumped the gun". This is very common at the end of a bar, tied into the next bar's beat 1, the so-called **anticipation**: the melody arrives half a beat early.

```figure

sound: "E4:q G4:q A4:q G4:e C5:e C5:h. r:q"
caption: The C arrives on the "and" of 4 and is tied over the bar line: it anticipates beat 1.
```

### Accents on weak beats

Simply accenting beats 2 and 4 instead of 1 and 3, the **backbeat**, is a kind of syncopation that defines rock and pop: the snare drum hits on 2 and 4. In classical music, a composer might write accent marks (>) or *sf* on weak beats for a jolt.

> **Syncopation** moves the emphasis off the strong beat: a long or accented note on a weak beat or on an off-beat, often tied over the next beat. Keep the true beat steady underneath.

## The golden rule: keep the beat

Syncopation only works if the beat underneath stays rock-solid. A syncopated note means something only in relation to the beat it avoids. The most common mistake is to let the syncopation drag the beat with it: the player "corrects" the rhythm by shifting the beat so the accent falls on it, and all the energy disappears.

How to keep it:

- **Count in the smallest value** ("1 + 2 + 3 + 4 +"), out loud, saying every syllable even when no note starts.
- **Tap your foot** on the beats. Notice which notes come with the foot and which between.
- **Practise with the click**, and in this app, with one hand tapping the beat while the other plays the syncopated rhythm. That is exactly what the two-hand syncopation exercises do.

## Syncopation between the hands

On the piano, syncopation is often created **between the hands**: the left hand keeps a steady beat (bass notes on 1 and 3, or a steady oom-pah), while the right hand plays a syncopated melody against it. This is the texture of **ragtime**, the American piano style of the 1890s to 1910s whose name probably comes from "ragged time". Scott Joplin's *The Entertainer* and *Maple Leaf Rag* are the classic examples. The steady left hand makes the right hand's syncopations sparkle. Playing ragtime is wonderful practice for hand independence.

## Syncopation across styles

- **Ragtime**: syncopated right hand, steady "oom-pah" left hand.
- **Jazz and swing**: syncopations and anticipations everywhere, often combined with swung eighths.
- **Latin music**: patterns like the Cuban *clave* and the *tresillo* (3 + 3 + 2 eighths in a bar of 4/4) are built on syncopation.
- **Pop and rock**: the backbeat, anticipated chord changes, syncopated vocal lines.
- **Classical**: Beethoven loved off-beat *sforzandos*; Brahms often blurs the bar line with syncopation and ties.

## Common confusions

- **Syncopation is playing out of time.** It is playing exactly in time, but with the accents in unexpected places.
- **Moving the beat to the accent.** The beat must stay; only the emphasis moves.
- **Syncopation means fast.** It works at any tempo.

## In short

Syncopation puts emphasis off the strong beat: a long note starting on an off-beat, a note tied across a beat or bar line (anticipation), or accents on weak beats (the backbeat). It drives ragtime, jazz, Latin and pop. Its power depends on a steady beat underneath: count every syllable, tap the beat, and let the hands play steady beat and syncopation against each other.
