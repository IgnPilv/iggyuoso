## Two ways to name a chord

Imagine you are giving someone directions. You can say "go to 14 Elm Street", which is exact but only works in one town, or "go to the second house past the church", which describes where it is relative to something else and works in any town with a church. Musicians name chords in both ways, and each is useful for different things.

- **Chord symbols** name the chord exactly: **C**, **F**, **G**, **Am**, **G7**. They appear above the tune in songbooks, lead sheets and guitar music.
- **Roman numerals** name the chord by its place in the key: **I**, **IV**, **V**. They describe the job the chord does, and they work in every key.

## Chord symbols

A chord symbol is simply the name of the root, with a little extra to say what kind of chord it is:

| Symbol | Means | Notes (from the root) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| C | C major | C E G |
| F | F major | F A C |
| G | G major | G B D |
| Am | A minor | A C E |
| G7 | G seventh (dominant 7th) | G B D F |

A letter on its own means a **major** chord. A small **m** after it means **minor**, a darker-sounding chord you will study properly later. A **7** adds a fourth note, a 7th above the root. There are many more symbols (Cmaj7, Bdim, Fsus4...), and you will meet them as they come up.

In a songbook the chord symbols sit above the tune and show where each chord starts. The chord lasts until the next symbol appears. A pianist reading a lead sheet plays the tune in the right hand and makes up an accompaniment in the left hand from the symbols, which is one of the most useful skills you can learn.

## Roman numerals

Now the second way. Number the notes of the scale from the key note: in C major, C is 1, D is 2, E is 3, F is 4, G is 5, and so on. Build a triad on each note, and give each chord the Roman numeral of its root:

- The chord on the 1st note (C) is **I** ("one").
- The chord on the 4th note (F) is **IV** ("four").
- The chord on the 5th note (G) is **V** ("five").

```figure
staff: { notes: "C4+E4+G4:w | F4+A4+C5:w | G4+B4+D5:w", labels: names }
caption: In C major: I (C), IV (F) and V (G).
```

Roman numerals use capital letters for major chords. Later you will see small numerals (ii, iii, vi) for minor chords.

## Why bother with numbers?

Because the numbers stay the same in every key. In the key of G, the I chord is G, the IV chord is C, and the V chord is D. In the key of F, I is F, IV is B♭ and V is C. The *letters* change, but the *relationships*, and the way the chords sound together, are identical. A song that goes I–IV–V–I sounds like the same progression whatever key you play it in.

| Key | I | IV | V |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| C | C | F | G |
| G | G | C | D |
| F | F | B♭ | C |

This is why musicians talk in numbers. A band can say "it's a one-four-five in G" and everyone knows what to play. And once you understand what each numeral *does*, you understand every song that uses it, not just one.

> **Chord symbols** name the exact chord (C, F, G7). **Roman numerals** name its place in the key (I, IV, V), and stay the same in every key.

## The jobs of I, IV and V

These three chords are called the **primary chords**, and each has a character:

- **I** is **home**. Pieces almost always end on it, and it feels completely settled.
- **V** is **away**, the furthest point of tension. It strongly wants to return to I. Adding a 7th (V7) makes that wish even stronger.
- **IV** is a gentler step away, a sort of warm departure, often heard on the way to V or on its way back to I (the "Amen" at the end of a hymn is IV–I).

## A first look at playing from symbols

In the lessons you will play these chords in your left hand under simple tunes. To begin, the left hand holds a chord while the right hand plays the melody, changing chord where the symbol changes. A very easy version uses only the root of each chord in the left hand, one note at a time. The next step uses the full triad; later you will learn how to move between chords smoothly by changing as few notes as possible.

## Common confusions

- **"Am" and "A" are different chords.** The small m means minor: A–C–E, not A–C♯–E.
- **Roman numerals are not finger numbers or beat numbers.** They are scale-step numbers of the chord's root.
- **"V" is not always G.** It is G only in the key of C.

## In short

Chord symbols name chords exactly: a letter for major, m for minor, 7 for an added seventh. Roman numerals name a chord by the scale step of its root, so I, IV and V are the chords on the 1st, 4th and 5th notes of the key, and they keep their meaning in every key. I is home, V is away and wants to come back, IV is a gentler step away.
