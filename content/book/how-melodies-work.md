## Not a mystery

Many people think melodies arrive by magic, and that only "musical" people can make them. In fact, the melodies we remember follow a handful of principles that have been taught to composition students for centuries. You have already met most of them in pieces you've played. This chapter collects them, so you can hear them in any tune, shape your playing around them, and use them to write tunes of your own in the Sketchbook and the composing lessons.

## 1. The motif: a small idea

Most melodies grow from a **motif**: a short, recognisable idea of a few notes, defined by its **rhythm** and its **shape**. Beethoven's Fifth Symphony opens with four notes, short-short-short-long, falling a 3rd; the whole first movement is built from them. "Ode to Joy" grows from a stepwise rise and fall. A good motif is simple, has a clear rhythm, and has something characteristic: a leap, a dotted rhythm, a repeated note.

## 2. Repetition, with variation

The listener's memory needs **repetition**: an idea heard once is forgotten, an idea heard three times is learned. But exact repetition gets boring, so composers repeat **with variation**. Ways to vary a motif:

- **Sequence**: repeat it starting on a different note (higher or lower), keeping the shape. Very common and very effective.
- **Change one note** or the ending.
- **Change the rhythm** slightly while keeping the shape.
- **Inversion**: turn the shape upside down (up becomes down).
- **Extension** or **fragmentation**: lengthen the motif, or repeat only part of it.

A good rule of thumb, from the folk song to the pop chorus: **say it, say it again, then say something different**. This is the AAB pattern you met in the blues and the basis of the sentence structure taught in composition.

## 3. Steps, leaps and the gap-fill rule

Melodies mostly move by **step** (2nds), with occasional **skips** (3rds) and less frequent **leaps** (4ths and larger). Stepwise motion sounds smooth and singable; leaps add energy and emotion. A useful principle, sometimes called **gap-fill**: after a large leap, the melody usually turns back and moves by step in the opposite direction, filling in the gap. "Somewhere over the Rainbow" leaps up an octave and then steps back down; so does countless other music. Several leaps in a row in the same direction tend to sound like an arpeggio rather than a tune.

## 4. Shape and the high point

Good melodies have a **contour**: an overall shape you could draw as a line. The classic shapes are the **arch** (rise to a high point, then fall), the **descending line** (start high and gently fall, very common in folk songs), the **wave** and the **rising line** (builds tension). Within a phrase or a whole melody, there is usually **one high point** (climax), reached once, often about two thirds of the way through, sometimes on a longer note or a strong beat. Reaching the same high note many times weakens it.

When you play a melody, find its high point and shape the phrase towards it and away from it: slightly louder approaching, a touch of space or weight at the top, relaxing after. This alone makes playing sound musical.

## 5. Phrases that ask and answer

Melodies are organised into **phrases**, usually two or four bars long, like lines of a poem. Phrases come in pairs: an **antecedent** (question), ending with an open, imperfect cadence, and a **consequent** (answer), often starting the same way but ending with a perfect cadence on the tonic. Together they make a **period**, typically eight bars. This is the most common structure of all, from Mozart to folk songs to children's songs.

## 6. The melody knows its chords

A melody implies a harmony. Strong beats and long notes usually belong to the chord; quick stepwise notes in between are passing and neighbour notes. Ending notes matter most: a phrase ending on the tonic (1) sounds final, on the 3rd or 5th more open, on the 2nd or 7th (over V) clearly unfinished. When you write a tune over a chord progression, put chord notes on the strong beats and let the other notes connect them.

## 7. Rhythm gives character

The same notes in a different rhythm make a different tune. Most good melodies use a **limited set of rhythmic patterns**, repeated, often with longer notes at the ends of phrases (letting the phrase breathe) and some variety (not all quarter notes). A distinctive rhythm (a dotted figure, a syncopation, an upbeat) is often what makes a melody instantly recognisable.

> **Motif** (a small idea) · **repeat with variation** (sequence!) · **mostly steps**, leaps filled in · **one high point** · **phrases in question and answer** · **chord notes on strong beats** · **a characteristic rhythm**.

## Writing your own

A simple recipe for an eight-bar tune:

1. Choose a key, a meter and a chord plan (for example I – IV – V – I | I – IV – V – I).
2. Invent a one-bar motif with a clear rhythm.
3. Bar 2: repeat it, or put it in sequence.
4. Bars 3–4: something different, ending on 2, 5 or 7 over V (the question).
5. Bars 5–6: the same as bars 1–2.
6. Bars 7–8: a new ending, rising to the high point and falling to the tonic over V–I (the answer).
7. Play it, sing it, change what doesn't feel right. Keep it in the Sketchbook.

The rules are not laws. Great melodies sometimes break them, but they break them on purpose, and they keep enough of them that the listener can follow.

## Common confusions

- **Melodies come from inspiration only.** Craft does most of the work; inspiration chooses the details.
- **More notes make a better tune.** Simple, repeated ideas are what people remember.
- **Repeating is lazy.** Repetition is what makes a melody memorable; variation keeps it alive.

## In short

Memorable melodies grow from a motif, repeated with variation (especially sequence). They move mostly by step, turn back after leaps, and have a clear shape with one high point. Phrases pair up as question and answer, ending on open and closed cadences. Strong beats carry chord notes, and a characteristic rhythm gives the tune its identity. Use these ideas both to shape your playing and to write your own tunes.
