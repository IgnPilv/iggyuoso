## What a dot does

You have already met one dotted note: the dotted half, which lasts three beats. The rule behind it works for every note:

> **A dot after a note adds half of that note's value.** Half note (2) + dot (1) = 3 beats. Quarter note (1) + dot (½) = 1½ beats. Eighth note (½) + dot (¼) = ¾ of a beat.

The dot always sits just to the right of the note head, in a space. (If the note is on a line, the dot is placed in the space just above, so it can be seen.) Do not confuse it with a dot *above or below* the note head, which means **staccato**, short and detached: that is a completely different sign about how to play the note, not how long to count it.

## The dotted quarter and eighth

A **dotted quarter note** lasts one and a half beats. On its own that leaves half a beat over, and the natural thing to fill it is an eighth note. Together, a dotted quarter plus an eighth make exactly two beats, a long-short pair that is one of the most familiar rhythms in music. You hear it at the start of *Auld Lang Syne* ("Should auld"), in *Silent Night*, in *Jingle Bells* ("Jin-gle all the way"), and in countless marches.

## Counting it

This is where counting "and" earns its keep. Count in eighth notes, "1 + 2 + 3 + 4 +":

- The dotted quarter starts on **1** and lasts through "+, 2": three eighth-note syllables.
- The eighth note falls on the **"+" of 2**.
- The next note starts on **3**.

Say it out loud, stressing where notes start: "**ONE** and two **AND** **THREE**..." The common mistake is to play the eighth note too early, on beat 2 itself, which turns the rhythm into two equal quarter notes, or to make it lazy and late. Counting the "and" puts it exactly in place.

## Why the dot and not a tie?

You could write the same sound as a quarter note **tied** to an eighth note: a curved line joining two notes into one held sound (ties have a chapter of their own). A dotted quarter is just a tidier way to write it. Composers use the dot when the long note sits neatly within the beats, and the tie when the note has to cross a bar line or would be confusing otherwise. Both mean: hold for one and a half beats.

## The reverse: short-long

Rhythms can also go the other way: an eighth note followed by a dotted quarter, short-long. It sounds snappy and off-balance, because the long note lands on the offbeat. It is common in Scottish folk music, where it is called the **Scotch snap** (in its fastest form). For now, notice that the counting method handles it with no trouble: eighth on "1", dotted quarter from "+" through "2 +".

## Dotted rhythms and character

Dotted rhythms give music a lilt or a spring. A march with dotted rhythms sounds proud and bouncy; a lullaby with gentle dotted patterns rocks. In Baroque music there is a grand style of overture built on sharp dotted rhythms, meant to sound like a king entering the room. As you play, try to feel the long note leaning forward into the short one, and the short one as a quick step into the next beat.

## Common confusions

- **Dot after vs dot over.** A dot after the head lengthens the note; a dot above or below means staccato.
- **The eighth note too early.** It belongs on the "and" of the second beat, not on the beat.
- **Dotting a dotted note again.** Double dots exist (adding a half and then a quarter), but you will rarely meet them for a long time.

## In short

A dot adds half the note's value. A dotted quarter lasts one and a half beats and is usually followed by an eighth note, together two beats, long-short. Count in eighths: the dotted quarter takes "1 + 2", the eighth falls on the "+" of 2. Dotted rhythms give music its bounce.
