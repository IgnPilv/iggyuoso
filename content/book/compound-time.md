## Two ways to split a beat

Every meter you have used so far, 2/4, 3/4 and 4/4, divides its beat into **two**: a quarter-note beat splits into two eighths. These are called **simple meters**. But some music feels as if every beat divides into **three**. Think of a jig, *Row, Row, Row Your Boat*, *Greensleeves*, or a lullaby that rocks: "ONE-and-a TWO-and-a". These are **compound meters**.

You could write such music in 2/4 with triplets everywhere, but that would be tiring to read. Instead, compound meters use a **dotted note as the beat**, because a dotted note divides naturally into three: a dotted quarter = three eighths.

## 6/8: two beats, each in three

The most common compound meter is **6/8**. Read literally, it says six eighth notes per bar. But it is **not** felt as six beats. It is felt as **two** beats, each a **dotted quarter**, each divided into three eighths:

**ONE** two three **FOUR** five six, or "**1** la li **2** la li".

That is the key to compound time: **the top number is not the number of beats**. For compound meters, divide the top number by three to get the beats:

| Meter | Beats per bar | Beat | Each beat splits into |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 6/8 | 2 | dotted quarter | 3 eighths |
| 9/8 | 3 | dotted quarter | 3 eighths |
| 12/8 | 4 | dotted quarter | 3 eighths |

Each compound meter is the cousin of a simple one: 6/8 feels like 2/4 with triplets, 9/8 like 3/4 with triplets, 12/8 like 4/4 with triplets. A slow blues or a 1950s rock ballad is usually 12/8: four big beats, each rolling in three.

> **Simple meter**: beats divide in two (2/4, 3/4, 4/4). **Compound meter**: beats divide in three, and the beat is a dotted note (6/8 = 2 beats, 9/8 = 3, 12/8 = 4). A top number of 6, 9 or 12 usually means compound.

## 3/8: the odd one out

**3/8** has three eighth notes per bar. Is it simple or compound? At a slow tempo it is felt as three eighth-note beats, a simple triple meter like a small 3/4. At a fast tempo the whole bar is felt as **one** beat (a dotted quarter), divided in three. Fast waltzes and scherzos often work this way. Let the tempo decide how you count it.

## The tempo counts the big beat

When a 6/8 piece says ♩. = 60, it means sixty **dotted-quarter** beats per minute, two per bar. In this app, the metronome and the tempo in compound meters follow the big beat too, just as musicians count it: in 6/8 you hear two clicks per bar, not six. This is important. If you count six quick beats instead of two big ones, the music loses its lilt and becomes plodding.

## Common rhythms in 6/8

Because each beat has three parts, the common patterns are different from simple time:

- **Three eighths** (1 la li): rolling.
- **Quarter + eighth** (1 – li): the classic lilting long-short, the jig rhythm. Hear it in *Row, Row, Row Your Boat*: "Row, row, row your boat".
- **Eighth + quarter** (1 la –): short-long.
- **Dotted quarter** (1 – –): one note for the whole beat.
- **Dotted half**: the whole bar.

## Feeling compound time

Compound time has a characteristic sway, like a boat on waves or a horse cantering. To feel it, walk or sway on the big beats while saying "1 la li 2 la li". Notice the difference between the quarter-eighth pair of 6/8 (a rounded long-short, two-thirds and one-third of the beat) and the dotted-eighth-sixteenth of simple time (a sharper long-short, three-quarters and one-quarter). Confusing them is a very common reading mistake.

## Simple or compound? A quick test

- Top number **2, 3 or 4**: simple (beats are the bottom-number note).
- Top number **6, 9 or 12**: compound (beats are dotted notes; divide by 3).
- Look at the beaming: in compound time, eighth notes are beamed in **threes**; in simple time, in **twos** (or fours).

## Common confusions

- **6/8 has six beats.** It has two, each of three eighths (unless it is very slow).
- **6/8 is the same as 3/4.** Both have six eighths per bar, but 3/4 is three beats of two (1 + 2 + 3 +), 6/8 is two beats of three (1 la li 2 la li). They sound different.
- **Dotted rhythms in 6/8.** The long-short of 6/8 is quarter + eighth, not dotted eighth + sixteenth.

## In short

Simple meters divide the beat in two; compound meters divide it in three and use a dotted note as the beat. 6/8 has two beats, 9/8 three, 12/8 four, each split into three eighths. 3/8 is felt in three slowly or in one quickly. Count the big beats, "1 la li 2 la li", and feel the sway. Beams in threes give compound time away.
