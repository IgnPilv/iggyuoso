## Only seven letters

There are 52 white keys on a full piano, but music uses only **seven letter names** for them: **A B C D E F G**. After G the alphabet does not go on to H; it starts again at A. So the white keys read, from left to right:

A B C D E F G A B C D E F G A B C ... and so on, all the way up.

This is called the **musical alphabet**. It is short on purpose. As you will see in the chapter on octaves, a note eight white keys higher sounds so much like the first one that it makes sense to give it the same name. Seven names, repeating, are all you need.

> **The musical alphabet** runs A B C D E F G, then starts again at A. Going up (to the right) you go forwards through the alphabet; going down (to the left) you go backwards: G F E D C B A G F...

Going backwards is worth practising out loud, because it is the one that catches people out. Say it now: G, F, E, D, C, B, A. You will need it every time a tune goes down.

## Finding C

Pianists find every white key from the black-key groups you met in the last chapter. The most important one to find is **C**.

> **C is the white key just to the left of a group of two black keys.**

Every group of two black keys has a C just below it, so there are many Cs on the piano: eight on a full-size one. They all sound like the same note, at different heights.

The C nearest the middle of the piano is called **middle C**. It is the meeting point between the two hands and between the two main staves of written music, so it is the most important single key you will learn. When you set up this app, it worked out where middle C is on your keyboard; it shows as a small orange dot on the keyboard pictures.

## D and E: the group of two

Once you have C, the next two letters are easy:

- **D** sits **between** the two black keys, right in the middle. A popular way to remember it: D is a dog in its kennel, with a black wall on each side.
- **E** is just to the **right** of the group of two.

```figure
keys: { notes: "C4 D4 E4", labels: names }
sound: "C4:q D4:q E4:h"
caption: C, D and E around a group of two black keys.
```

So the group of two black keys holds C, D and E between its outer edges. If you only learned these three you could already play a surprising number of tunes. *Hot Cross Buns* and the first line of *Mary Had a Little Lamb* use nothing else.

## F, G, A and B: the group of three

The group of three black keys holds the other four letters:

- **F** is just to the **left** of the group of three.
- **G** and **A** sit between the black keys, G between the first and second, A between the second and third.
- **B** is just to the **right** of the group of three, and right after it comes the next C.

```figure
keys: { notes: "F4 G4 A4 B4", labels: names }
sound: "F4:q G4:q A4:q B4:q"
caption: F, G, A and B around a group of three black keys.
```

Put the two groups together and you have a full set of seven: C D E around the two, F G A B around the three. That set, from one C to the next B, is the pattern that repeats from one end of the keyboard to the other.

## Two pairs of white keys with no black key

Look at the keyboard once more. Between most neighbouring white keys there is a black key. In two places there is not: between **E and F**, and between **B and C**. These two places are where the groups of black keys break off.

This looks like a small detail, but it is one of the most important facts in all of music theory. Those two pairs of white keys are closer together in sound than the other pairs. In a later chapter you will learn the word for this (a **semitone**), and you will find that the whole system of scales and keys is built on exactly where these two gaps fall. For now, simply notice: E–F and B–C are neighbours with nothing between them.

## Why does the piano start on C?

If the alphabet begins with A, why do pianists always start with C? The answer is history. Letter names for notes were in use in Europe more than a thousand years ago, when the most common scale started on the note we now call A. Over the following centuries a different scale became the everyday one, and that scale happens to begin on C and use only the white keys. The names stayed where they were, and the white-key scale from C to C became the first thing every piano pupil learns.

So "C" is not first in the alphabet, but it is first on the piano. That is also why C major, the scale of all white keys, is the first key you meet.

## Other names you might see

Not every country uses the letters. In France, Italy, Spain, Russia and much of Latin America notes are sung and named **do re mi fa sol la si**, where *do* is C. You may know these syllables from *The Sound of Music*. In German-speaking countries the letters are used, but B is called **H**, and the letter B means the black key just below it. You do not need any of this now. It only explains why a German score might say "H" and why a singer might talk about "do".

## Common confusions

- **"The black keys are sharps."** They are, but they are also flats; each black key has two names. That has its own chapter.
- **Counting from the end.** Never count keys from the bottom of the keyboard. Find the nearest group of two or three and name the key from there. It is faster and it works on every keyboard, of any size.
- **Mixing up D and G and A.** All three sit "between black keys". D is in the group of two; G and A are in the group of three, G on the left, A on the right.

## In short

Seven letters, A to G, name every white key, and they repeat up the keyboard. C sits left of the two black keys, D between them, E to their right. F sits left of the three, G and A between them, B to their right. E–F and B–C are the two pairs with no black key between them, and that small fact will turn out to shape every scale you play.
