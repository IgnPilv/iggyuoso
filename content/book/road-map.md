## Music repeats itself

Almost all music repeats: a verse comes back with new words, a chorus returns, a dance tune is played twice. Writing out every repeated bar would waste paper and make pieces look longer than they are. So composers use a set of signs that work like a road map, telling you where to go back to and where to jump ahead. Learning them is simple, and once you know them you will never get lost in a piece.

## The repeat sign

A **repeat sign** is a double bar line with two dots beside it. Dots on the right side of the lines (at the start of a section) mean "repeat from here". Dots on the left side (at the end of a section) mean "go back".

- When you reach an end repeat (dots before the double bar), go back to the matching start repeat and play that section again.
- If there is no start repeat, go back to the very beginning.
- The second time through, carry straight on past the repeat sign.

So a piece with sections A and B, each with repeat signs, is played **A A B B**. That is the shape of most dances from the Baroque era, including the Minuet in G you will learn.

> **Repeat signs**: dots facing the music mean "this section repeats". At the end sign go back to the start sign (or the beginning), play the section again, then carry on.

## First and second endings

Often a section is repeated almost exactly, but the last bar or two need to be different the second time, perhaps to finish the section rather than lead back to its start. Composers use **first and second endings** (also called volta brackets, from the Italian for "time"). These are brackets over the last bars, numbered **1.** and **2.**

1. The first time, play through the bars under bracket **1**, and take the repeat back.
2. The second time, **skip** the bars under bracket 1 and jump straight to bracket **2**.

It is like a fork in the road that you take one way the first time and the other way the second.

## D.C. and D.S.

For bigger jumps, composers use Italian instructions:

- **D.C.** (*Da Capo*, "from the head"): go back to the **beginning**.
- **D.S.** (*Dal Segno*, "from the sign"): go back to the **sign** 𝄋, a stylised S with a slash and two dots, somewhere in the music.

These are usually combined with an instruction about where to stop:

- **al Fine** ("to the end"): play until the word **Fine** (Italian for "end", said *FEE-nay*), and stop there. *D.C. al Fine* = go back to the start and play until *Fine*.
- **al Coda** ("to the tail"): play until the instruction **To Coda** (or the coda sign 𝄌), then jump to the separate ending section marked **Coda**. *D.S. al Coda* = go back to the sign, play until "To Coda", then jump to the Coda.

A **coda** is an ending section, a tail added to finish the piece off. Many pop songs have one too, an outro after the last chorus.

## Following a road map: an example

Imagine a piece laid out like this:

| Bars | What is written |
| --- | --- |
| 1–8 | Section A, with *Fine* at the end of bar 8 |
| 9–16 | Section B, with *D.C. al Fine* at the end of bar 16 |

You play bars 1–8 (A), then 9–16 (B), then go back to bar 1 because of D.C., and play A again until *Fine*, where you stop. The order is **A B A**, a very common shape called **ternary form**, which you will study in the chapter on form.

When a repeat sign comes after a D.C., there is an old convention that repeats are usually not taken the second time through, unless the music says otherwise. When in doubt, follow the recording or your teacher's advice.

## Before you play: plan the route

Good sight-readers look over the whole piece before starting, just as a driver looks at the map before setting off. Find the repeat signs, the endings, any D.C. or D.S., the sign, Fine and Coda. Some people trace the route with a finger first. Getting lost in the middle of a performance is far more often a road-map problem than a note problem.

The road-map signs also tell you about the **shape** of the music. A piece that says D.C. al Fine is telling you it has an A B A form: an opening idea, a contrasting middle, and a return. Noticing these shapes will make pieces easier to learn and remember.

## Common confusions

- **Which way do the dots face?** The dots are always on the side of the music that repeats.
- **Playing the first ending twice.** The second time, skip straight from before bracket 1 to bracket 2.
- **Fine is not the last bar.** It is where you stop after a D.C. or D.S., often in the middle of the page.

## In short

Repeat signs send you back to the matching sign or the start, once. First and second endings change the last bars the second time. D.C. goes back to the beginning, D.S. to the sign; al Fine stops at Fine, al Coda jumps to the Coda. Plan the route before you play, and notice the shape it reveals.
