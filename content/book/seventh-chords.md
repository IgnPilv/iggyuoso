## One more 3rd

A triad is three notes stacked in 3rds. Stack **one more 3rd** on top and you get a four-note chord whose top note is a 7th above the root: a **seventh chord**. Built on G in C major, using only notes of the scale: G B D **F**. That chord, the seventh chord on the dominant, is the **dominant 7th**, written **V7** in Roman numerals and **G7** as a chord symbol.

```figure

sound: "G3+B3+D4:h G3+B3+D4+F4:h C4+E4+G4:w"
caption: G major, then G7 with the added F, then home to C. Hear how much harder G7 pulls.
```

## Why V7 pulls so hard

The dominant triad already contains the leading note (B), which wants to rise to C. The added 7th, **F**, is degree 4 of the scale, which wants to fall to E. Between B and F is a **tritone** (an augmented 4th or diminished 5th), the most unstable interval of all. In the dominant 7th, that tritone resolves inwards: B rises to C and F falls to E, the two semitone tendencies of the key working at once. Add the root falling a 5th (G to C), and you have three separate forces pushing towards the tonic chord.

> **Dominant 7th (V7)** = major triad + minor 7th (G B D F). Its tritone (B–F) resolves inwards: the leading note rises to the tonic, the 7th falls a step to the 3rd.

```figure
staff: { notes: "B3+F4:h C4+E4:h", labels: names }
sound: "B3+F4:h C4+E4:w"
caption: The tritone inside G7 resolving: B up to C, F down to E.
```

That is why V7–I is the most common cadence in Western music and why a dominant 7th on its own sounds like a question that demands an answer. In minor keys V7 uses the raised 7th: in A minor, E G♯ B D resolves to A minor.

## Spelling a dominant 7th

A dominant 7th is a **major triad plus a minor 7th** above the root (10 semitones). So in any key: take the major chord on the 5th degree and add the note a tone below the root's octave.

| Key | V7 | Notes | Resolves to |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| C | G7 | G B D F | C |
| G | D7 | D F♯ A C | G |
| F | C7 | C E G B♭ | F |
| D | A7 | A C♯ E G | D |
| B♭ | F7 | F A C E♭ | B♭ |

## Inversions and voicings

With four notes, a seventh chord has **three inversions**, depending on which note is in the bass: root position (V7), first inversion (V⁶₅, 3rd in the bass), second inversion (V⁴₃, 5th in the bass) and third inversion (V⁴₂, 7th in the bass). Each gives a different bass line. A pianist often leaves out the 5th to make the chord easier to play and voice-lead: G B F (root, 3rd, 7th) still sounds perfectly like G7, because the 3rd and 7th carry its character. These **shell voicings** are the basis of jazz piano.

```figure
caption: G7 without its 5th, and G7 with B in the bass (first inversion), each resolving to C.
```

## The other seventh chords

Stack a 3rd on every diatonic triad and you get a seventh chord on every degree. Their qualities depend on the 3rds they are built from:

| Type | Built from | Symbol | Example | Sound |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Major 7th | major triad + major 7th | Cmaj7 | C E G B | dreamy, soft, jazzy |
| Dominant 7th | major triad + minor 7th | G7 | G B D F | tense, bluesy, wants to resolve |
| Minor 7th | minor triad + minor 7th | Dm7 | D F A C | mellow, smooth |
| Half-diminished 7th | diminished triad + minor 7th | Bm7♭5 (Bø7) | B D F A | dark, questioning |
| Diminished 7th | diminished triad + diminished 7th | B°7 | B D F A♭ | dramatic, suspenseful (in minor keys) |

In a major key the diatonic sevenths are: **Imaj7, ii7, iii7, IVmaj7, V7, vi7, viiø7**. In C: Cmaj7, Dm7, Em7, Fmaj7, G7, Am7, Bø7. Notice that only the chord on the 5th degree is a dominant 7th. That is why the name "dominant 7th" is used even when the chord appears elsewhere (as in secondary dominants and the blues): it always means a major triad with a minor 7th.

## Sevenths in different styles

In classical music, the 7th of a chord is a dissonance that is "prepared" and resolved carefully by step downward. In the blues, dominant 7ths are used on every chord (I7, IV7, V7) and never need to resolve; they are the colour of the style. In jazz, almost every chord has a 7th, and often more notes on top (9ths, 11ths, 13ths). In pop, major 7ths and minor 7ths give a smooth, soulful sound. Learning to hear and play the main seventh-chord types opens up all these styles.

## Common confusions

- **"7th chord" always means dominant 7th.** In casual speech "G7" does, but Cmaj7 and Dm7 are seventh chords too.
- **Cmaj7 vs C7.** Cmaj7 has B (major 7th); C7 has B♭ (minor 7th). Very different sounds.
- **The 7th resolves up.** In V7–I the 7th falls a step (F to E).

## In short

A seventh chord adds a 3rd on top of a triad. The dominant 7th, a major triad plus a minor 7th on degree 5, holds a tritone between the leading note and degree 4, which resolves inward to the tonic chord: the strongest pull in harmony. Seventh chords have inversions and are often voiced without the 5th. The main types, major 7th, dominant 7th, minor 7th, half-diminished and diminished, colour classical, blues, jazz and pop harmony.
