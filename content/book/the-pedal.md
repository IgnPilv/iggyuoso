## What the pedal does

Inside an acoustic piano, every string (except the very highest) has a **damper**, a pad of felt resting on it. When you press a key, its damper lifts and the string can ring. When you let go, the damper falls back and stops the sound. The right-hand pedal, the **sustain pedal** (also called the **damper pedal**, or loosely the "loud pedal"), lifts **all** the dampers at once. While it is down, every note you play keeps ringing after you release the key, until the pedal comes up.

That has two effects. First, notes can be **joined** even when your hands cannot hold them, for example when a chord in the left hand must be held while the hand jumps elsewhere. Second, the whole piano **resonates**: undamped strings vibrate in sympathy with the notes you play, giving a richer, warmer sound. A digital piano imitates both.

Most pianos have two or three pedals. The left one, the **soft pedal** (*una corda*), makes the sound softer and gentler. A middle one, if present, varies: on grand pianos it is usually the **sostenuto** pedal, which sustains only the notes held down when it was pressed. The sustain pedal is by far the most used, and it is the one this chapter is about.

## How it is written

Pedal markings appear below the bass staff. Two common styles:

- **Ped.** where the pedal goes down and **✱** (an asterisk-like sign) where it comes up.
- A **line** under the music: a bracket starts where the pedal goes down, continues while it is held, and ends with an upward tick where it lifts. A notch in the line (like a little inverted V) means **change the pedal**: lift and press again quickly.

In this app the pedal appears under the staff in the line style, and as a strip down the right edge of the falling notes.

## Simple pedalling

The simplest use is to press the pedal down with a chord or note, hold it, and lift it before the next. This is called **direct** or **rhythmic** pedalling: pedal and keys go down together. It gives each chord resonance but leaves a small gap between chords, because the pedal lifts and the dampers stop the sound before the next chord is struck.

## Legato pedalling: the key skill

To join chords smoothly, with no gap and no blur, pianists use **legato pedalling** (also called **syncopated** or **overlapping** pedalling). It feels backwards at first:

1. Play the first chord and press the pedal **just after** it.
2. When you play the next chord, **lift the pedal at the same moment the new chord goes down**.
3. Press the pedal down again **just after** the new chord sounds.
4. Repeat for every chord: hands down, pedal up, pedal down.

The pedal goes **up when the keys go down**, and **down just after**. The new chord catches the pedal on its way down, so the dampers clear the old harmony at exactly the moment the new one sounds, and the new harmony is then held. The result is perfectly connected chords with no smearing between them.

## Change with the harmony

The golden rule: **change the pedal whenever the harmony changes**. If you hold the pedal through two different chords, their notes blend into a muddy noise. Melodies too: notes of a scale held together in the pedal blur. That is why pedal markings usually change with each chord, and why, when there are no markings, the pianist decides by ear. Listen while you pedal: if it sounds blurred, change more often; if it sounds dry and choppy, pedal more.

The best pedalling is decided by the **ear**, not the foot. Different pianos and rooms need different amounts: a resonant grand in a big hall needs less pedal than a small digital piano at home.

## Technique

- Keep your **heel on the floor** and press with the ball of your foot. The pedal is a hinge, not a button to stamp on.
- Keep the foot **in contact** with the pedal; do not lift it into the air between changes.
- The pedal need not go all the way down; "half pedal" clears the sound partially, a subtle technique for later.
- Practise pedalling slowly, chord by chord, listening for clean changes with no gaps and no blur.

## A little history

Early pianos in the 1700s had knee levers instead of pedals; foot pedals became standard around 1800. Beethoven was one of the first composers to write pedal markings, and Chopin's music depends on the pedal for its singing, shimmering sound. For Bach, written for instruments with no sustain pedal, pianists use it sparingly or not at all, to keep the separate lines clear.

## Common confusions

- **It is the "loud pedal".** It sustains; any extra loudness comes from resonance.
- **Pressing pedal and chord together joins chords.** That leaves gaps. For legato, press just after.
- **More pedal is more beautiful.** Too much pedal blurs. Change with the harmony.

## In short

The sustain pedal lifts all the dampers, so notes keep ringing and the piano resonates. It is marked under the staff with Ped. and ✱ or a line with notches for changes. Legato pedalling lifts the pedal as the new chord sounds and presses again just after, giving smooth connection without blur. Change the pedal with every change of harmony, keep your heel down, and let your ear decide.
