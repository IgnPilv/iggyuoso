## Music moves because it is unsettled

Why does a melody feel like it is going somewhere? Why does the end of a song feel like an end? Because inside a key, notes are not equal. Some feel **stable**: you could stop on them and feel at rest. Others feel **unstable**: they lean towards a neighbour, and stopping on them leaves you hanging. Music moves by creating that leaning, that tension, and then releasing it. Learning to feel it is one of the biggest steps from playing notes to playing music.

## Stable notes: the tonic triad

In a major key, the most stable notes are the three notes of the **tonic triad**: degrees **1, 3 and 5**. In C major, that is C, E and G. Stop a melody on any of them and it feels, more or less, at rest. Of the three, **1** is the most final, the true home. **5** is stable but open, like a comma. **3** is stable and colourful; it is the note that tells you the key is major (or, as a minor 3rd, minor).

## Unstable notes and where they lean

The other four degrees, **2, 4, 6 and 7**, are unstable. Each tends to move by step to a nearby stable note:

| Degree | In C | Tends to move to | Why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 7 (leading note) | B | up to **1** (C) | only a semitone below the tonic: the strongest pull |
| 4 (subdominant) | F | down to **3** (E) | a semitone above the mediant |
| 2 (supertonic) | D | down to **1** (C), or up to 3 | a step from two stable notes |
| 6 (submediant) | A | down to **5** (G) | a step above the dominant |

```figure

sound: "B4:h C5:h F4:h E4:h D4:h C4:h A4:h G4:h"
caption: Each unstable note resolving to its stable neighbour: 7→1, 4→3, 2→1, 6→5.
```

Notice the two strongest pulls are the two **semitone** steps of the major scale: 7 up to 1, and 4 down to 3. Semitones pull harder than whole tones, because the notes are so close that the unstable one seems to want to slide into the stable one. Play B and hold it, and you can almost feel your finger wanting to move to C.

> **Stable**: 1, 3, 5 (the tonic triad). **Unstable**: 2, 4, 6, 7, each leaning by step to a stable neighbour. The strongest pulls are the semitones: **7 → 1** and **4 → 3**.

## Tension and release in a melody

Look at almost any tune and you will find this shape again and again: the melody moves away from the stable notes, lingers on unstable ones, and then settles. Long notes and phrase endings tend to fall on stable notes; unstable notes tend to come in passing, or on strong beats where they create a moment of tension before resolving.

Sing *Twinkle Twinkle*. The first phrase ends on G ("star"), degree 5: stable but open, a comma. The very last phrase ends on C, degree 1: a full stop. The middle phrase, "Up above the world so high", circles G F E D, leaning down the scale but not landing on home. The tune breathes in and out with tension and release.

## Tension in harmony

The same thing happens with chords. The tonic chord (I) is stable. Chords containing unstable notes, especially the leading note, are unstable. The dominant chord (V), which contains the leading note, is the most unstable of the primary triads, and that is exactly why it resolves so satisfyingly to I. A dominant 7th chord adds degree 4 to that, so it contains both semitone pulls at once (B→C and F→E): maximum tension, and maximum release when it resolves.

## Expectation: why endings work

Your ear knows all this already. Years of listening to music have taught you, without words, which notes are stable in a key. That is why a song that stops on the leading note sounds broken, and why a cadence sounds like an ending. Composers play with these expectations: sometimes they delay the resolution to build suspense, sometimes they resolve somewhere unexpected for a surprise. You will meet these tricks in the chapters on cadences.

## Hearing degrees

This is the foundation of the ear exercises that play a cadence and then a single note. Instead of trying to recognise the note's absolute pitch, feel its **tendency**. Does it feel at rest? Then it is probably 1, 3 or 5: 1 if totally final, 5 if open, 3 if sweet. Does it lean upward urgently? Probably 7. Does it want to sink? Probably 4 or 6. After each answer the app lets the note resolve, so you hear where it wanted to go. Over time, these feelings become as clear as colours.

## Common confusions

- **Unstable means wrong.** Unstable notes are essential; they create the movement.
- **Every note must resolve immediately.** Resolution can be delayed, decorated or reached later. The pull remains.
- **Stability is about loudness or length.** It is about the note's place in the key, though composers often put stable notes on long, strong positions.

## In short

In a key, 1, 3 and 5 (the tonic triad) are stable; 2, 4, 6 and 7 are unstable and lean by step to stable neighbours, most strongly 7 up to 1 and 4 down to 3. Melodies breathe by moving between tension and release, with phrase endings on stable notes. V is unstable because it holds the leading note. Feeling these tendencies is the key to hearing scale degrees.
