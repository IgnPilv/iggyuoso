## Same number, different size

Play C up to E. That is a 3rd: C, D, E. Now play C up to E♭. Still a 3rd, still three letters: C, D, E♭. But it sounds clearly different. The first is bright and open; the second is softer, darker, a little sad. On the keyboard the difference is one semitone: C to E is **four** semitones, C to E♭ is **three**.

```figure
staff: { notes: "C4+E4:h C4+Eb4:h | C4:q E4:q C4:q Eb4:q", labels: names }
sound: "C4+E4:h C4+Eb4:h C4:q E4:q C4:q Eb4:q"
caption: C–E, a major 3rd, then C–E flat, a minor 3rd. Same number, one semitone apart in size.
```

So an interval's **number** (2nd, 3rd, 4th...) only tells you how many letters it spans. To describe it exactly you also need its **quality**: how many semitones it really contains. The two qualities you meet first are **major** (the larger) and **minor** (the smaller, by one semitone). These are the same words used for keys and chords, and as you will see, that is no coincidence.

## Counting semitones

The surest way to tell major from minor is to count the semitones, every key black or white, from the bottom note to the top note (not counting the starting key itself):

| Interval | Semitones | Example from C |
| --- | --- | --- |
| minor 2nd | 1 | C–D♭ |
| major 2nd | 2 | C–D |
| minor 3rd | 3 | C–E♭ |
| major 3rd | 4 | C–E |

A **minor 2nd** is the same as a semitone; a **major 2nd** is a tone. You have known these all along: the major scale's "T T S" is really "major 2nd, major 2nd, minor 2nd".

> **2nds**: minor = 1 semitone, major = 2. **3rds**: minor = 3 semitones, major = 4. In each pair, minor is one semitone smaller than major.

## 3rds on white keys

Not every 3rd between white keys is the same size. Count them:

- C–E, F–A and G–B are **major 3rds** (4 semitones): there are two whole tones inside.
- D–F, E–G, A–C and B–D are **minor 3rds** (3 semitones): one of the steps inside is a white-key semitone (E–F or B–C).

```figure
keys: { notes: "C4 E4 D4 F4", labels: names }
sound: "C4+E4:h D4+F4:h"
caption: C–E is major (4 semitones); D–F is minor (3), because E–F inside it is only a semitone.
```

This explains why some white-key chords sound bright and others dark. The C chord (C E G) starts with a major 3rd; the D chord on white keys (D F A) starts with a minor 3rd. You will put this to work in the chapter on minor chords.

A quick rule for 3rds on white keys: if the 3rd **contains E–F or B–C**, it is minor; if not, it is major.

## Changing the size with sharps and flats

Sharps and flats change an interval's quality without changing its number:

- Raising the top note by a semitone makes the interval one semitone bigger: D–F (minor 3rd) becomes D–F♯ (major 3rd).
- Lowering the top note makes it smaller: C–E (major) becomes C–E♭ (minor).
- Changing the bottom note works the other way round: raising the bottom note makes the interval smaller.

```figure

```

## Why the 3rd is so important

The 3rd is the interval that decides whether a chord, a scale or a key is major or minor. A major chord has a major 3rd from its root; a minor chord has a minor 3rd. A major scale has a major 3rd between degrees 1 and 3; a minor scale has a minor 3rd. That single semitone, E or E♭ above C, is the difference between the bright sound we call major and the darker sound we call minor.

Many people describe major as "happy" and minor as "sad". That is a reasonable starting point for the ear, and it is how the ear branch first introduces them. But it is only a starting point: there are cheerful songs in minor and heartbreaking ones in major. Think of major and minor as two colours rather than two emotions.

## Hearing 2nds and 3rds

- A **minor 2nd** sounds tense and close, the two notes grinding together: the shark theme from *Jaws*.
- A **major 2nd** is gentler: the start of *Happy Birthday* going up to "birth".
- A **minor 3rd** is soft and slightly melancholy: the start of *Greensleeves* ("A-las"), or a playground "na-na".
- A **major 3rd** is bright and clear: the start of *Oh When the Saints*.

## Common confusions

- **Counting the first key.** Count the semitone steps, not the keys: C to D is two semitones (C→C♯, C♯→D).
- **"Major is bigger" means the number.** Both a major and a minor 3rd are 3rds; major is one semitone wider.
- **All white-key 3rds are the same.** No: four of the seven are minor.

## In short

An interval has a number (how many letters) and a quality (its exact size). Minor is one semitone smaller than major: minor 2nd 1, major 2nd 2, minor 3rd 3, major 3rd 4 semitones. On white keys, a 3rd containing E–F or B–C is minor. The 3rd decides whether chords, scales and keys are major or minor.
