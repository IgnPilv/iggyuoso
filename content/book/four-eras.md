## Why eras matter to a pianist

When you open a new piece, the composer's name and dates tell you a great deal: how the music is built, what the instrument of the time could do, how much pedal to use, how freely to bend the tempo, even how to play an ornament. Musicians divide the music of the last four centuries into a few broad **eras** (or periods). The borders are blurry and every great composer breaks the rules of their time, but the eras are a map that every music student learns, and they will help you make sense of everything you play.

| Era | Roughly | Keyboard of the time | Famous names |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Baroque | 1600–1750 | harpsichord, clavichord, organ | Bach, Handel, Scarlatti, Couperin |
| Classical | 1750–1820 | early piano (fortepiano) | Haydn, Mozart, early Beethoven, Clementi |
| Romantic | 1820–1900 | the modern grand piano takes shape | Schubert, Chopin, Schumann, Liszt, Brahms, Tchaikovsky |
| Modern (20th century and after) | 1900– | modern piano, electric and digital keyboards | Debussy, Ravel, Bartók, Prokofiev, Gershwin, Satie, Glass |

## Baroque (about 1600–1750)

The word comes from a Portuguese term for an irregular pearl: ornate, busy, full of decoration. Baroque keyboard music was written mostly for the **harpsichord**, which plucks its strings and cannot play loud and soft by touch. So composers created interest through **lines**: two, three or four independent melodies woven together. This is **polyphony** (or counterpoint), and its high point is J. S. Bach's inventions, fugues and preludes.

What to listen for and how to play:

- **Independent voices**: each hand (and sometimes each finger) has its own melody. Bring out the entries of the main theme.
- **A steady, motoric pulse**: rhythms run on in even notes; the tempo stays firm.
- **Terraced dynamics**: whole sections loud or soft, rather than gradual swells (the harpsichord changed volume by changing keyboard).
- **Ornaments**: trills, mordents and turns decorate the melody.
- **Dances**: minuets, gigues, sarabandes, gavottes. The Minuet in G you know (once credited to Bach, now to Christian Petzold) is a perfect example.
- **Little or no pedal** on a modern piano; clarity comes from the fingers.

## Classical (about 1750–1820)

Around 1750, taste changed towards **clarity, balance and elegance**. The new **fortepiano** could play soft and loud by touch (hence its name), and composers loved the new expressive range. Instead of many interwoven lines, Classical music usually has **one clear melody with an accompaniment** (homophony), often an Alberti bass in the left hand. Phrases fall into neat, balanced pairs, question and answer, four bars plus four. The great forms of the era are the **sonata** and the **symphony**.

- **Singing melody over a light accompaniment**: keep the left hand quieter.
- **Balanced phrases** with clear cadences.
- **Gradual dynamics** (crescendo, diminuendo) and sudden contrasts (*sf*), now possible on the piano.
- **Clean articulation**: slurs and staccatos matter; the pedal is used sparingly.
- **Steady tempo**, with small breaths at the ends of phrases.

The Ode to Joy, from Beethoven's Ninth Symphony, shows the Classical love of simple, balanced melody, though Beethoven himself bridges into the Romantic era.

## Romantic (about 1820–1900)

The 19th century put **emotion and the individual** at the centre. The piano grew into the iron-framed grand we know: louder, richer, with a longer sustain. It became the instrument of the age, and composers wrote intimate **character pieces**, songs without words, nocturnes, waltzes, mazurkas, and dazzling showpieces.

- **Rich harmony**: more chromatic notes, seventh chords and distant modulations.
- **Long, singing melodies**, often in the middle of the texture.
- **Wide range** of the keyboard and big dynamics, from *ppp* to *fff*.
- **Pedal** used constantly to blend harmonies and sustain the sound.
- **Rubato**: the tempo bends expressively, pushing forward and holding back, while the overall pulse survives.
- **Titles and moods**: Schumann's *Scenes from Childhood*, Chopin's *Nocturnes*, Tchaikovsky's *Album for the Young*.

## Modern (from about 1900)

The 20th century broke many rules and explored new sounds. **Impressionists** like Debussy and Ravel used whole-tone and pentatonic scales, unresolved seventh and ninth chords and the pedal to make shimmering colour. **Bartók** brought in folk rhythms and sharp dissonance; **Prokofiev** wit and percussive energy; **Satie** spare simplicity; **Gershwin** the blues and jazz. Later came minimalism (Glass, Reich), film music, and the jazz and pop piano styles you can learn in this app.

- **New scales**: modes, whole-tone, pentatonic, blues.
- **Free use of dissonance**: chords no longer need to resolve.
- **Rhythmic adventure**: changing meters, odd meters, syncopation.
- **Colour** as an aim in itself.

> **Baroque**: lines woven together, steady pulse, ornaments, harpsichord. **Classical**: one clear melody, balanced phrases, the early piano. **Romantic**: emotion, rich harmony, pedal and rubato. **Modern**: new scales, free dissonance, new rhythms and colours.

## Using the eras when you practise

When you learn a new piece, ask which era it comes from, and let that guide your choices. A Bach minuet wants clear fingers, a steady pulse and almost no pedal. A Mozart sonata wants a singing right hand, a quiet left hand and clean phrase endings. A Chopin nocturne wants a long legato line, generous pedal and a bending tempo. A Debussy prelude wants colour and atmosphere. Playing Bach like Chopin, or Chopin like Bach, is a common beginner mistake, and avoiding it is the beginning of musical style.

## Common confusions

- **"Classical music" means all old music.** In everyday speech it does; to musicians "Classical" is also the specific era of Haydn and Mozart.
- **The eras had sharp borders.** They overlap: Beethoven belongs to both the Classical and the Romantic.
- **Old pieces should be played without expression.** Every era is expressive; only the means differ.

## In short

Four broad eras shape keyboard music: Baroque (1600–1750, counterpoint, harpsichord, ornaments, steady pulse), Classical (1750–1820, melody and accompaniment, balanced phrases, the fortepiano), Romantic (1820–1900, emotion, rich harmony, pedal, rubato) and Modern (1900 on, new scales, dissonance, rhythm and colour). Knowing a piece's era tells you how to play it.
