## Beyond I, IV and V

The primary triads can harmonise any simple tune, but music built only from them can sound plain, like a story told with a very small vocabulary. The other diatonic chords, especially the minor ones, **ii**, **iii** and **vi**, add colour, softness and new routes between home and away. In C major they are **Dm** (D F A), **Em** (E G B) and **Am** (A C E).

## Relatives and substitutes

Each minor chord shares **two notes** with a primary chord, so it can do a similar job with a different colour:

- **vi** (A C E) shares C and E with **I** (C E G). It is the **relative minor** chord, and it can stand in for I. It sounds like home, but in shadow. When a progression lands on vi where you expected I, it is called a **deceptive cadence**: the music seems about to finish, then turns away.
- **ii** (D F A) shares F and A with **IV** (F A C). It does the same "moving away" job, and it is the most common chord before V: **ii – V – I** is one of the strongest progressions there is.
- **iii** (E G B) shares notes with both I and V. It is the least used of the three, a soft, slightly ambiguous colour.

So the three jobs from earlier grow: **tonic** function (I, vi, sometimes iii), **subdominant** function (IV, ii), **dominant** function (V, vii°).

> **vi** stands in for I (home in shadow). **ii** stands in for IV and leads beautifully to V. Chords sharing two notes can share a job.

## How chords like to move

Over centuries, composers found that some chord moves sound especially natural. A few guidelines:

- **Roots falling by a 5th** (or rising a 4th) are the strongest: vi → ii → V → I is a chain of falling 5ths (A → D → G → C).
- **Roots moving by step** sound smooth and connected: IV → V, V → vi.
- **Roots falling by a 3rd** gently change colour while sharing two notes: I → vi → IV → ii.

Following the "functions", music tends to flow **tonic → subdominant → dominant → tonic**: I → (vi) → ii or IV → V → I.

## Progressions everyone uses

A **chord progression** is a series of chords. A few progressions are so common that you have heard each of them in hundreds of songs:

| Progression | In C | Where you hear it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I – IV – V – I | C F G C | folk, hymns, early rock and roll |
| I – V – vi – IV | C G Am F | countless pop songs since the 1990s ("Let It Be", "With or Without You", "No Woman No Cry") |
| I – vi – IV – V | C Am F G | 1950s doo-wop ("Stand by Me", "Heart and Soul") |
| vi – IV – I – V | Am F C G | the same four chords starting on vi: a darker, minor-sounding pop progression |
| ii – V – I | Dm G C | the backbone of jazz, and countless classical cadences |
| I – IV – vi – V | C F Am G | pop ballads |

Because these are written in Roman numerals, they work in every key. I – V – vi – IV in G major is G D Em C; in D major it is D A Bm G. A pianist who knows these progressions can accompany a huge range of songs by ear, and can recognise them in music, which is exactly what the ear branch trains with its progression questions.

## The circle progression

Chain falling 5ths through all the diatonic chords and you get the **circle progression**: I – IV – vii° – iii – vi – ii – V – I (in C: C F B° Em Am Dm G C). Each root falls a 5th (or rises a 4th) to the next. It sounds inevitable, like a staircase going down. Baroque composers loved it, and so did writers of jazz standards like *Autumn Leaves* and pop songs like *I Will Survive*.

## Voicing progressions at the piano

At the piano, the usual way to play these progressions is **root in the left hand, chord in the right**, with the right-hand chords voice-led: keep common notes, move the rest by step. For I – V – vi – IV in C, a smooth right hand is C E G → B D G → C E A → C F A. The hand hardly moves, the left hand plays C, G, A, F, and the result sounds like a real accompaniment. The harmony lessons drill this in several keys.

## Common confusions

- **Minor chords make the song sad.** In a major key, ii, iii and vi add colour but the key stays major.
- **Progressions are only for pop.** The same progressions run through Bach, Mozart, jazz and folk.
- **vi is a different key.** It is a chord in the same key; a piece only changes key when the tonic itself moves (modulation, later).

## In short

ii, iii and vi are the minor chords of a major key. vi shares two notes with I and can stand in for it; ii shares two with IV and leads to V. Chords tend to flow tonic → subdominant → dominant → tonic, with roots falling by 5ths the strongest move. Famous progressions, I–IV–V–I, I–V–vi–IV, I–vi–IV–V, ii–V–I, work in every key and can be voiced smoothly with the root in the left hand.
