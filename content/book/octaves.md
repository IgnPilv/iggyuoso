## The same note, higher

Play middle C, then the next C up, eight white keys to the right. Then play them together. They are clearly different heights, yet they blend so completely that they almost sound like one note. Play C and then D together, by contrast, and you hear two separate notes rubbing against each other.

```figure
keys: { notes: "C4 C5", labels: names }
sound: "C4:h C5:h C4+C5:w"
caption: Middle C, the C above, then both at once. They melt into one sound.
```

This distance, from one note to the next note with the same letter, is called an **octave**. The word comes from the Latin for "eight", because counting both ends there are eight white keys from C up to the next C: C D E F G A B C.

## Why octaves sound alike

Remember from the first chapter that pitch is the speed of a vibration. When you go up an octave, the vibration becomes **exactly twice as fast**. Middle C vibrates about 262 times a second; the C above it about 523 times; the one above that about 1,047.

Because one is exactly double the other, the two waves line up perfectly: every second wiggle of the high note lands on a wiggle of the low one. Your ear hears that perfect fit as sameness. This is not a Western habit. People all over the world hear octaves as "the same note", and when men and women sing a tune together without thinking about it, they usually sing it an octave apart.

> **An octave** is the distance from a note to the next note of the same name. Its vibration is exactly twice as fast, which is why the two sound like the same note at different heights.

## Telling the Cs apart

Since there are many Cs (and many of every letter), we need a way to say which one we mean. The system used in this app, and in most modern books, is called **scientific pitch notation**: each note gets its letter and a number for its octave.

- **Middle C is C4.**
- The C above it is **C5**, the one above that **C6**.
- The C below middle C is **C3**, then **C2**, and so on.

The number belongs to every note from a C up to the B above it. So the notes going up from middle C are C4 D4 E4 F4 G4 A4 B4, and then the next C starts a new number: C5.

```figure
keys: { notes: "C3 C4 C5 C6", labels: names }
sound: "C3:q C4:q C5:q C6:q"
caption: C3, C4 (middle C), C5 and C6.
```

> **The number changes at C**, not at A. B3 is the white key just below middle C; C4 is middle C itself. That is the single most common mistake with octave numbers.

On a full 88-key piano the lowest key is **A0** (A in octave zero, below the first C), and the highest is **C8**. The A just above middle C, **A4**, is the note orchestras tune to: it vibrates 440 times a second.

## Your keyboard's middle

Many keyboards are smaller than 88 keys, and some are set to play an octave higher or lower than they look. That is why this app asked you to press the lowest and highest keys when you set it up: it worked out which of your keys is really C4 and confirmed it with you. Every exercise in the app is written with this one convention: **middle C is C4**. When a lesson says "play G4", it means the G just above middle C, whatever your keyboard calls it.

## Octaves in music

Octaves are everywhere once you listen for them:

- A bass line is often doubled an octave lower to make it fuller. Your left hand will soon play octaves with the thumb and little finger.
- When a tune is too high or too low to sing comfortably, people simply move it up or down an octave and it is still "the same tune".
- On the staff (next chapters), moving a note up an octave moves it up by seven lines and spaces: the same letter, much higher on the page.

## Common confusions

- **"An octave is eight keys."** Eight *white* keys, counting both ends. Counting the black keys as well, it is twelve steps; you will see why in the chapter on semitones.
- **Starting the number at A.** The number goes up at C. A4 is above C4; A3 is below.
- **"High C" and "low C".** People use these loosely. In this app we always say which C we mean by its number.

## In short

An octave goes from one note to the next note of the same letter; it vibrates exactly twice as fast and so sounds like the same note, higher. Every note has a letter and an octave number; the number goes up at each C. Middle C is C4, A4 is the tuning note, and a full piano runs from A0 to C8.
