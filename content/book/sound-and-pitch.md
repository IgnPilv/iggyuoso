## A sound has four sides

Before we touch a single letter or line of notation, it helps to ask a very plain question: what is a musical sound? You already know far more about it than you think. You can tell a dog's bark from a doorbell with your eyes closed, you know when someone is shouting, and you notice when a phone rings for a long time. Each of those judgements is about one side of a sound, and music uses exactly the same four sides.

- **Pitch**: how high or low the sound is. A bird's chirp is high; a lorry's engine is low.
- **Loudness**: how strong the sound is. Musicians call this **dynamics**.
- **Duration**: how long the sound lasts. Short and long sounds in a pattern make **rhythm**.
- **Tone colour**: what kind of sound it is. A piano and a violin can play the same note at the same loudness and still sound nothing alike. The French word **timbre** (say "tam-bruh") is often used for it.

Everything you will learn in this book is a way of organising one or more of these four sides. Note names and the staff organise pitch. Note values and time signatures organise duration. Words such as *forte* and *piano* organise loudness. Tone colour is mostly decided by the instrument, though a good pianist can change it a great deal with touch and pedal.

> **The big four.** Pitch (high or low), dynamics (loud or soft), duration (long or short) and tone colour (what it sounds like). When something in music confuses you, ask which of the four it is about.

## What pitch really is

A sound is a vibration. When you press a piano key, a small felt hammer hits a steel string and the string shivers back and forth very fast. Those shivers push the air, the air pushes your eardrum, and your brain hears a tone.

How fast the string vibrates decides the pitch. A long, thick string vibrates slowly and sounds **low**. A short, thin one vibrates quickly and sounds **high**. The lowest note on a piano shakes about 27 times every second; the highest shakes more than 4,000 times. Your ear turns that speed into a feeling of "low" and "high", the same way your eye turns light into colours.

Notice that the words "high" and "low" are only a picture. Nothing is physically higher in the air. But nearly every culture uses the idea, and written music uses it too: higher notes are drawn higher on the page, as you will see in a few chapters.

## Pitch is not loudness

The most common mix-up for beginners, children and adults alike, is between **high** and **loud**, and between **low** and **soft**. People say "turn it up" when they mean louder, so it is easy to blur the two. In music they are completely separate.

A tiny bird can sing very high and very softly. A big drum can be very low and very loud. You can play a high note gently, or a low note hard. Try it at the keyboard: press a key on the far left gently, then press it firmly. The pitch stays the same; only the loudness changes. Then play a key on the far right just as gently. Now the pitch is different, but the loudness is the same.

## The keyboard is a map of pitch

The piano is a wonderful instrument for learning theory because it lays pitch out in a straight line in front of you. The rule is simple and never changes:

> **Left is low, right is high.** Every key sounds a little higher than the key to its left. Moving to the right is "going up"; moving to the left is "going down".

That is why the bass notes of a piece, the deep foundation, are usually played by the left hand, and the tune, which often sits higher, by the right. It is also why your teacher, or this app, will say "go up a step" and mean "move one key to the right".

A modern piano has 88 keys: 52 white and 36 black. You will not need most of them for a long time, and the keyboard you use may have fewer. What matters is the pattern, which repeats over and over from one end to the other.

## The black keys are your landmarks

If every key looked the same, you would be lost. Look closely and you will see that the black keys are not spread evenly. They sit in **groups of two** and **groups of three**, taking turns all the way up the keyboard: two, three, two, three.

```figure

caption: One group of two black keys, then one group of three.
```

These groups are the map you will use for the rest of your life at the piano. In the next chapter every white key gets a name, and every name is found by its place next to a group: "just left of the two", "between the second and third of the three". Pianists do not count keys from the end of the keyboard. They glance at the black-key groups, the way you find your house by the shape of the street rather than by counting every house from the edge of town.

Spend a minute just looking. Put your left hand on any group of two and your right hand on any group of three. Slide your hand along and feel the gaps between the groups with your fingertips. Many pianists can find their place without looking at all, purely by feeling those gaps.

## Where you will meet this again

- **Ear**: the very first ear exercises ask whether two notes are the same or different, and whether the second is higher or lower. That is pure pitch, and it is a skill that grows with practice, even if you believe you "can't hear" it now.
- **Playing**: when a tune goes up on the page, your hand will move right. When the bass goes down, your left hand will move left.
- **Reading**: the staff, which you will meet soon, is just this keyboard turned on its side. High notes high, low notes low.

## In short

Music is made of four sides of sound: pitch, loudness, duration and tone colour. Pitch comes from how fast something vibrates, and on the piano it runs from low on the left to high on the right. High is not the same as loud. The black keys come in groups of two and three, and those groups are your map of the keyboard.
