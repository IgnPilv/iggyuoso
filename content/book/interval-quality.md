## Two families of intervals

You know that 2nds and 3rds come in two sizes, major and minor. What about the others? Intervals fall into two families:

- The **perfect** family: **unison, 4th, 5th and octave**. These have one "normal" size, called **perfect**.
- The **major/minor** family: **2nd, 3rd, 6th and 7th**. These have two normal sizes, **major** and **minor**, a semitone apart.

Why "perfect"? Because these intervals sound especially pure and stable, and they are the same in major and minor keys. Their vibrations fit together in very simple ratios (2 : 1 for the octave, 3 : 2 for the 5th, 4 : 3 for the 4th). Since ancient Greece they were considered the most consonant, the most "perfect", and the name stuck.

The interval of six semitones, exactly half an octave, is the **tritone** (three whole tones). It is the most unstable interval of all, and in older music was nicknamed "the devil in music". It is either an **augmented 4th** (C–F♯) or a **diminished 5th** (C–G♭), depending on spelling.

## The major-scale test

Counting semitones always works, but there is a faster method that musicians use, based on the major scale:

> **From the lower note, imagine its major scale.** If the upper note is in that scale, the interval is **major** (2nd, 3rd, 6th, 7th) or **perfect** (unison, 4th, 5th, octave). If the upper note is a semitone lower than the scale note, a major interval becomes **minor**.

From D, the major scale is D E F♯ G A B C♯ D. So D–F♯ is a major 3rd, D–A a perfect 5th, D–B a major 6th, D–C♯ a major 7th. D–F (a semitone below F♯) is a minor 3rd, D–C a minor 7th.

## Augmented and diminished

Any interval can be stretched or squeezed further. An interval a semitone **larger** than major or perfect is **augmented**; a semitone **smaller** than minor or perfect is **diminished**. You met one already: the augmented 2nd in the harmonic minor (F–G♯, three semitones but spelled as a 2nd). These intervals sound like their neighbours on the keyboard but are spelled differently, and their spelling tells you what job they do. A later chapter is devoted to them.

## Inversion pairs: a shortcut

Turn an interval upside down, by moving the lower note up an octave, and you get its **inversion**. C up to E is a 3rd; E up to C is a 6th. The pairs always follow two simple rules:

- The numbers **add up to 9**: 2nd ↔ 7th, 3rd ↔ 6th, 4th ↔ 5th, unison ↔ octave.
- The qualities **swap**: major ↔ minor, augmented ↔ diminished, and **perfect stays perfect**.

| Interval | Inverts to |
| --- | --- |
| major 3rd (C–E) | minor 6th (E–C) |
| minor 3rd (A–C) | major 6th (C–A) |
| perfect 4th (G–C) | perfect 5th (C–G) |
| major 2nd (C–D) | minor 7th (D–C) |

This is a great time-saver for large intervals. To name C up to B♭, think of the inversion: B♭ up to C is a major 2nd, so C up to B♭ is a minor 7th.

## Consonance and dissonance

Intervals have different degrees of tension:

- **Perfect consonances** (unison, octave, 5th) sound pure and open, almost empty.
- **Imperfect consonances** (3rds and 6ths) sound sweet and full. They are the backbone of harmony: so many duets move in parallel 3rds or 6ths.
- **Dissonances** (2nds, 7ths, the tritone) sound tense and want to move to a consonance.

The 4th is a curious case: on its own it sounds consonant, but above the bass in harmony it was treated as a dissonance in classical style, which is why the second inversion chord (with a 4th above the bass) is used carefully. Dissonance is not bad. It is the energy of music; resolving it is what makes music feel like it is going somewhere.

## Hearing intervals

Song hooks help: a perfect 4th starts *Here Comes the Bride*; a perfect 5th, *Twinkle Twinkle* (first "twinkle" to second); a major 6th, *My Bonnie*; a minor 6th, the *Love Story* theme; a minor 7th, *Somewhere* from West Side Story ("There's a place"); a major 7th sounds like a near-octave that misses, bright and aching; the tritone, *Maria* ("Ma-ri"). The ear branch trains these in stages, starting with 3rds against 5ths.

## Common confusions

- **"Major 5th".** 4ths, 5ths and octaves are perfect, not major.
- **The tritone has two names.** Augmented 4th or diminished 5th, by spelling.
- **Inversion pairs add up to 8.** They add up to **9**, because the shared note is counted twice.

## In short

Unisons, 4ths, 5ths and octaves are perfect; 2nds, 3rds, 6ths and 7ths are major or minor. Count semitones, or use the major-scale test: in the lower note's major scale means major or perfect, a semitone lower means minor. Augmented is a semitone wider, diminished a semitone narrower. Inverted intervals add up to 9 and swap major and minor. 3rds and 6ths are sweet, 5ths and octaves pure, 2nds, 7ths and the tritone tense.
