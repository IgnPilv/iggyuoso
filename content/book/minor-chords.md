## Two kinds of 3rd, two kinds of chord

You know that a triad is three notes stacked in 3rds: root, third and fifth. And you know that 3rds come in two sizes: major (four semitones) and minor (three). Put those two facts together and the difference between a major and a minor chord becomes simple:

- A **major triad** has a **major 3rd** at the bottom (root to third) and a minor 3rd on top. C E G: C–E is 4 semitones, E–G is 3.
- A **minor triad** has a **minor 3rd** at the bottom and a major 3rd on top. A C E: A–C is 3 semitones, C–E is 4.

In both, root to fifth is the same: **seven semitones**, a perfect 5th. The only difference is where the middle note sits. Lower the third of a major chord by a semitone and it becomes minor: C E G becomes C E♭ G.

> **Major triad**: major 3rd + minor 3rd (4 + 3 semitones). **Minor triad**: minor 3rd + major 3rd (3 + 4). Both have a perfect 5th (7 semitones) from root to top. The third decides the colour.

## The triads on white keys

Build a triad on every white key, using only white keys, and you get a mix:

| Root | Notes | Bottom 3rd | Quality |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| C | C E G | major | **major** |
| D | D F A | minor | **minor** |
| E | E G B | minor | **minor** |
| F | F A C | major | **major** |
| G | G B D | major | **major** |
| A | A C E | minor | **minor** |
| B | B D F | minor | **diminished** (see below) |

The triad on B is special. Both its 3rds are minor (B–D is 3 semitones, D–F is 3), so from root to fifth is only **six** semitones, not seven. This squeezed chord is called **diminished**, and it sounds tense and unstable. It has its own chapter later; for now just notice it is the odd one out.

## Writing minor chords

In chord symbols, a minor chord adds a small **m** to the root: **Am**, **Dm**, **Em**. (You may also see "min" or a minus sign: Amin, A–.) A plain letter means major. In Roman numerals, minor chords use **lower-case** numerals: in C major, the chord on D is **ii**, on E **iii**, on A **vi**. Major chords stay upper case: **I, IV, V**. The diminished chord on B is written **vii°**, with a little circle.

So the complete set of triads in a major key, from 1 to 7, is: **I ii iii IV V vi vii°**. That pattern is the same in every major key, which is why musicians can say "it goes I, vi, IV, V" and everyone knows the sound.

## Spelling chords correctly

A triad is always spelled with **every other letter**: root, skip one, third, skip one, fifth. D minor is D F A; E major is E G♯ B (not E A♭ B, because A♭ would break the every-other-letter rule). To build any major or minor chord:

1. Write the three letters: root, third, fifth (e.g., E G B).
2. Adjust the fifth to be **7 semitones** above the root (here B is already right).
3. Adjust the third: **4 semitones** for major (G♯), **3** for minor (G).

## Hearing major and minor

Major chords sound bright, open and settled; minor chords sound darker, softer, more inward. Most people can hear the difference immediately when the two are played side by side, even if they cannot yet name a single note. The ear branch starts with exactly that comparison, then asks you to name single chords without a reference.

A tip: listen to the **middle** of the chord. The root and fifth are the same in both; the colour lives entirely in the third. Some people find it helps to sing the three notes up from the root: the minor third "sags" compared with the major.

## Minor chords at the keyboard

In a five-finger position, a minor triad is the same shape as a major one, fingers 1-3-5 (right hand) or 5-3-1 (left hand); only the middle finger moves a semitone. On white keys, Dm, Em and Am fall straight under the hand. Practise switching between C and Cm, G and Gm, D and Dm by moving only the middle finger, and listen to the colour change.

## Common confusions

- **Minor chord means a chord with black keys.** Am, Dm and Em are all white.
- **"m" vs "M".** A small m is minor; "M" or "maj" usually appears only with 7th chords (Cmaj7).
- **Changing the root or fifth.** To go from major to minor, move only the third.

## In short

Major triads stack a major 3rd then a minor 3rd; minor triads stack a minor 3rd then a major 3rd. Both span a perfect 5th, and the third decides the colour. In a major key, the triads run I ii iii IV V vi vii°. Minor chords take a small m (Am) and lower-case numerals (vi). Spell chords with every other letter, and listen to the middle note to hear major or minor.
