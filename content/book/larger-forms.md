## Building something bigger

A short piece can be a single idea, or two ideas in binary or ternary form. But how does a composer hold a listener's attention for five, ten or twenty minutes? The answer is **form**: a plan that balances **repetition** (so the listener has something to hold on to) with **contrast** (so they don't get bored), and **home** with **away**. Three larger forms have served composers for centuries, and you will meet all of them as soon as you start playing sonatinas and sonatas: rondo, theme and variations, and sonata form.

## Rondo: always coming home

In a **rondo**, a main theme, the **refrain** (A), keeps returning, with contrasting sections called **episodes** in between:

> **A B A C A** (five-part rondo), or **A B A C A B A** (seven-part rondo).

The refrain is always in the home key and usually recognisable, catchy, often cheerful. The episodes visit other keys (the dominant, the relative minor, the subdominant) and bring new material, sometimes a different mood. Each return of A is a homecoming, and the listener enjoys spotting it. Rondos are often fast and light; they are the usual choice for the **last movement** of a Classical sonata or concerto, sending the audience home happy.

Beethoven's *Für Elise* is a rondo: its famous opening theme (A) returns three times, with two contrasting episodes, one gentle in F major and one stormy over a repeated bass note.

| Section | A | B | A | C | A |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *Für Elise* | A minor, the famous theme | F major, lighter | theme | stormy, over a repeated A | theme, to the end |

## Theme and variations

In **theme and variations**, a tune (often simple, sometimes borrowed from a folk song or another composer) is stated first, and then repeated several times, each time **changed** in some way while its outline, phrase lengths and usually its harmony stay recognisable:

> **A A¹ A² A³ A⁴ …**

Ways to vary a theme:

- **Decorate the melody**: add passing notes, neighbour notes, runs and trills.
- **Change the rhythm**: eighths become triplets, sixteenths, dotted rhythms.
- **Change the accompaniment**: chords become an Alberti bass, broken chords, an oom-pah.
- **Move the melody**: put it in the left hand, or in the middle.
- **Change the mode**: a variation in the minor (often slow and expressive) is almost compulsory.
- **Change the meter or tempo**: a slow variation, a march, a waltz version.
- **Change the harmony**: new chords under the old tune.

You have already played a small set of variations in this app (on "Lightly Row"). Mozart's twelve variations on "Ah vous dirai-je, Maman" (the tune we know as "Twinkle Twinkle Little Star") are a delightful model: the same tune is turned into runs, triplets, a minor variation, an adagio and a brilliant finale. Variations are excellent for learning, because the same harmony makes each variation easier once you know the theme, and for composing, because you can apply the techniques to any tune.

## Sonata form

**Sonata form** is the most important form of the Classical and Romantic eras. It is used for the first movements (and often other movements) of nearly every sonata, symphony, string quartet and concerto from Haydn to Brahms. It is a kind of large ternary form with a plot built on **two keys**.

### Exposition

The **exposition** presents the main material:

- **First subject** (first theme) in the **home key**.
- A **transition** (bridge) that modulates.
- **Second subject** (second theme) in a **new key**: the dominant if the piece is in major, the relative major if it is in minor. Often contrasting in character: if the first is bold, the second is lyrical.
- A **codetta** with strong cadences in the new key.

The exposition is usually marked to be repeated.

### Development

In the **development**, the composer takes fragments of the themes and works on them: transposes them through many keys, breaks them into motifs, combines them, builds tension. This is the most unstable, adventurous part. It ends with a long passage on the dominant of the home key (a **retransition**), building anticipation for the return.

### Recapitulation

The **recapitulation** brings back the exposition's material, but now **both subjects stay in the home key**. The conflict between the two keys is resolved. The transition is rewritten so that it doesn't modulate. A **coda** may round the movement off.

| Section | Exposition | | Development | Recapitulation | | (Coda) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Material | 1st subject | 2nd subject | fragments, worked out | 1st subject | 2nd subject | ending |
| Key (major piece) | I | V | many keys, ends on V | I | I | I |

Think of it as a story: two characters introduced in two different places (exposition), adventures and conflict (development), and both brought home together (recapitulation). That's why sonatas can last so long without losing their thread.

A **sonatina** ("little sonata") is a shorter, simpler sonata, usually with a short development or none at all. Clementi's, Kuhlau's and Diabelli's sonatinas are standard pieces for pianists at your level, and you can now follow their form.

## Listening for form

When you listen to a larger piece, ask: Has this tune come before? Are we home, or away? Is this new material or a working-out of old material? Is the music stable (themes, cadences) or unstable (modulating, fragmenting)? You don't need to label every bar; just following the big returns makes a long piece much easier to understand and enjoy, and to play.

## Common confusions

- **"Sonata" and "sonata form" are the same.** A sonata is a whole work (usually three or four movements); sonata form is the plan of one movement, usually the first.
- **The development is just a middle section.** It is where the themes are worked on and keys keep changing.
- **Variations change the tune completely.** The outline and usually the harmony stay recognisable.

## In short

Larger forms balance repetition and contrast, home and away. A rondo (A B A C A) keeps returning to its refrain. Theme and variations repeats a tune with ornaments, new rhythms, new accompaniments, minor mode or new harmony. Sonata form has an exposition with two subjects in two keys, a development that works on them through many keys, and a recapitulation that brings both home. Sonatinas are short sonatas, ideal pieces at this stage.
