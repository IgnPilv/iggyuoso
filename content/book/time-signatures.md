## Beats come in groups

Tap a steady beat and say "one, two, one, two". Now try "one, two, three, one, two, three". Something changes, even though the beats themselves are just as even. The first beat of each group feels heavier, like a foot coming down. Our ears love to hear beats in groups, and nearly all music is organised that way: in twos (a march), in threes (a waltz) or in fours (most pop songs).

The first beat of each group is the **strong beat**, called the **downbeat**. The others are weaker. This pattern of strong and weak is called the **meter**.

## Bars and bar lines

In written music each group of beats is fenced off by a vertical line called a **bar line**. The space between two bar lines is a **bar** (American books say **measure**). At the very end of a piece there is a **double bar line**, a thin line and a thick one, which means "the end".

Bars make music much easier to read. Instead of a long string of notes, you see small, equal boxes, each holding the same number of beats. If you lose your place you can find the next bar line and pick up from its strong first beat.

## The time signature

How many beats go in each bar? That is shown once, at the very start of the music, by the **time signature**: two numbers stacked on top of each other, written just after the clef and key signature.

- The **top number** says **how many beats** are in each bar.
- The **bottom number** says **what kind of note gets one beat**: 4 means a quarter note, 2 a half note, 8 an eighth note.

So **4/4** means four quarter-note beats in every bar. It is the most common time signature of all, so common that it is often written as a large **C** (for "common time").

```figure
staff: { notes: "C5:q D5:q E5:q F5:q | G5:h G5:h | A5:q A5:q G5:h", meter: 4/4 }
sound: "C5:q D5:q E5:q F5:q G5:h G5:h A5:q A5:q G5:h"
caption: 4/4. Four quarter beats in each bar. Count "ONE two three four".
```

> **Top number: how many beats in a bar. Bottom number: which note gets the beat** (4 = quarter, 2 = half, 8 = eighth). 4/4 is "common time".

## The three everyday meters

| Time signature | Count | Feels like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2/4 | **ONE** two | a march, left-right |
| 3/4 | **ONE** two three | a waltz, a lullaby swaying |
| 4/4 | **ONE** two **three** four | most pop, rock, hymns and folk songs |

In 4/4 the third beat is a little stronger than beats 2 and 4, but not as strong as beat 1. That is why 4/4 feels like two strong-weak pairs rather than one long group.

```figure
staff: { notes: "G4:q E4:q | G4:q E4:q | F4:q D4:q | C4:h", meter: 2/4 }
sound: "G4:q E4:q G4:q E4:q F4:q D4:q C4:h"
caption: 2/4. Two beats a bar, like marching feet.
```

```figure
staff: { notes: "C4:h E4:q | G4:h E4:q | F4:q E4:q D4:q | C4:h.", meter: 3/4 }
sound: "C4:h E4:q G4:h E4:q F4:q E4:q D4:q C4:h."
caption: 3/4. Three beats a bar, the strong ONE and two lighter beats: a waltz.
```

## The dotted half note

In 3/4 time there is a note you have not met yet. A whole note lasts four beats, which would overflow a three-beat bar. To fill a whole bar of 3/4 with one sound, music uses a **dotted half note**: a half note with a small dot after it. The dot adds half of the note's own length: a half note is 2 beats, half of that is 1, so the dotted half note lasts **3 beats**. You will meet the dot again with other notes in the next chapter.

```figure
staff: { notes: "E4:h. | D4:h. | C4:h.", meter: 3/4, labels: names }
caption: Dotted half notes, each filling a bar of 3/4.
check:
  ask: "How many quarter-note beats fit in one bar of 3/4?"
  choices: ["3", "4", "2"]
  correct: 0
  why: "The top number says **three** beats; the bottom number 4 says each beat is a quarter note."
```

## Every bar must add up

A rule that always holds: the notes and rests in each bar must add up to exactly the number of beats the time signature asks for. In 4/4, a half note plus two quarter notes (2 + 1 + 1 = 4) makes a full bar. A half note and three quarters (5 beats) would not fit. Checking this is a great way to catch reading mistakes: if your count does not come to four, you have misread something. The next chapter is all about this kind of counting.

## Feeling the meter when you play

Knowing the meter changes how you play. The first beat of each bar deserves a tiny bit more weight; it is the anchor everything else hangs from. In a waltz, the left hand often plays a low note on beat 1 and lighter chords on 2 and 3: **OOM**-pah-pah. In a march, LEFT-right. You do not have to force accents; just know where "one" is and the music starts to swing.

When you tap along to a recording, try to find "one". Count along with the beats and listen for where the pattern seems to start again, often where the chords change or the drums hit hardest.

## Common confusions

- **The bottom number is not a number of beats.** It names the type of note that counts as a beat.
- **4/4 and 2/4 are not the same thing doubled.** They feel different: 2/4 has a strong beat every two beats, 4/4 every four (with a medium one in the middle).
- **A bar line is not a pause.** The beat goes straight on across it.

## In short

Beats group into twos, threes or fours, with a strong first beat. Bar lines fence off each group into a bar. The time signature's top number gives the beats per bar, its bottom number the note that gets one beat. 2/4 marches, 3/4 waltzes, 4/4 is common time. The dotted half note fills a bar of 3/4, and every bar must add up to its time signature.
