## Melody and harmony meet

When you harmonised tunes with I, IV and V, you chose the chord that contained most of the melody notes in a bar. But you will have noticed that some melody notes did not belong to the chord: a C major bar with the melody C D E, for example. The D is not in the chord C E G. Is it a mistake? Not at all. It is a **non-chord tone** (also called a non-harmonic note), and melodies are full of them. They make a tune flow; without them, melodies would be nothing but broken chords.

Non-chord tones are classified by **how they arrive and how they leave**. Learning the main types will help you analyse pieces, choose chords for tunes, write your own melodies and hear why some notes feel tense and others relaxed.

## Passing notes

A **passing note** fills the gap between two chord notes a 3rd apart, moving by step in one direction. Over C major, the melody C – **D** – E passes through D on its way from C to E. Passing notes usually fall on weak beats or weak halves of beats, so they sound light and natural.

```figure

sound: "C3+E3+G3+C5:q D5:q C3+E3+G3+E5:q F5:q C3+E3+G3+G5:h C3+E3+G3+E5:h"
caption: Over a C chord, D and F are passing notes: each fills a gap between chord notes (C to E, E to G).
```

A passing note can also be **chromatic** (C – C♯ – D), and two passing notes can fill a 4th (C – D – E – F, over a chord of C and then F).

## Neighbour notes

A **neighbour note** (or auxiliary note) steps away from a chord note and comes straight back to it: E – **F** – E (upper neighbour) or E – **D** – E (lower neighbour). It decorates a single note, like a little shake. Neighbour notes are also usually weak, and a quick upper-and-back is the seed of the **trill** and the **mordent**.

```figure
staff: { notes: "E5:q F5:q E5:q D5:q | E5:w", labels: names }
sound: "C3+E3+G3+E5:q F5:q C3+E3+G3+E5:q D5:q C3+E3+G3+E5:w"
caption: Over C major, F is an upper neighbour of E, D a lower neighbour.
```

## Suspensions

A **suspension** is a chord note that is **held over** while the harmony changes under it, making a dissonance with the new chord, and then **falls by step** to a note of the new chord. It has three stages: the **preparation** (the note is a chord note), the **suspension** (it is held while the chord changes, now a dissonance, on a strong beat) and the **resolution** (it steps down). The common types are named by the intervals with the bass: 4–3, 7–6, 9–8.

```figure
staff: { notes: "F2+C3+A3+F4:h C3+G3+C4+F4:h | C3+G3+C4+E4:w", clef: grand }
sound: "F2+C3+A3+F4:h C3+G3+C4+F4:h C3+G3+C4+E4:w"
caption: A 4–3 suspension. Preparation, F belongs to the F chord. Suspension, the bass moves to C and F is held, a 4th above it. Resolution, F falls to E, the 3rd.
```

Suspensions give music a feeling of yearning and longing: the held note seems to resist, and the resolution is a sigh of relief. They are everywhere in Baroque music and in slow, expressive pieces. In pop and jazz, the "sus" chords (Csus4 = C F G, Csus2 = C D G) are suspensions that sometimes resolve and sometimes simply float.

## Appoggiaturas

An **appoggiatura** (Italian *appoggiare*, "to lean") is a dissonant note that arrives **by leap** onto a **strong beat** and then resolves **by step**. Unlike a suspension it is not prepared; it simply leans on the chord and then sinks into it. Appoggiaturas are deeply expressive: the "sigh" figure (a falling two-note slur, strong–weak) is often one. Many of the most emotional moments in music are appoggiaturas.

```figure
staff: { notes: "C5:q A5:q G5:h", labels: names }
sound: "C3+E3+G3+C5:q C3+E3+G3+A5:q G5:h"
caption: Over a C chord, A leaps in on the beat and sighs down to G: an appoggiatura.
```

## Other kinds, briefly

- **Escape note** (échappée): steps away from a chord note and leaps back in the other direction.
- **Anticipation**: arrives early, sounding a note of the next chord before the chord changes (very common at cadences and in pop and jazz melodies).
- **Pedal point**: a long note, usually in the bass, held while the harmony changes above it, sometimes clashing with it. A tonic or dominant pedal at the start or end of a piece creates a strong sense of home or of waiting.

> **Passing note**: by step between two chord notes, one direction. **Neighbour**: step away and back. **Suspension**: held over a chord change, then down by step. **Appoggiatura**: leaps onto a strong beat, then steps. **Anticipation**: arrives before its chord.

## Using non-chord tones

### When choosing chords

When you harmonise a melody, don't let every melody note dictate the chord. Look at the notes on the strong beats and the notes held longest: they usually belong to the chord. The quick notes in between, moving by step, are often passing and neighbour notes. This simple idea will make your harmonies much smoother.

### When playing

Non-chord tones on strong beats (suspensions, appoggiaturas) carry tension. Lean into them slightly, and let the resolution be softer: strong–weak, tension–release. This is one of the oldest secrets of expressive phrasing. Passing and neighbour notes on weak beats should flow lightly.

### When composing

Start with a melody of chord notes, then add passing notes to fill the leaps, neighbour notes to decorate long notes, and an appoggiatura at the climax. You will hear a plain tune turn into a real melody.

## Common confusions

- **A note outside the chord is wrong.** Most melodies are full of them; what matters is how they move.
- **Every melody note decides the chord.** Strong-beat and long notes usually do; quick stepwise notes are often decoration.
- **Suspensions resolve upward.** They normally fall by step.

## In short

Melody notes that are not in the chord are non-chord tones. Passing notes fill gaps by step, neighbour notes step away and back, suspensions are held over a chord change and resolve down, appoggiaturas leap onto strong beats and resolve by step, anticipations come early. Strong-beat dissonances carry expression; weak-beat ones give flow. Knowing them makes harmonising, phrasing and composing easier.
