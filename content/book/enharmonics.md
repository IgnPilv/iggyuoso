## The puzzle

In the chapter on sharps and flats you saw that every black key has two names. The key between F and G is F♯ and also G♭. They are the same key, the same string inside the piano, the same sound. Two names for the same sound are called **enharmonic** names (from Greek, roughly "in tune with each other"), and F♯ and G♭ are **enharmonic equivalents**.

White keys can have extra names too. E♯ is the same key as F, B♯ is C, F♭ is E, C♭ is B. And with **double sharps** (written 𝄪, raising a note by two semitones) and **double flats** (𝄫, lowering it by two), even more spellings are possible: F𝄪 is the same key as G.

So why does music not simply pick one name per key and keep it simple? It is a fair question, and the answer reveals something deep about how music is written.

## A name says how a note is used

Think about the words "there" and "their". They sound exactly alike, but you cannot swap them in writing; the spelling tells you what the word *does* in the sentence. Note names work the same way. The spelling tells a reader where a note belongs in the key, and what it is likely to do next.

Here is the rule that settles most cases:

> **A scale uses each letter once.** Each of the seven notes of a major or minor scale has its own letter name, A to G, with no letter repeated and none left out. Choose sharps or flats so that this works.

Take G major. Its notes are G A B C D E and then a note a semitone below G. That note could be called F♯ or G♭. But G is already taken (it is the first note), and F has not been used yet. So it must be **F♯**. Written as G♭, the scale would have two kinds of G and no F at all: G A B C D E G♭ G. That would be confusing to read and misleading about how the note behaves.

Now take F major. After F G A comes a note a semitone above A. It could be A♯ or B♭. A is taken; B is free. So it is **B♭**.

## Hearing the difference in context

On the piano, F♯ and G♭ sound identical. But they *behave* differently in music, and a reader's eye uses the spelling to predict what comes next:

- **F♯** usually leans **up** towards G, the way a leading note leans up to its home.
- **G♭** usually leans **down** towards F.

```figure

sound: "F#4:h G4:h Gb4:h F4:h"
caption: The same key twice: as F sharp it rises to G, as G flat it falls to F.
```

The same idea applies to intervals and chords. From C up to E♭ is a 3rd (C, D, E: three letters), a minor 3rd. From C up to D♯ is the same distance on the keyboard but it is a 2nd (C, D: two letters), an augmented 2nd. They sound alike alone, but in music they do different jobs, and the spelling tells you which job. You will see this clearly when you learn interval qualities and chord spelling.

## Whole keys with two names

A few keys can be spelled entirely two ways. F♯ major (six sharps) and G♭ major (six flats) sound exactly the same on the piano. So do C♯ major (seven sharps) and D♭ major (five flats), and B major (five sharps) and C♭ major (seven flats). Composers usually choose the spelling with fewer sharps or flats, or the one that fits the keys around it. You will meet these on the circle of fifths much later.

## Why not just tune them differently?

On some instruments, enharmonic notes really are slightly different. A string player or singer will often make an F♯ leading up to G a hair higher than a G♭ falling to F. On the piano that cannot happen: the keyboard is tuned in **equal temperament**, where all twelve semitones are exactly the same size. That tuning was a compromise that took over in the 1800s. It makes every key equally usable on one instrument, at the cost of making enharmonic notes identical. The two names survive because the music still needs them to make sense on the page.

## Choosing the right name: a checklist

1. **In a key, use the key's spelling.** In G major it is F♯; in F major, B♭; in D♭ major, G♭.
2. **In a scale, use each letter once.**
3. **Going up chromatically, sharps are common; going down, flats are common.** C C♯ D D♯ E going up; E E♭ D D♭ C going down.
4. **In a chord, keep the stacked-3rds spelling.** A D major chord is D F♯ A (every other letter), never D G♭ A.

## Common confusions

- **"Enharmonic" means a different sound.** No: same sound, different name.
- **Any name will do.** It will sound right but read wrong. Spell notes so each letter appears once in the scale.
- **E♯ and B♯ are mistakes.** They are real notes, needed in keys like F♯ major and C♯ minor.

## In short

Enharmonic notes are one key with two names, like F♯ and G♭. The name shows how the note is used: a scale uses each letter once, so the key decides the spelling. F♯ tends to rise, G♭ to fall. Pianos make enharmonic notes identical through equal temperament, but the names still tell the reader what the music is doing.
