## The weave of music

The word **texture** comes from weaving: the way threads are combined to make cloth. In music it means the way the **lines**, the separate strands of melody and accompaniment, are put together at any moment. Is there one tune alone? A tune with chords underneath? Several tunes at once, each as important as the others? The answer changes how a piece sounds, how it should be played, and how you should listen to it.

Musicians describe texture with a few words, mostly from Greek.

## Monophony: one line

**Monophony** ("one sound") is a single melody with no harmony: one person singing, or a group singing the same tune in unison or octaves. Medieval chant, many folk songs sung alone, and a tune you hum are monophonic. On the piano, monophony is one hand alone, or both hands playing the same tune an octave apart.

## Homophony: tune and accompaniment

**Homophony** ("same sound") is the texture you know best: **one main melody with chords supporting it**. The tune is the star; the accompaniment is the backing. Almost all pop songs, hymns, and much classical piano music are homophonic. When you play a tune in the right hand over chords or an Alberti bass in the left, you are playing homophony.

There are two flavours. In **chordal homophony** all the parts move together in the same rhythm, like a hymn sung in four parts. In **melody and accompaniment**, the tune moves freely over a pattern, as in most piano pieces and songs.

```figure
staff: { notes: "E4+C3+G3:q D4:q C4+E3+G3:h | D4+G2+G3:q E4:q C4+C3+G3:h", clef: grand }
sound: "E4+C3+G3:q D4:q C4+E3+G3:h D4+G2+G3:q E4:q C4+C3+G3:h"
caption: Homophony: a melody in the right hand, supported by chords in the left.
```

**Balance** is everything in homophony. The melody must sing out clearly above the accompaniment. On the piano that means playing the accompanying hand a dynamic level softer, and often bringing out the top note of the right hand above the other notes in the same hand. Many pianists practise this by playing the accompaniment as quietly as they can while the tune stays full.

## Polyphony: several melodies at once

**Polyphony** ("many sounds"), also called **counterpoint**, is two or more **independent melodies** sounding together, each with its own shape and rhythm, each important. No line is merely backing. The great master of polyphony is J. S. Bach. In a Bach minuet or invention, the left hand is not "accompaniment"; it is a second melody that answers, imitates and weaves around the right hand.

A simple kind of polyphony you already know is the **round** (or canon): *Frère Jacques* sung by several voices, each starting a few bars after the last. The same tune, overlapping with itself, creates harmony.

```figure
staff: { notes: "C4:q D4:q E4:q C4:q | C4+E4:q D4+F4:q E4+G4:q C4+G4:q", clef: treble }
sound: "C4:q D4:q E4:q C4:q C4+E4:q D4+F4:q E4+G4:q C4+G4:q"
caption: Frère Jacques as a round: when a second voice enters, two melodies sound at once. That is polyphony.
```

Playing polyphony asks something different of you: **each hand must sound like a singer** with its own phrasing. Practise each line alone until you can sing it, then put them together listening to both. A good test is whether a listener could follow either line.

## Heterophony and other textures

A rarer word, **heterophony**, describes several performers playing the same tune at once with small individual variations and ornaments, common in folk music from many cultures. And of course, music can mix textures: a piece may begin with a single line, add chords, then break into counterpoint. Composers often use changes of texture for contrast, just as they use changes of key or dynamics.

## Thick and thin

Musicians also describe texture as **thick** (many notes and lines, dense) or **thin** (few notes, spacious), and as **high** or **low**. A big chord in both hands in the low register is thick and dark; a single high line is thin and bright. These choices affect the mood as much as the notes themselves.

## Why texture matters at the piano

The piano can play every texture with one person, which is part of its magic, and part of its difficulty. Before learning a piece, ask: is this homophony (find the tune and make it sing, keep the rest softer), or polyphony (give every line its own life)? Many beginners play everything at one level, so the melody gets buried. Knowing the texture tells you what to bring out.

## Common confusions

- **Polyphony means many notes.** It means many independent melodies. A thick chord progression with one tune is homophony.
- **Homophony means boring accompaniment.** The accompaniment can be rich; it is still supporting one main tune.
- **The left hand is always accompaniment.** In polyphony it is a melody too.

## In short

Texture is how musical lines are woven together. Monophony is a single line; homophony is one melody with chordal support (balance the melody above it); polyphony is several independent melodies at once, like Bach's counterpoint or a round. Textures can be thick or thin, and change within a piece. Knowing the texture tells you what to bring out.
