## The missing pull

In the chapter on the major scale you heard how strongly the 7th degree, the **leading note**, pulls up to the tonic, because it sits only a semitone below. Now play A natural minor and listen to the top: G to A. That is a whole **tone**. The pull is much weaker. The scale seems to drift home rather than arrive.

```figure
sound: "A3:q B3:q C4:q D4:q E4:q F4:q G4:h A4:w"
caption: A natural minor. The G does not lean on A the way B leans on C in C major.
```

Composers in the 1600s and 1700s wanted minor keys to come home as firmly as major ones, especially at cadences, the musical full stops. Their solution was simple: **raise the 7th degree by a semitone**. In A minor, G becomes G♯, and now it sits right under A and pulls hard.

## The harmonic minor

The natural minor scale with its 7th raised is called the **harmonic minor**. It is called "harmonic" because the raised 7th is needed for the harmony: it turns the chord on the 5th degree, E G B, into E G♯ B, a **major** chord, which leads home to A minor as strongly as G major leads to C. You will study this V–i move in the chapter on cadences.

```figure
staff: { notes: "A3:q B3:q C4:q D4:q E4:q F4:q G#4:q A4:q", labels: names }
keys: { notes: "A3 B3 C4 D4 E4 F4 G#4 A4", labels: names }
sound: "A3:q B3:q C4:q D4:q E4:q F4:q G#4:q A4:h"
caption: A harmonic minor: the natural minor with G raised to G sharp.
```

Listen to the step from F to G♯. It is bigger than any step in the major scale: **three semitones**, a tone and a half. This interval is called an **augmented 2nd** (F to G♯ is two letters, F and G, so it is a 2nd, but stretched beyond major). It gives the harmonic minor its famous exotic, Middle-Eastern-sounding colour, used in everything from Bach to flamenco to film scores for deserts.

The pattern of the harmonic minor is: **T S T T S (T+S) S**.

> **Harmonic minor** = natural minor with the **7th raised**. It gains a leading note and a strong pull home, and an augmented 2nd between degrees 6 and 7.

## An important detail: key signatures

The raised 7th is **never** put in the key signature. A minor's key signature stays empty, and every G♯ is written as an accidental, in the music itself. That is why spotting accidentals on the 7th degree is such a reliable clue that a piece is in a minor key, as you saw in the last chapter. E minor has one sharp (F♯) in its signature and writes its D♯s in the music; D minor has one flat (B♭) and writes its C♯s.

## The melodic minor

Singers and melody writers found the augmented 2nd awkward. It is a wide, unusual leap in the middle of what should be smooth stepwise motion. So, when a melody climbs up to the tonic, they raised the **6th** degree too, to close the gap. In A minor, F becomes F♯ as well as G becoming G♯. Going up, that gives A B C D E **F♯ G♯** A: smooth whole steps leading to the leading note.

Coming down, there is no need to pull up to the tonic, so the traditional melodic minor **returns to the natural minor** on the way down: A G F E D C B A, with G and F natural again. The descending line sounds properly minor and gently sad.

```figure
staff: { notes: "A3:q B3:q C4:q D4:q E4:q F#4:q G#4:q A4:q | G4:q F4:q E4:q D4:q C4:q B3:q A3:h", labels: names }
sound: "A3:q B3:q C4:q D4:q E4:q F#4:q G#4:q A4:q G4:q F4:q E4:q D4:q C4:q B3:q A3:h"
caption: A melodic minor: 6th and 7th raised going up, natural minor coming down.
```

> **Melodic minor**: going **up**, raise the **6th and 7th**; coming **down**, play the **natural minor**.

Notice that the bottom half of every minor scale is the same: A B C D E. Only the top half changes:

| Version | Degrees 6 and 7 in A minor |
| --- | --- |
| Natural | F, G |
| Harmonic | F, G♯ |
| Melodic going up | F♯, G♯ |
| Melodic coming down | G, F (natural again) |

(In jazz, a "melodic minor" usually means the raised version both up and down. In classical theory exams, and in this app, the melodic minor changes direction as described above.)

## Three scales, one key

It is important to understand that these are not three different keys. They are three versions of the same **minor key**, and real music moves freely between them, raising the 6th and 7th when the line climbs to the tonic or when the harmony needs a major V chord, and leaving them natural at other times. Learning the three scales is learning the three most common ways a minor key behaves.

```figure
check:
  ask: "Which notes are raised in D harmonic minor? (D minor has one flat, B♭.)"
  choices: ["Only C, to C♯", "B and C, to B♮ and C♯", "Only B, to B♮"]
  correct: 0
  why: "Harmonic minor raises only the **7th** degree. The 7th of D minor is C, so it becomes **C♯**. B♭ stays. (The melodic minor going up would also raise B♭ to B♮.)"
```

## Common confusions

- **Putting G♯ in the A minor key signature.** Never: raised 6ths and 7ths are always accidentals.
- **Raising the 6th in the harmonic minor.** Only the 7th. The harmonic minor keeps its augmented 2nd.
- **Using the raised notes coming down in the melodic minor.** Classical melodic minor descends as natural minor.

## In short

The natural minor has no leading note. The harmonic minor raises the 7th, giving a strong pull home and a major V chord, plus an exotic augmented 2nd between degrees 6 and 7. The melodic minor raises the 6th and 7th going up and returns to natural minor coming down. All three are one minor key; the raised notes are always written as accidentals.
