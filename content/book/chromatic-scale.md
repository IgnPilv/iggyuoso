## All twelve keys

Start on any key and play every key in turn, black and white, up to the next octave. Every step is a **semitone**, and there are twelve before you reach the starting note again. This is the **chromatic scale**, from the Greek *chroma*, "colour". It contains every note our music uses.

Unlike the major and minor scales, the chromatic scale has no tonic, no pattern of tones and semitones, no sense of key. All its steps are the same, so it sounds like a slide rather than a staircase: no step sounds more like "home" than another. That is why it is used for colour, for slides and runs, rather than as a key of its own.

## Spelling the chromatic scale

Since every black key has two names, how should a chromatic scale be written? The usual convention:

> **Going up, use sharps; coming down, use flats.** C C♯ D D♯ E F F♯ G G♯ A A♯ B C; C B B♭ A A♭ G G♭ F E E♭ D D♭ C.

This keeps each note leaning in the direction of travel: a sharp leans up to the note above, a flat down to the note below. It also means each letter appears at most twice, which keeps it readable. (A stricter "harmonic" spelling exists in theory exams, where each letter is used twice except two, but the simple sharps-up, flats-down rule is the one you will see most.)

## Fingering the chromatic scale

The chromatic scale has its own simple fingering, the same in every starting place: **use 3 on every black key**, and **1 on every white key**, except where two white keys sit next to each other (E–F and B–C), where you use **1–2** (right hand going up: E with 1, F with 2). The hand stays compact and moves smoothly along the keyboard, with the thumb tucking under the 3rd finger constantly. It is a wonderful exercise for an even, relaxed thumb.

## Chromatic notes in music

In a key, the seven scale notes are **diatonic**. Any other note, one of the five that do not belong, is **chromatic**, and it is written with an accidental. Chromatic notes appear in music for several reasons:

- **Raised leading notes** in minor keys (G♯ in A minor). You know these already.
- **Chromatic passing notes**: a note filling the gap between two scale notes a tone apart, moving by semitones. In C major, a melody going G – G♯ – A slides chromatically through G♯.
- **Chromatic neighbour notes**: a note a semitone away that decorates a scale note and returns: E – D♯ – E.
- **Borrowed chords and modulation**: notes from another key, brought in for colour or to change key (later chapters).

## Chromatic colour

Chromatic notes add tension, colour and expression. A chromatic slide in the bass sounds dramatic; a chromatic line in a melody can sound yearning or sly. Romantic composers like Chopin, Liszt and Wagner used chromaticism lavishly; film composers use it for suspense (think of the creeping two-note semitone figure of *Jaws*). Even in a simple piece, a single chromatic note at the right moment can be the most memorable thing in it.

At the same time, chromatic notes are what make sight-reading harder, because they break the key's pattern. When you see one, ask: is it a passing note, a neighbour, a raised leading note, or a sign of a new key? The answer tells you how to hear it and where it is going.

## Common confusions

- **The chromatic scale is a key.** It has no tonic; it is a set of all twelve notes.
- **Chromatic means black keys.** In C major, black keys are chromatic; in G♭ major, many white keys are.
- **Any spelling is fine.** Use sharps going up and flats coming down, so each note leans the way it moves.

## In short

The chromatic scale plays all twelve semitones in order; it has no tonic and sounds like a slide. Spell it with sharps going up and flats coming down. Finger it with 3 on black keys and 1 on white keys, 1–2 where two white keys meet. Notes outside the key are chromatic: raised leading notes, passing and neighbour notes, borrowed notes, and signs of a new key. They add colour and tension.
