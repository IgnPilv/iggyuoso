## Twelve keys in a ring

You have noticed a pattern: C major has no sharps; G major, a 5th higher, has one; D major, a 5th above G, has two; A major has three. Each step **up a perfect 5th** adds one sharp. Going the other way, each step **down a 5th** adds one flat: F has one, B♭ two, E♭ three.

Keep going in both directions and something remarkable happens. After twelve steps of a 5th you arrive back where you started (or at its enharmonic twin). The keys form a closed loop: the **circle of fifths**.

| Going clockwise (up a 5th) | Sharps | Going anticlockwise (down a 5th) | Flats |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| C | 0 | C | 0 |
| G | 1 | F | 1 |
| D | 2 | B♭ | 2 |
| A | 3 | E♭ | 3 |
| E | 4 | A♭ | 4 |
| B | 5 | D♭ | 5 |
| F♯ | 6 | G♭ | 6 |
| C♯ | 7 | C♭ | 7 |

At the bottom of the circle the two sides meet: **F♯ major** (six sharps) and **G♭ major** (six flats) are the same keys on the piano, just spelled differently. Similarly B major (five sharps) equals C♭ major (seven flats), and D♭ major (five flats) equals C♯ major (seven sharps). This is enharmonic spelling on the scale of whole keys.

Picture it as a clock face: C at twelve o'clock, G at one, D at two, A at three, E at four, B at five, F♯/G♭ at six, D♭ at seven, A♭ at eight, E♭ at nine, B♭ at ten, F at eleven, and back to C.

> **The circle of fifths**: clockwise, each key is a 5th higher and adds a sharp (C G D A E B F♯); anticlockwise, each is a 5th lower and adds a flat (C F B♭ E♭ A♭ D♭ G♭). They meet at F♯ = G♭.

## Why it works

Remember that a major scale is two identical halves (T T S) joined by a tone. The **upper half** of C major, G A B C, is the **lower half** of G major. G major then needs a new upper half, D E F♯ G, and that is where the new sharp, F♯, comes from: it is the new leading note. Each move up a 5th reuses one half and builds a new one with one more sharp. That is why the keys go up in 5ths, and why each new sharp is a 5th above the last: F♯, C♯, G♯, D♯, A♯, E♯, B♯. The order of sharps you learned is the circle of fifths itself.

## Minor keys on the circle

Every major key has its relative minor, a minor 3rd below, with the same key signature. Write them on an inner ring and you have the minor circle: A minor (0), E minor (1♯), B minor (2♯), F♯ minor (3♯), C♯ minor (4♯), G♯ minor (5♯), D♯/E♭ minor (6); and on the flat side D minor (1♭), G minor (2♭), C minor (3♭), F minor (4♭), B♭ minor (5♭). The minor keys are also a 5th apart from each other.

## Neighbours are relatives

Keys next to each other on the circle differ by only **one note**. C major and G major share six of their seven notes (only F becomes F♯). So neighbouring keys are **closely related**: moving between them sounds smooth and natural. Keys on opposite sides of the circle (C major and F♯ major) share very few notes and sound far apart.

The closely related keys of any key are its two neighbours on the circle and the relative minors of all three. For C major: G major, F major, A minor, E minor and D minor. When pieces change key (modulation, in a later chapter), they most often move to one of these, especially to the **dominant** key (one step clockwise: C to G) or the **relative minor**.

## The circle and chords

The circle is also a map of harmony. A root moving **down a 5th** (anticlockwise) is the strongest chord move in music: V to I is exactly one step anticlockwise. A chain of such moves, like vi – ii – V – I (A D G C), walks steadily anticlockwise towards the tonic. Many jazz standards and Baroque sequences are built on this, so the circle tells you not only where keys are, but where chords want to go.

## How to use it

- **Find a key signature**: count steps from C. Four steps clockwise = E major, four sharps.
- **Find the sharps or flats**: they are the keys you passed, in order: going to E you add F♯ C♯ G♯ D♯.
- **Find the relative minor**: the inner ring, or a minor 3rd down.
- **Find related keys**: look either side.
- **Practise scales**: going round the circle (C, G, D, A...) adds one new black key at a time, a gentle way to learn every key.

## Common confusions

- **The circle has twelve different-sounding keys and fifteen names.** Three keys have two spellings (B/C♭, F♯/G♭, C♯/D♭).
- **Going clockwise means going up the keyboard forever.** Each step is a 5th up, but musicians usually come back down an octave; the circle is about note names, not height.
- **Neighbours a semitone apart are close.** C and C♯ are neighbours on the keyboard but opposite each other on the circle: very distant keys.

## In short

The circle of fifths arranges the twelve keys a perfect 5th apart. Clockwise adds sharps (C G D A E B F♯), anticlockwise adds flats (C F B♭ E♭ A♭ D♭ G♭); they meet at F♯ = G♭. Each step reuses half a scale and adds one new leading note. Relative minors form an inner ring. Neighbouring keys differ by one note and are closely related, and roots falling by 5ths walk the circle towards home.
