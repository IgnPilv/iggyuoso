## A form everyone knows

The **blues** grew out of the songs of African American communities in the southern United States in the late 19th century, from work songs, spirituals and field hollers. By the 1910s it had a standard shape, and since then that shape has fed almost every popular style: jazz, rhythm and blues, rock and roll, soul, funk and rock. If you know the **twelve-bar blues**, you can play with musicians all over the world after a single word: "Blues in F?"

## The twelve-bar pattern

The twelve-bar blues uses just the three primary chords, **I, IV and V**, in a fixed twelve-bar plan, usually all as dominant 7ths. In C: C7, F7, G7.

| Bars 1–4 | I | I | I | I |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Bars 5–8** | **IV** | **IV** | **I** | **I** |
| **Bars 9–12** | **V** | **IV** | **I** | **I** (or V, to go round again) |

Common variations: the "quick change" (IV in bar 2: I – IV – I – I), V in bar 12 as a **turnaround** leading back to the start, and in jazz blues, more chords (ii–V's, secondary dominants). The basic plan stays recognisable.


Notice that I7, IV7 and V7 are all **dominant 7th chords**, even I7, which in classical harmony would sound as if it must move. In the blues these chords are not tensions but colours: they are the sound of the style, and nothing needs to resolve.

## The words fit the form

The twelve bars fall into three four-bar lines, and blues lyrics follow the same **AAB** plan: a line is sung, repeated (over IV, which gives it a new colour), and answered by a third line that rhymes with it. Each sung line usually takes about two bars, leaving two bars for an instrumental answer, a **fill**. That call-and-response is at the heart of the blues: the voice calls, the piano or guitar replies.

## The blues scale

Blues melodies use notes that sit between major and minor. The **minor pentatonic** scale (C E♭ F G B♭) with one added note, the **flat 5th** (G♭, or F♯), gives the **blues scale**:

> **C blues scale: C E♭ F G♭ G B♭ C**

```figure
staff: { notes: "C4:q Eb4:q F4:q Gb4:q G4:q Bb4:q C5:h", labels: names }
sound: "C4:q Eb4:q F4:q Gb4:q G4:q Bb4:q C5:h"
caption: The C blues scale. The flat 3rd, flat 5th and flat 7th are the "blue notes".
```

The **blue notes**, the flat 3rd, flat 5th and flat 7th, clash with the major chords underneath, and that clash is the blues sound. Singers and guitarists bend these notes, sliding between the flat and natural versions. The piano cannot bend a note, so pianists imitate it with a **crushed note**: playing E♭ and sliding immediately to E, or playing both at once briefly. The tension between E♭ in the melody and E in the C7 chord is pure blues.

## Swing and the shuffle

Blues and jazz are usually played with a **swing** feel. Written eighth notes are not played evenly. Instead each pair is played **long – short**, like the first and third notes of a triplet:

> Written: ♫ ♫ ♫ ♫ (even eighths). Played: ♩♪ ♩♪ (the first note of each pair two thirds of the beat, the second one third).

A score shows this with the word **Swing**, or a little sign at the start showing two eighths = a triplet quarter and eighth. When the whole band plays this feel with a heavy, chugging accompaniment, it is called a **shuffle**. Count it "**1** – a – **2** – a – **3** – a – **4** – a" or "doo-BA doo-BA": the notes fall on the beat and on the last third of the beat.

```figure

caption: Written as plain eighths, played swung: each pair long–short, like a triplet quarter and eighth. Listen.
```

Swing is a feel, not a formula: at slow tempos it is closer to the triplet; at very fast tempos it straightens out; and every great player has their own. Listen to recordings and imitate.

## Blues left hands

Classic blues piano patterns for the left hand:

- **Root–fifth "boogie" or shuffle**: C–G, C–A alternating (5th and 6th), two per beat, swung. Move the shape for F and G.
- **Walking bass**: four quarter notes per bar, walking up and down chord and scale notes.
- **Shell chords**: root and 7th (C and B♭), held or played on 2 and 4.

The right hand can then play chords on the off-beats, blues-scale riffs, or improvised melodies.

## Starting to improvise

The blues is the classic starting point for improvisation, because the form is short and repeats, and the C blues scale fits over all three chords. A recipe:

1. Loop the twelve bars with the left hand (or the app's accompaniment).
2. Use only three or four notes of the blues scale at first.
3. Make a short riff (a motif) and **repeat** it, the way blues singers do: AAB, call and response.
4. Leave space. Rests are part of the answer.
5. Aim for chord notes on strong beats: C or E♭ over C7, F or A (or E♭) over F7, G or B (or F) over G7.

## Common confusions

- **The blues is always sad.** It can be joyful, funny, defiant; the name refers to the style, not just a mood.
- **Swing means playing triplets.** It is a long–short feel that only approximates the triplet.
- **The blues scale is the major scale.** It is the minor pentatonic plus the flat 5th, played over major (dominant 7th) chords.

## In short

The twelve-bar blues uses I, IV and V (usually all dominant 7ths) in a fixed plan: I I I I | IV IV I I | V IV I I. Lyrics follow an AAB pattern with instrumental answers. The blues scale (C E♭ F G♭ G B♭) adds the blue notes, which clash expressively with the chords. Swing turns pairs of eighths into long–short. With a shuffle bass and a few blues-scale notes, you can start improvising.
