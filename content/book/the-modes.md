## Seven scales from one set of notes

Play the white keys from C to C and you get the major scale. Play them from A to A and you get the natural minor. What happens if you start on the other white keys? You get five more scales, each with its own pattern of tones and semitones and its own character. These seven scales are called the **modes**. They are much older than major and minor: medieval church music was written in them, and their names come (somewhat muddled) from ancient Greek.

| Start on | Mode | Pattern | Like major or minor? | Character |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| C | **Ionian** | T T S T T T S | = major | bright, settled |
| D | **Dorian** | T S T T T S T | minor with a raised 6th | soulful, cool, folky |
| E | **Phrygian** | S T T T S T T | minor with a flat 2nd | dark, Spanish, intense |
| F | **Lydian** | T T T S T T S | major with a raised 4th | dreamy, floating, magical |
| G | **Mixolydian** | T T S T T S T | major with a flat 7th | bluesy, rocky, relaxed |
| A | **Aeolian** | T S T T S T T | = natural minor | sad, serious |
| B | **Locrian** | S T T S T T T | diminished 5th | unstable, rarely used |

A memory aid for the order: "**I D**on't **P**articularly **L**ike **M**odes **A** **L**ot".

## Hearing a mode: compare it to major or minor

The easiest way to understand a mode is as **major or minor with one note changed**. The changed note is the mode's characteristic note, its colour. Play the mode over its tonic held in the bass, and lean on that note:

- **Dorian** = natural minor with a **raised 6th**. D Dorian: D E F G A **B** C D (D minor would have B♭). The raised 6th gives a brighter, hopeful minor. "Scarborough Fair", "Eleanor Rigby", Miles Davis's "So What" and Santana's "Oye Como Va" are Dorian.
- **Mixolydian** = major with a **flat 7th**. G Mixolydian: G A B C D E **F** G (G major would have F♯). No leading note, so it sounds relaxed and earthy. "Norwegian Wood", "Sweet Home Alabama", many Celtic tunes and the blues lean Mixolydian.
- **Lydian** = major with a **raised 4th**. F Lydian: F G A **B** C D E F (F major would have B♭). The raised 4th floats upward: the sound of wonder in film music, and the theme of *The Simpsons*.
- **Phrygian** = natural minor with a **flat 2nd**. E Phrygian: E **F** G A B C D E. The semitone right above the tonic gives a dark, Spanish flamenco sound, and heavy metal riffs love it.

## Modes on any note

Any mode can start on any note. To build one, use the pattern, or the easier method: take the major or minor scale and change the characteristic note.

- C Dorian = C minor with a raised 6th: C D E♭ F G **A** B♭ C.
- C Mixolydian = C major with a flat 7th: C D E F G A **B♭** C.
- C Lydian = C major with a raised 4th: C D E **F♯** G A B C.

Or think of the "parent" major scale: Dorian is the major scale started on its 2nd degree, Mixolydian on its 5th, Lydian on its 4th, Phrygian on its 3rd. So C Dorian has the notes of B♭ major (C is the 2nd note of B♭ major); C Mixolydian has the notes of F major.

## Harmony in the modes

Modal music works differently from major and minor. Because Dorian and Mixolydian have no leading note, the strong V–I pull is missing. Instead, modal music often sits on the tonic chord and moves to the chord that shows off the characteristic note:

- **Dorian**: i – IV (Dm – G in D Dorian; the G major chord contains the bright B).
- **Mixolydian**: I – ♭VII (G – F in G Mixolydian; the F chord contains the flat 7th). The ♭VII–I cadence is everywhere in rock.
- **Lydian**: I – II (F – G in F Lydian; G contains the raised 4th, B).
- **Phrygian**: i – ♭II (Em – F in E Phrygian).

A **drone** (the tonic held in the bass, or tonic and 5th) is often the simplest way to make a mode sound like itself. Without it, the ear tends to hear the white keys as C major or A minor.

## Where modes live

- **Folk music**: many English, Irish and Scottish tunes are Dorian or Mixolydian.
- **Medieval and Renaissance music**: written in modes before major and minor existed.
- **Jazz**: modal jazz (Miles Davis's *Kind of Blue*) improvises over one mode for many bars; jazz musicians think of each chord as having its own mode.
- **Rock and pop**: Mixolydian riffs and ♭VII chords; Dorian grooves.
- **Film music**: Lydian for wonder and flight, Phrygian for menace, Dorian for heroic folk-tinged themes.
- **Impressionism**: Debussy and Ravel used modes to escape the pull of major and minor.

## Common confusions

- **Modes are just scales starting on different notes.** They are different patterns, with different tonics and characters, and need their tonic to be heard as home.
- **D Dorian is the same as C major.** Same notes, different home: D Dorian centres on D and sounds minor.
- **Modes are only old music.** They are everywhere in jazz, rock and film.

## In short

The seven modes are the scales you get by starting the white-key pattern on each of its notes. Ionian is major, Aeolian is natural minor. Dorian (minor with a raised 6th), Phrygian (minor with a flat 2nd), Lydian (major with a raised 4th) and Mixolydian (major with a flat 7th) each have one characteristic note. Without a leading note, modal harmony avoids V–I, using chords such as i–IV in Dorian and I–♭VII in Mixolydian, often over a drone.
