## Borrowing a little pull

You know that V7 pulls strongly to I: in C major, G7 resolves to C. What if a composer wants that same strong pull towards **another** chord in the key, say towards G (V) or towards A minor (vi)? The answer is to put **that chord's own dominant** in front of it, just for a moment. A dominant chord used to point at a chord other than the tonic is called a **secondary dominant** (or applied dominant).

Think of the key as a family, with the tonic as the head. A secondary dominant briefly treats another family member as if they were the head, without really changing key.

## V of V

The most common secondary dominant points at the dominant itself. In C major, V is G. What is the dominant of G? D, or D7 (D F♯ A C). Played in C major, D7 is written **V7/V** ("five seven of five") and resolves to G:

```figure

sound: "C4+E4+G4:h A3+D4+F#4+C5:h G3+B3+D4:h G3+B3+D4+F4:h C4+E4+G4:w"
caption: C – D7 (V7 of V) – G – G7 – C. The F sharp leads up to G, just as B leads up to C.
```

Notice the **F♯**. It does not belong to C major. It is the leading note of G, borrowed for a moment to make G sound like a destination. Then, as soon as we hit G and G7, F♮ returns and we are back in C. That is the fingerprint of a secondary dominant: **a chromatic note that rises by a semitone to the root of the next chord**, then disappears.

V/V is so common that it has its own feeling: a lift, a brightening, a sense of "here comes the end". It appears before countless final cadences: I – IV – **V/V** – V – I.

## Secondary dominants of every chord

Any major or minor chord in the key can be "tonicised" this way. The secondary dominant is the major chord (or dominant 7th) a 5th above the target. In C major:

| Target | Target chord | Secondary dominant | Notes | Chromatic note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ii | Dm | V7/ii = A7 | A C♯ E G | C♯ |
| iii | Em | V7/iii = B7 | B D♯ F♯ A | D♯ (and F♯) |
| IV | F | V7/IV = C7 | C E G B♭ | B♭ |
| V | G | V7/V = D7 | D F♯ A C | F♯ |
| vi | Am | V7/vi = E7 | E G♯ B D | G♯ |

The diminished chord vii° can't have a secondary dominant, because a diminished triad can't sound like a tonic.

```

> **Secondary dominant**: the dominant (V or V7) of a chord other than I, written **V/x** or **V7/x**. It brings in a chromatic note that rises a semitone to the root of its target, and briefly makes that chord sound like home.

## Secondary dominants and modulation

A secondary dominant is a short visit; a modulation is a move. If D7 – G is followed straight away by C again, we never left C major: G was just tonicised for a moment. If D7 – G is followed by more F♯s, more D7s and a cadence in G, the music has modulated. The difference is time and confirmation. In fact, secondary dominants are the usual doorway into a modulation.

## Chains of dominants

Since a dominant chord resolves down a 5th, and the result can itself be a dominant, composers can build **chains**: E7 – A7 – D7 – G7 – C. Each chord is the dominant of the next, and the bass falls by 5ths round the circle. This is the **circle progression**, the backbone of countless jazz standards and ragtime pieces ("Sweet Georgia Brown", the bridge of many 1930s songs) and also of Baroque sequences.

```

## Recognising secondary dominants in music

When you find an unexpected accidental in a piece:

1. Ask whether it is a **sharp or natural that rises** a semitone (or a flat that falls).
2. Look at the chord it belongs to: is it a major chord or a dominant 7th?
3. Look at the next chord: is its root a 5th below (or a 4th above), and is the chromatic note the semitone below that root?

If so, you have found a secondary dominant. In songs written with chord symbols, you'll often see them as "unexpected" major chords: in C major, a D or D7 instead of Dm, an E or E7 instead of Em, an A or A7 instead of Am. Each points towards the chord a 5th below.

## How they sound and how to play them

Secondary dominants add warmth and forward motion. They are the "colour" chords that make a hymn, a Beatles song or a Schubert song sound rich rather than plain. When playing, let the chromatic note lead: voice it in the melody or top of the chord if you can, and feel it rise into the next chord.

## Common confusions

- **A sharp means a new key.** Often it is only a secondary dominant, a short visit.
- **Secondary dominants are minor.** They are always major or dominant 7th chords; that's what gives them their pull.
- **V7/V is the same as V7.** In C, V7 is G7; V7/V is D7, the dominant of G.

## In short

A secondary dominant is the dominant of a chord other than the tonic: V/V (D in C major), V/vi (E in C), V/ii, V/iii, V/IV. Its chromatic note rises a semitone to the root of the target chord, tonicising it for a moment without changing key. Chains of dominants run round the circle of fifths. They explain most of the surprise accidentals and "unexpected" major chords in tonal music.
