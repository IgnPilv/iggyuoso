## Harmony: more than one note at once

A tune on its own is **melody**: one note at a time. As soon as two or more notes sound together you have **harmony**. Harmony is what turns a bare tune into something full and rich. It is the difference between humming a song and hearing it with a band or a piano behind it. A group of notes played together is called a **chord**.

You could press any three keys at once and call it a chord, and some combinations sound lovely while others sound like an accident. Over several hundred years, musicians settled on one kind of chord as the basic building block of almost all Western music, from Bach to the Beatles. It is called the **triad**.

## How a triad is built

A triad is three notes stacked in **3rds**: you take a note, add the note a 3rd above it, and then the note a 3rd above that. Starting on C:

- **C** is the bottom note, called the **root**. It gives the chord its name.
- **E**, a 3rd above C, is called the **third**.
- **G**, a 3rd above E (and a 5th above C), is called the **fifth**.

C, E and G together make the **C major chord**, or simply the **C chord**.

```figure
sound: "C4:q E4:q G4:q C4+E4+G4:w"

```

> **A triad** is three notes stacked in 3rds: **root**, **third** and **fifth**. The root names the chord.

On the staff a triad in this basic shape looks like a little snowman: three notes on three neighbouring lines (C E G with C on a ledger line, then E and G on lines) or on three neighbouring spaces. Line-line-line or space-space-space. That is the "every other note" pattern of 3rds, and it makes triads easy to spot.

## Under the hand

In a five-finger position, a triad is the simplest shape there is: **fingers 1, 3 and 5**. The thumb plays the root, the middle finger the third, the little finger the fifth. In the left hand it is the same three fingers in mirror image: 5 on the root, 3 on the third, 1 on the fifth.

```figure
keys: { notes: "C3 E3 G3 C4 E4 G4", labels: fingers, fingers: "5 3 1 1 3 5" }
caption: The C triad in both hands: 5-3-1 in the left, 1-3-5 in the right.
```

Because the shape is the same everywhere, you can build a triad on any white key by putting your thumb (right hand) on it and playing "skip, skip". Try it on F: F, A, C. On G: G, B, D. Those three chords, C, F and G, are the next topic.

## Three chords that go a long way

With just three triads, **C** (C E G), **F** (F A C) and **G** (G B D), you can accompany a remarkable number of songs: folk tunes, carols, children's songs, rock and roll classics. Together these three chords contain every note of the C major scale, so any tune that uses only white keys and stays in C can be harmonised with them.

```figure
staff: { notes: "C4+E4+G4:h F4+A4+C5:h | G4+B4+D5:h C4+E4+G4:h", labels: names }
sound: "C4+E4+G4:h F4+A4+C5:h G4+B4+D5:h C4+E4+G4:h"
caption: C, F, G and back to C. Listen to how the G chord seems to want to go home.
```

Listen carefully to that last move from G back to C. It sounds like coming home, like the end of a sentence. That pull is the beginning of everything you will learn about harmony. In the next chapter you will meet the numbers musicians use for these three chords, **I, IV and V**, and in later chapters you will find out why V pulls so strongly to I.

## Broken chords

The notes of a chord do not always have to sound at once. Played one after another, they make a **broken chord** (or **arpeggio**, from the Italian for "like a harp"). Much accompaniment works this way: the left hand plays C, G, E, G over and over instead of a solid chord. It is still the C chord; only the way of playing it has changed.

## Hearing chords

Chords are harder to hear "inside" than single notes, because the notes melt together. That is exactly what they are meant to do. The ear branch begins chord work by asking simply **how many notes** are sounding (one, two, or three), and only later asks what kind of chord it is. Do not worry if you cannot pick out the three notes yet. With practice the chord starts to sound like a single colour, and you learn to recognise it the way you recognise a face.

```figure

  ask: "Which three notes make a triad on G?"
  choices: ["G, B, D", "G, A, B", "G, C, E"]

```

## Common confusions

- **Root, third and fifth are not finger numbers.** They are the chord's own names for its notes. In the right hand they happen to match fingers 1, 3, 5 in the basic shape, but the left hand plays the root with finger 5.
- **The root is not always at the bottom.** In later chapters the notes get rearranged (inversions). The root is still C even if E is on the bottom.
- **Pressing the notes unevenly.** All three keys of a chord should go down at exactly the same moment. This app treats notes within about 60 ms as one chord.

## In short

Harmony is notes sounding together; a chord is a group of them. The triad, three notes stacked in 3rds, is the basic chord of Western music: root, third and fifth, played with fingers 1-3-5. On the staff it looks like a snowman on lines or on spaces. C, F and G, three triads, harmonise a huge number of tunes, and the move from G back to C sounds like coming home. Chords can also be broken into patterns.
