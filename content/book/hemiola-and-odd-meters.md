## When groupings shift

Every meter groups beats in a regular way: 3/4 in threes, 6/8 in two groups of three. Your ear locks onto that grouping and expects it to continue. This chapter is about two ways music plays with that expectation: **hemiola**, where the grouping briefly changes within a meter, and **odd meters**, where the grouping itself is uneven.

## Hemiola: three twos in the space of two threes

Six beats can be grouped in two ways: **3 + 3** (two groups of three) or **2 + 2 + 2** (three groups of two). A **hemiola** (from Greek, "one and a half") is a passage where music in groups of three is briefly heard in groups of two, or the other way round.

The most common kind happens across **two bars of 3/4**: normally you feel **1** 2 3 | **1** 2 3. In a hemiola, the accents and long notes fall so that you feel **1** 2 | **1** 2 | **1** 2 across the two bars, as if one bar of 3/2 had been written in.


Hemiola is especially common **at cadences** in Baroque and Classical music in triple time: minuets, sarabandes, courantes. The broadening of the beat just before the end makes the cadence feel weighty and final. Brahms used it constantly, blurring the meter for longer stretches. In Latin American music, alternating 6/8 and 3/4 feel (as in "America" from *West Side Story*: "I like to be in A-me-ri-ca") is a hemiola rhythm repeated as a groove.

> **Hemiola**: six beats felt as 2 + 2 + 2 instead of 3 + 3 (or the reverse), most often across two bars of 3/4 just before a cadence.

### Playing a hemiola

The danger is to let the new grouping change the tempo. The **beat stays exactly the same speed**; only the accents move. Count the underlying beats steadily ("1 2 3 1 2 3"), feel the half-note groupings on top of them, and enjoy the friction. In music with hemiolas, it often helps to give a slight weight to each of the three long notes, then let the cadence resolve the tension.

## Odd meters: fives and sevens

Most Western music is in two, three or four beats (and their compound forms). **Odd meters** (or asymmetric meters) have five, seven, or other uneven numbers of beats in a bar: 5/4, 7/8, 5/8, 11/8. They cannot be split into equal groups, so they are felt as a mix of twos and threes:

| Meter | Common groupings | Feels like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5/4 | 3 + 2 or 2 + 3 | a waltz with a short bar, or a march with an extra beat |
| 7/8 | 2 + 2 + 3, 3 + 2 + 2, 2 + 3 + 2 | a limp, a dance step |
| 5/8 | 3 + 2 or 2 + 3 | a quick, lopsided pulse |

The trick is to **feel the groups, not count to seven**. 7/8 as 2 + 2 + 3 is "**1** 2 **1** 2 **1** 2 3", or in words, "**quick** **quick** **slow**": two short beats and a long one. 5/4 as 3 + 2 is "**1** 2 3 **4** 5".

### Where odd meters live

- **Folk music of the Balkans, Greece and Turkey**, where dances in 7/8, 9/8 and 11/8 are an everyday rhythm, felt as naturally as a waltz. Bartók, who collected folk music in Hungary and Bulgaria, wrote "Bulgarian rhythm" pieces in his *Mikrokosmos*.
- **Jazz**: Dave Brubeck's "Take Five" (5/4) and "Blue Rondo à la Turk" (9/8 as 2+2+2+3).
- **Classical**: the second movement of Tchaikovsky's "Pathétique" symphony is a waltz in 5/4; Holst's "Mars" is in 5/4.
- **Rock and film**: the *Mission: Impossible* theme (5/4), Pink Floyd's "Money" (7/4), many progressive rock songs.

## Practising uneven rhythms

1. **Say the groups** out loud ("1 2 1 2 1 2 3") with an accent on each "1". Clap on the accents only.
2. **Walk or tap** the groups: one step per group, so the steps are uneven (short, short, long).
3. **Play with a click on the eighths** at first, then a click only on the group starts.
4. For hemiola, **conduct in three** while singing in two, then swap.
5. Listen to music in these meters until the uneven pulse feels like dancing, not counting.

## Common confusions

- **A hemiola changes the tempo.** The beat stays the same; only the grouping changes.
- **Odd meters are irregular or random.** They are completely regular, just with uneven groups that repeat every bar.
- **You must count every eighth.** Counting is for learning; at tempo, feel the groups.

## In short

A hemiola regroups six beats from 3 + 3 to 2 + 2 + 2 (or back), most often over two bars of 3/4 before a cadence, broadening the music without changing the beat. Odd meters such as 5/4 and 7/8 have uneven groups of twos and threes, felt as long and short beats rather than counted. Both are found in Baroque dances, Balkan folk music, jazz, classical and rock, and both become natural with practice.
