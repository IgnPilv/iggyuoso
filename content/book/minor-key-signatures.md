## Borrowed signatures

A minor key never has a key signature of its own. It uses the signature of its **relative major**, the major key whose 6th degree it starts on, a minor 3rd above it. A minor uses C major's signature (nothing); E minor uses G major's (one sharp); D minor uses F major's (one flat). The raised 6ths and 7ths of the harmonic and melodic minor are always written as accidentals in the music, never in the signature.

| A minor | C | none |
| E minor | G | 1♯ (F♯) |
| B minor | D | 2♯ (F♯ C♯) |

## Finding the signature of a minor key

Two methods, use whichever is faster for you:

1. **Go up a minor 3rd** to the relative major, and use its signature. F minor → up a minor 3rd → A♭ major → four flats.
2. **Shift by three on the circle of fifths.** A minor key has three fewer sharps (or three more flats) than its parallel major. C major has none, so C minor has three flats. E major has four sharps, so E minor has one. D major has two sharps, so D minor has one flat (two minus three is "minus one", one flat).

## Major or minor? Reading the clues

A key signature always stands for two keys, a major and its relative minor. How do you know which a piece is in? Look for these clues, roughly in order of reliability:

1. **The final bass note** (and the final chord). Pieces nearly always end on the tonic. If a piece with one sharp ends on E in the bass, it is E minor; on G, G major.
2. **Raised leading notes.** In a minor piece the 7th degree is often raised with an accidental: D♯ in E minor, G♯ in A minor, C♯ in D minor. Lots of these is a strong sign of the minor key.
3. **The opening.** The first bass note or chord is often the tonic.
4. **The character.** Does it sound major or minor? Trust your ear, then confirm with the clues.

## What to expect inside a minor piece

Because minor keys use three versions of their scale, minor music is often full of accidentals, even in simple pieces. Do not be alarmed: they are almost always the raised 6th and 7th degrees. In A minor that means F♯ and G♯ will pop up, especially near cadences and in rising lines; F and G natural will appear in falling lines. Knowing this in advance makes sight-reading minor music much easier. Before reading, say to yourself: "E minor, one sharp, watch for D sharps (and C sharps going up)."

## Minor keys and moods

Different minor keys have long had associations, partly from how they sit on instruments. D minor was often thought of as solemn (Mozart's Requiem, Bach's Toccata and Fugue); C minor as stormy and heroic (Beethoven's Fifth Symphony, the *Pathétique* sonata); G minor as restless (Mozart's Symphony No. 40). On a piano tuned in equal temperament, the keys are technically the same sound shifted, but each still feels different under the hand, and composers' choices have shaped how we hear them.

## Common confusions

- **Putting the raised 7th in the signature.** It always goes in the music as an accidental.
- **Two flats always means B♭ major.** It may be G minor; check the ending.
- **Minor keys have more sharps or flats.** Each minor key has three fewer sharps (or three more flats) than its parallel major, not more in general.

## In short

A minor key borrows the key signature of its relative major, a minor 3rd above, or equivalently has three fewer sharps (three more flats) than its parallel major. Raised 6ths and 7ths are accidentals. To tell major from minor, check the final bass note, look for raised leading notes, and listen. Expect accidentals on degrees 6 and 7 in minor music.
