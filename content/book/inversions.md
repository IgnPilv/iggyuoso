## The same three notes, a different order

A C major chord is C, E and G. So far you have always played it with C at the bottom: C E G. But you could play E G C, or G C E, and it would still be a C major chord, made of the same three notes. These rearrangements are called **inversions**.

- **Root position**: the **root** is the lowest note. C E G.
- **First inversion**: the **third** is the lowest note. E G C.
- **Second inversion**: the **fifth** is the lowest note. G C E.

What decides the inversion is **only the lowest note** (the bass). The order of the notes above it does not matter: E C G and E G C are both first inversion, because E is at the bottom. On the piano, the bass is usually the lowest note of the left hand.

> **Root position**: root in the bass. **First inversion**: third in the bass. **Second inversion**: fifth in the bass. The chord's name never changes, only its bass note.

## Recognising inversions on the staff

A root-position triad in close position is the neat snowman: three notes on three lines or three spaces, each a 3rd apart. Inversions break the snowman, because one note jumps up an octave and leaves a **4th** inside the chord:

- **First inversion** (E G C): a 3rd at the bottom, a **4th on top**.
- **Second inversion** (G C E): a **4th at the bottom**, a 3rd on top.

So if you see a three-note chord with a 4th in it, find the note just above the 4th: that is the root. In E G C, the 4th is G–C, so C is the root. In G C E, the 4th is G–C, and again C is the root.

## Why inversions matter: smooth movement

Play C major in root position, then F major in root position, then G major in root position, all in the right hand. Your hand jumps about, and the chords sound blocky, like a series of separate thumps.

Now play C (C E G), then F in second inversion (C F A), then G in first inversion (B D G), then back to C. Your hand barely moves. Each note goes to the nearest note of the next chord, and the chords connect into one smooth flow.

```figure

```

This idea, moving each note of a chord the shortest possible distance to a note of the next chord, is called **voice leading**. It is one of the central skills in harmony, and inversions are the tool that makes it possible. Notice what happens: the note **C**, shared by the C and F chords, simply stays where it is. Common notes stay; the others step to their nearest neighbour. Good accompaniments sound smooth precisely because of this.

## Inversions in the bass

Inversions also change the **bass line**. With every chord in root position, the bass jumps between roots (C, F, G, C). Using a first-inversion chord can make the bass move by step instead: C, then G in first inversion has B in the bass, which leads up to C. A bass line C – B – C is smoother and more melodic than C – G – C. Composers choose inversions to make the bass line sing.

## The colours of inversions

Each position has its own colour:

- **Root position** sounds solid and final. Pieces nearly always end on a root-position tonic chord.
- **First inversion** sounds lighter and more flowing, less final. It is very common in the middle of phrases.
- **Second inversion** sounds unstable when the bass is prominent, because of the 4th above the bass. In classical music it is used in special ways, most famously just before the dominant at a cadence. In accompaniment patterns where the bass is not stressed, it is used freely.

## Figured bass: how inversions are labelled

Musicians label inversions with small numbers after the Roman numeral, from an old shorthand called **figured bass**. You will see:

- **I** (or I⁵₃): root position.
- **I⁶**: first inversion (the root is a 6th above the bass).
- **I⁶₄**: second inversion (the root is a 4th and the third a 6th above the bass).

Pop and jazz use **slash chords** instead: **C/E** means a C chord with E in the bass (first inversion); **C/G** means C with G in the bass (second inversion).

## Common confusions

- **The order of the upper notes decides the inversion.** Only the bass does.
- **An inversion is a different chord.** Same chord, same name, different bass.
- **Second inversion sounds wrong.** Only when it is used carelessly with a strong bass; in accompaniments it is everywhere.

## In short

An inversion rearranges a chord so that a note other than the root is in the bass: the third (first inversion) or the fifth (second inversion). Inverted chords contain a 4th, and the root sits just above it. Inversions let chords move smoothly, with common notes held and others stepping to the nearest note: voice leading. They also shape the bass line. Labels: I, I⁶, I⁶₄, or slash chords like C/E.
