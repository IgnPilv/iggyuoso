## The whole set

You have met keys one at a time: C, then G and F, then D, B♭, A, E♭, and their relative minors. Now it is time to see the complete system. There are **fifteen key signatures**: one with no sharps or flats, seven with sharps (one to seven) and seven with flats (one to seven). Each signature belongs to one major key and one minor key, its relative, so there are fifteen major keys and fifteen minor keys in all.

| 0 | C | A | | 0 | C | A |

| 6 | F♯ | D♯ | | 6 | G♭ | E♭ |

## The two orders

Sharps and flats are always written in the same fixed order. Learn these two lines and you can write any signature:

> **Sharps**: F C G D A E B. **Flats**: B E A D G C F (the sharps backwards).

A common memory aid for the sharps is "**F**ather **C**harles **G**oes **D**own **A**nd **E**nds **B**attle", and backwards for the flats: "**B**attle **E**nds **A**nd **D**own **G**oes **C**harles' **F**ather". A key with three sharps always has the first three: F♯, C♯, G♯. A key with four flats always has B♭, E♭, A♭, D♭. Never any other combination.

Each order is a chain of **5ths**: F up a 5th is C, up a 5th is G, and so on. Flats go down in 5ths: B, E, A, D... This is the circle of fifths again, written as accidentals.

## Trick one: naming a sharp key

Look at the **last sharp** (the one furthest right) and go **up a semitone**. That is the major key.

- Last sharp F♯ → G major.
- Last sharp C♯ → D major.
- Last sharp D♯ → E major.
- Last sharp A♯ → B major.

It works because the last sharp added is always the new key's leading note, a semitone below the tonic.

## Trick two: naming a flat key

Look at the **second-to-last flat**: that is the major key.

- B♭ E♭ → E♭ major.
- B♭ E♭ A♭ D♭ → A♭ major.

The one exception is F major, with one flat (B♭): there is no second-to-last, so simply remember it.

For the minor key, go **down three semitones** (a minor 3rd) from the major tonic, and use the letter name two letters back. E♭ major → C minor. E major → C♯ minor. The minor key shares the signature but not the tonic.

## Writing a signature on the staff

Each accidental has a fixed place on the staff, in a zigzag pattern that keeps them within or close to the staff:

- **Sharps** in the treble clef: F on the top line, C in the third space, G above the staff, D on the fourth line, A in the second space, E in the top space, B on the middle line. In the bass clef the same pattern is placed an octave lower (F on the fourth line, and so on).
- **Flats** in the treble clef: B on the middle line, E in the top space, A in the second space, D on the fourth line, G on the second line, C in the third space, F in the bottom space. They go up a 4th, down a 5th, up a 4th, down a 5th, a zigzag that drifts downward. In the bass clef the pattern sits a line lower in the same shape (B on the second line).

The signature is written at the start of every line of music, after the clef and before the time signature, in both staves of the grand staff.

## The keys that overlap

Look at the bottom of the table: B major (5 sharps) sounds the same as C♭ major (7 flats); F♯ major (6 sharps) sounds the same as G♭ major (6 flats); C♯ major (7 sharps) sounds the same as D♭ major (5 flats). These are **enharmonic keys**: the same keys on the piano, spelt differently. Composers choose whichever is easier to read, usually the one with fewer accidentals. So you will meet D♭ major far more often than C♯ major, and B major more often than C♭ major. F♯ and G♭, both with six, are equally common.

That gives the twelve different-sounding keys on the piano, with three of them having two names: 12 + 3 = 15 signatures.

## Why learn them all?

Most of the music you will play in the next few years uses keys with up to four sharps or flats. But knowing the complete system takes the mystery out of any score you open, makes transposing much easier, and lets you see at once which keys are close relatives (neighbours with one accidental difference) and which are far apart. Chopin's nocturnes in D♭ and B major, Schubert's impromptus in G♭ and A♭: these remote keys are full of beautiful music, and the black keys are, in fact, comfortable under the hand.

## Common confusions

- **Sharps and flats can be mixed in a signature.** Never; a signature is all sharps or all flats.
- **The last sharp is the key.** Go a semitone up from it.
- **Any order of accidentals is fine.** The order is always F C G D A E B or B E A D G C F.

## In short

There are fifteen key signatures: none, one to seven sharps, one to seven flats, each shared by a major key and its relative minor. Sharps come in the order F C G D A E B, flats in the reverse. For sharp keys, the last sharp plus a semitone names the major key; for flat keys, the second-to-last flat does (F major has one flat). Three pairs of keys are enharmonic. The minor key lies a minor 3rd below its relative major.
