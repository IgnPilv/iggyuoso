## A new home

So far, a key has been the home a piece starts and ends in. But most pieces of any length do not stay in one key the whole time. Partway through, the tonic itself shifts: a new note becomes home, with its own leading note and its own cadence. This change of key is called **modulation**. The music later usually returns to the original key, and that return is one of the most satisfying moments in music.

Modulation is different from simply using a chromatic note or two. A borrowed note decorates the old key; a modulation **establishes** a new one, usually confirmed by a cadence in the new key.

## Where pieces go

Composers can modulate anywhere, but most modulations go to **closely related keys**, the ones that share all but one note with the home key (neighbours on the circle of fifths). From a major key, the favourite destinations are:

- The **dominant** key, a 5th up (C major → G major). By far the most common, especially in classical music. Sonata form, which you will meet later, is built on it.
- The **relative minor** (C major → A minor).
- The **subdominant** key, a 5th down (C major → F major), often near the end of a piece.

From a minor key, the favourite destination is the **relative major** (A minor → C major), then the dominant minor (A minor → E minor).

## How a modulation works

To move from C major to G major, the music needs to make G sound like home instead of C. The key ingredient is the **new leading note**: G major's leading note is **F♯**, a note that does not exist in C major. When F♯ starts appearing regularly, and especially when a **D major or D7 chord** (the dominant of G) resolves to G, the ear accepts G as the new tonic.


The smoothest modulations use a **pivot chord**: a chord that belongs to both keys. In C major and G major, A minor is vi in C and ii in G. The music arrives at A minor as part of C major, and then treats it as ii in G, moving on to D7 (V7 in G) and G (I in G). The listener slides from one key to the other without a bump. Other common pivot chords between C and G are C itself (I in C, IV in G) and E minor (iii in C, vi in G).

> **Modulation**: a change of key, confirmed by the new key's leading note and a cadence in the new key. Smooth modulations use a **pivot chord** that belongs to both keys.

## Signs of a modulation in the score

- **A new accidental that keeps returning.** In a C major piece, repeated F♯s suggest G major; repeated B♭s suggest F major; repeated G♯s suggest A minor.
- **A cadence on a new tonic**: a phrase ending V–I in the new key.
- **A change of key signature** (for longer passages or big movements; short modulations usually use accidentals only).
- **The sound**: the music seems to settle somewhere new, often brighter (to the dominant) or darker (to a minor key).

## Direct modulation and the key change in pop

Not all modulations are smooth. Sometimes a piece simply jumps to a new key, without a pivot: a **direct** (or abrupt) modulation. The most famous kind is the "truck driver's gear change" in pop music: the last chorus of a song shifts up a semitone or a tone, giving a sudden lift of excitement. Think of the final chorus of many 1980s ballads. It is not subtle, but it works.

## Why composers modulate

- **Contrast**: a new key gives a new colour without changing the material. A theme played again in the dominant sounds fresh.
- **Drama and journey**: moving away from home and returning gives a piece a sense of narrative. The return to the home key feels like coming home after a journey.
- **Structure**: in larger forms, keys mark out the sections. In a sonata, the second theme comes in the dominant, and the whole recapitulation returns to the tonic.
- **Expression**: a sudden shift to a distant key can be breathtaking, a favourite device of Schubert and many film composers.

## Hearing modulation

Listen for the moment when the music seems to have changed its centre of gravity. A useful trick: at any point, try to sing the tonic. If the note you would sing has changed from the beginning, the music has modulated. In longer pieces, follow the journey: home, away, further away, and home again.

## Common confusions

- **Any accidental is a modulation.** A single chromatic note is decoration; a modulation needs a new tonic confirmed by a cadence.
- **Modulation always changes the key signature.** Usually it does not; accidentals do the work.
- **The relative minor is "the same key".** It shares the notes but has a different tonic: moving there is a real modulation.

## In short

Modulation moves a piece to a new home key, confirmed by the new leading note and a cadence in the new key. Most modulations go to closely related keys: the dominant, the relative minor or major, the subdominant. Smooth modulations pass through a pivot chord shared by both keys; direct ones jump. Watch for recurring new accidentals and cadences on new tonics, and listen for the music's centre of gravity shifting.
