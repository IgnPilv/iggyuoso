## Music has grammar

When you read a story, you do not experience it as a stream of letters. You hear words, sentences, paragraphs, chapters. Music is built the same way, in layers, and once you can see the layers, pieces become far easier to learn, remember and play expressively. This large-scale shape of a piece is called its **form**.

## Motif: the smallest idea

The smallest meaningful unit in music is a **motif** (or motive): a short group of notes, often just two to five, with a recognisable shape or rhythm. The most famous motif in history is the opening of Beethoven's Fifth Symphony: three short notes and a long one, "da-da-da-DUM". Beethoven builds a whole movement from it, repeating it, moving it to other notes, turning it upside down.

## Phrase: a musical sentence

Motifs combine into **phrases**: complete musical thoughts, usually **four bars** long in the music you are learning (sometimes two or eight). A phrase is like a sentence: it has a beginning, a direction and an ending, and it usually ends with a **cadence**, the musical punctuation from the last chapter. When you sing a phrase, it is roughly the length you can sing on one breath, and that is a good test: where would a singer breathe?

In written music, a phrase is sometimes shown by a long curved line over it, a **phrase mark**. It looks like a slur but spans a whole phrase. Even when it is not marked, you can find phrases by looking for cadences and for the places where the melody rests on a long note.

## Question and answer

Phrases very often come in pairs that belong together, like a question and its answer. The first phrase ends on something open, often on the dominant, a **half cadence** (a musical comma; you will meet it properly in the chapter on cadences). The second phrase usually starts the same way but ends on the tonic with a perfect cadence: the answer, a full stop. This pair is called a **period**. The two phrases are sometimes called the **antecedent** (question) and **consequent** (answer).

Listen to how the two halves of *Ode to Joy* are identical until the last two bars, and how that tiny change, ending on D, then on C, turns a question into an answer. This is one of the most common designs in all of music.

> **Motif** (a tiny idea) → **phrase** (a musical sentence, often 4 bars, ending with a cadence) → **period** (question phrase + answer phrase) → **sections** → the **form** of the piece.

## Repetition, variation and contrast

Music balances three things:

- **Repetition** makes music memorable and understandable. Hear an idea once and you notice it; hear it again and you recognise it.
- **Variation** keeps repetition interesting: the idea comes back changed, higher or lower, with a new ending, a new rhythm, a new harmony.
- **Contrast** brings something new: a different tune, a different key, a different mood, so that the return of the first idea feels fresh.

Composers are always weighing these. Too much repetition is boring, too much contrast is chaos. The Sketchbook and the composing lessons ask you to use exactly these tools.

## Naming sections with letters

To describe form, musicians label each section with a capital letter. The first idea is **A**. A different, contrasting idea is **B**, then **C** and so on. If A comes back changed, it is often written **A′** ("A prime"). The most common forms are:

- **Binary form, A B**: two contrasting sections, often each repeated (A A B B). Most Baroque dances, including Bach's minuets, are in binary form.
- **Ternary form, A B A**: a first section, a contrasting middle, and a return of the first. It is satisfying because of the homecoming. Many short pieces, and the "minuet and trio" of classical symphonies, are ternary. "D.C. al Fine" often signals it.
- **Rondo, A B A C A**: the main theme keeps coming back between different episodes, like a refrain. You will meet it later along with sonata form.
- **Strophic form, A A A**: the same music repeated with new words each time: hymns, folk songs, many ballads.
- **Verse and chorus**: the backbone of pop music, a verse (new words each time) alternating with a chorus (the same words each time), often with a **bridge** (a contrasting section) near the end.

A popular song form, especially in older pop and jazz standards, is **A A B A**: a tune, the same tune again, a contrasting bridge, and the tune once more. *Over the Rainbow* and countless others follow it.

## Why form helps you play

Knowing the form of a piece makes it much easier to learn. If you see that bars 9–12 repeat bars 1–4, you have one less thing to learn. If you know a phrase is a question, you can shape it to sound open, and play its answer to sound settled. When you memorise, you remember sections and their order rather than hundreds of notes. Before learning a new piece, take a minute to find its phrases, cadences and repeats, and label the sections.

## Common confusions

- **A phrase is any group of notes.** It is a complete thought, usually ending with a cadence.
- **Repeats are form.** Repeat signs are a writing shortcut; form is about which material comes back and when.
- **Letters name keys.** In form, A and B name sections, not notes.

## In short

Music is built in layers: motifs make phrases, phrases make periods and sections, sections make the form. A phrase is a musical sentence, often four bars, ending with a cadence; two phrases often pair as question and answer. Music balances repetition, variation and contrast. Forms are labelled with letters: binary A B, ternary A B A, rondo A B A C A, strophic A A A, verse and chorus, A A B A.
