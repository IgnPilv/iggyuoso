## Music has a home

Sing the last line of almost any familiar song and stop on the second-to-last note. It feels unfinished, as if the music is hanging in the air waiting to land. Then sing the last note and everything settles. That last note is the music's **home note**, and the feeling of everything leaning towards it is one of the strongest forces in music.

The home note is called the **tonic**. The family of notes a piece uses, gathered around that home, is its **key**. A piece "in C major" uses mostly the notes of the C major scale (the white keys) and treats C as home: it tends to start near C, wander away, and come back to C at the end.

So far nearly everything you have played has been in C, with C as home and no black keys. But home can be anywhere. Moving the whole family to a new home note is called changing the key, and that is where sharps and flats begin to do real work.

## The key of G: one sharp

Put your right thumb on G and play five notes up: G A B C D. So far, all white keys. That is **G position**. Now play the full scale from G to the next G using only white keys: G A B C D E F G. Something sounds wrong near the end. The F sounds too low; it does not lead up to G the way B leads up to C in the C scale.

```figure
sound: "G4:q A4:q B4:q C5:q D5:q E5:q F5:q G5:h"
caption: G to G on white keys only. Listen to the F near the top: it sounds flat and unsettled.
```

To make the G scale sound like the C scale, the F must be raised by a semitone, to **F sharp**. Then the next-to-last note sits a semitone below the home note, just as B sits below C, and the scale climbs home convincingly.

```figure
staff: { notes: "G4:q A4:q B4:q C5:q D5:q E5:q F#5:q G5:q", labels: names }
keys: { notes: "G4 A4 B4 C5 D5 E5 F#5 G5", labels: names }
sound: "G4:q A4:q B4:q C5:q D5:q E5:q F#5:q G5:h"
caption: The G major scale. Every F becomes F sharp.
```

That is the key of **G major**: the same pattern as C major, moved up to start on G, and it needs exactly one sharp, **F♯**. You will see exactly why in the next chapter, when we look at the pattern of tones and semitones that every major scale follows.

## The key of F: one flat

Try the same experiment from F: F G A B C D E F on white keys. This time the trouble comes early. The fourth note, B, sounds too high, too bright and pushy.

```figure
sound: "F4:q G4:q A4:q B4:q C5:q D5:q E5:q F5:h"
caption: F to F on white keys only. The B sticks out.
```

Lower the B by a semitone to **B flat**, and the scale sounds like a major scale again.

```figure
staff: { notes: "F4:q G4:q A4:q Bb4:q C5:q D5:q E5:q F5:q", labels: names }
keys: { notes: "F4 G4 A4 Bb4 C5 D5 E5 F5", labels: names }
sound: "F4:q G4:q A4:q Bb4:q C5:q D5:q E5:q F5:h"
caption: The F major scale. Every B becomes B flat.
```

So **F major** has one flat, **B♭**. In F position (right thumb on F) the fourth finger plays B♭, a black key. That is a nice reminder built into your hand.

## The key signature

In a piece in G major, nearly every F will be F sharp. Writing a sharp sign in front of every single F would be tiring to write and cluttered to read. So music uses a shortcut: the key's sharps or flats are written **once**, at the start of every line, just after the clef. This is the **key signature**.

```figure
staff: { notes: "G4:q B4:q D5:q F#5:q | G5:w", key: G, labels: names }
caption: The key signature of G major: one sharp, on the top line, which is F. Every F in the piece is now F sharp.
```

```figure
staff: { notes: "F4:q A4:q C5:q Bb4:q | F4:w", key: F, labels: names }
caption: The key signature of F major: one flat, on the middle line, B. Every B is B flat.
```

A key signature applies to **every note of that letter in every octave**, for the whole piece (unless it changes). The sharp in G major is drawn on the top line, F5, but it means every F, high or low. An accidental can still override it for a single bar: a natural sign in front of an F in G major makes it plain F until the bar line.

> **A key signature** writes the key's sharps or flats once, after the clef, on every line. It changes that letter in every octave for the whole piece. No sharps or flats: C major. One sharp (F♯): G major. One flat (B♭): F major.

## Reading with a key signature

The danger with key signatures is that the sharp or flat is out of sight, at the start of the line, while you are reading far to the right. Good habits:

1. **Before you play, look at the key signature** and say it out loud: "one sharp, F sharp, G major".
2. Find where those notes fall in the hand position. In G position, the right hand has no F inside its five notes, but the left hand might. In F position, finger 4 of the right hand is on B♭.
3. While reading, keep a tiny alarm in your head for that one letter.

```figure
check:
  ask: "A piece has one sharp in its key signature. You see a note on the bottom space of the treble staff (F). What do you play?"
  choices: ["F sharp", "F", "G"]
  correct: 0
  why: "The key signature's sharp is F sharp, and it applies to **every** F in every octave, even though it is drawn on the top line."
```

## Hearing the key

The key is not only a matter of which black keys you press. Each key has the same home-and-away feeling built in, just at a different height. A tune in G feels exactly like the same tune in C, a little higher. The ear branch trains you to feel the home note: after a short chord pattern sets up the key, you will be asked which scale step you heard and to hear it resolve home. That sense of home is one of the most important things a musician hears.

## Common confusions

- **The sharp only applies on the top line.** No: every F in every octave.
- **Every piece with one sharp is in G.** Usually, but there is a second key with one sharp (E minor). You will meet it with the minor keys.
- **Forgetting the signature on the second line.** It is repeated at the start of every line, as a reminder. Look at it each time.

## In short

Music circles a home note, the tonic; the family of notes around it is the key. G major is the C major pattern moved to G and needs F♯. F major needs B♭. A key signature writes these once after the clef, and they apply to every note of that letter, in every octave, all the way through. Check it before you play.
