## Punctuation, in full

In an earlier chapter you met the **perfect cadence**, V–I, music's full stop. But just as writing needs commas, question marks and the occasional dash, music has several kinds of cadence, each with its own effect. The four main ones in traditional harmony are:

| Cadence | Chords | Effect | Like |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Perfect (authentic) | V – I | final, complete | a full stop |
| Plagal | IV – I | gentle, final | "Amen" |
| Imperfect (half) | any – V | open, unfinished | a comma or question mark |
| Interrupted (deceptive) | V – vi | surprise, not finished | a dash, "but…" |

Two of them end on I and feel finished (perfect and plagal). Two end elsewhere and keep the music going (imperfect and interrupted).

## Perfect cadence: V – I

You know this one: the dominant (often V7) resolving to the tonic. The leading note rises, the bass falls a 5th, and the music arrives home. It ends nearly every classical piece and most songs.

## Plagal cadence: IV – I

The subdominant moving to the tonic. It is final too, but softer and broader than V–I, because IV contains no leading note: there is no urgent pull, just a warm settling. It is called the **"Amen" cadence** because it is sung to "A-men" at the end of so many hymns. It often comes right after a perfect cadence, as a gentle extra ending. In rock and pop it is very common: IV–I endings feel relaxed and open-hearted.

## Imperfect cadence: ending on V

A phrase that ends **on** the dominant, coming from I, ii, IV or any other chord, ends with an **imperfect cadence** (American: **half cadence**). The music pauses on V, full of expectation, and you know more must follow. It is the musical question mark, or comma. This is the cadence at the end of the first phrase of a question-and-answer pair: the first phrase ends on V (the question), the second on I (the answer).

```figure
sound: "C4+E4+G4:h F4+A4+C5:h C4+E4+G4:h G3+B3+D4:w"
caption: I – IV – I – V: an imperfect cadence. The music waits on V.
```

## Interrupted cadence: V – vi

Here the music sets up a perfect cadence, everything leading to I, and then the dominant moves to **vi** instead. The bass rises a step instead of falling a 5th, and the expected home chord is replaced by its minor relative. The effect is a surprise, a turn, often poignant: "and they lived happily ever… *after all that*…". It is also called the **deceptive cadence**. Composers use it to extend a phrase: after the interruption, the music has to come round again to reach the real perfect cadence.

> **Perfect** V–I: full stop. **Plagal** IV–I: amen. **Imperfect** ending on V: comma or question. **Interrupted** V–vi: surprise, the music must go on.

## Cadences in minor

In a minor key the same four cadences exist: perfect V–i (with the raised 7th making V major), plagal iv–i, imperfect ending on V, and interrupted V–VI (in A minor, E to F). A special minor-key imperfect cadence, **iv⁶–V** (a minor IV in first inversion moving to V, the bass falling a semitone), is called the **Phrygian cadence**; it was a favourite of Baroque composers.

A delightful old trick is the **Picardy third**: a piece in minor ends on a **major** tonic chord. In A minor, the final chord is A major (A C♯ E) instead of A minor. It sounds like a sudden ray of light. Bach used it constantly.

## Hearing cadences

To recognise cadences by ear:

- Does the phrase sound **finished**? Then it ends on I: perfect (strong, with a pull) or plagal (soft, "amen").
- Does it sound **unfinished**? Then it is imperfect (ends on V: open, expectant, a major-sounding chord hanging) or interrupted (sounds like it was about to finish, then turned to a minor chord).

Listen to the bass: falling a 5th (or rising a 4th) to the tonic means perfect; rising a step from 5 to 6 means interrupted; from 4 to 1 means plagal.

## Cadences and form

Cadences are the joints of musical form. Phrases are defined by them, and the type of cadence tells you how a phrase relates to the next: an imperfect cadence asks for a reply; a perfect cadence closes a section; an interrupted cadence delays the close. When you analyse a piece, marking every cadence and its type is the fastest way to understand its shape.

## Common confusions

- **Imperfect means "badly done".** It means "incomplete", ending on V.
- **Any ending on V is perfect.** A perfect cadence ends **on I**, coming from V.
- **Interrupted cadence = wrong note.** It is a deliberate surprise that keeps the music going.

## In short

The four cadences are perfect (V–I, a full stop), plagal (IV–I, "Amen"), imperfect (ending on V, a comma or question) and interrupted (V–vi, a surprise that extends the phrase). Minor keys have the same, plus the Phrygian cadence and the Picardy third. Cadences are the joints of form; recognise them by whether the phrase sounds finished and by the bass movement.
