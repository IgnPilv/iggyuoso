## Why intervals matter

Sing the first two notes of *Twinkle Twinkle*: "Twin-kle, twin-kle". Now sing them starting from a different note. It is still clearly *Twinkle Twinkle*. What makes a tune recognisable is not the exact notes it uses, but the **distances** between them. Those distances are **intervals**, and they are the real building blocks of music: tunes are intervals one after another, chords are intervals stacked together.

You met the two smallest in the chapter on steps and skips. This chapter completes the set up to the octave, at least by their number. (A later chapter adds their exact size, "major", "minor" and "perfect".)

## Counting an interval

The rule is the same as before: count the letter names from the lower note to the upper one, **including both**.

- C to D: C, D = a **2nd**
- C to E: C, D, E = a **3rd**
- C to F: four letters = a **4th**
- C to G: five letters = a **5th**
- C to A: a **6th**
- C to B: a **7th**
- C to C: an **8th**, called an **octave**

The same note twice is a **unison**. Sharps and flats do not change the number: C to E♭ is still a 3rd, because it still spans C, D, E. They change the exact size, which is a story for later.

## Intervals on the staff

Each interval has its own shape on the staff, and learning to see them is a huge help in reading:

| Interval | On the staff |
| --- | --- |
| 2nd | line to the next space (or space to the next line): touching |
| 3rd | line to the next line, or space to the next space |
| 4th | line to space, with one line and one space between |
| 5th | line to the line two above, or space to the space two above |
| 6th | line to space, a wide gap |
| Octave | line to space, or space to line, very far apart |

A useful trick: **odd intervals (3rd, 5th, 7th) go line-to-line or space-to-space; even intervals (2nd, 4th, 6th, octave) go line-to-space or space-to-line.** If both notes are on lines, the interval must be a 3rd, 5th or 7th.

## Intervals under the hand

In a five-finger position each interval matches a pair of fingers. With the right thumb on C:

- Thumb to finger 2: a 2nd (C–D)
- Thumb to finger 3: a 3rd (C–E)
- Thumb to finger 4: a 4th (C–F)
- Thumb to finger 5: a 5th (C–G)

So the **5th is the span of your whole hand** in a five-finger position. An octave needs the hand stretched open, thumb and little finger, which adult hands can usually manage and children's often cannot.

This connection between fingers and intervals is why reading by interval works so well. See a 3rd on the staff, and the hand plays "skip a finger" without you naming the notes.

## Intervals by ear

Each interval has its own colour, and you can learn to recognise them. Many musicians use the opening of a familiar tune as a hook:

| Interval (going up) | Sounds like the start of... |
| --- | --- |
| 2nd | *Frère Jacques* ("Frè-re") or *Happy Birthday* ("Hap-py-birth") |
| 3rd | *Oh When the Saints* ("Oh when") |
| 4th | *Here Comes the Bride*, *Amazing Grace* |
| 5th | *Twinkle Twinkle* (between the first and second "twinkle") |
| 6th | *My Bonnie Lies Over the Ocean* ("My Bon-") |
| Octave | *Somewhere Over the Rainbow* ("Some-where") |

The ear branch trains this step by step, starting with simply telling a step from a leap, then hearing 3rds from 5ths, and only later the finer differences.

## Melodic and harmonic

When the two notes of an interval are played one after the other, as in a tune, it is a **melodic interval**. When they are played together, as in a chord, it is a **harmonic interval**. They are the same distance; the name only says how it is used. Some intervals sound smooth and restful played together (3rds, 6ths, octaves, 5ths); others sound tense and spiky (2nds, 7ths). That tension is not "wrong". It is the spice of harmony, as you will discover.

## Common confusions

- **Counting the gaps instead of the notes.** C to E is a 3rd: count C, D, E.
- **Thinking sharps change the number.** C to E♭ and C to E are both 3rds; they differ in size, not number.
- **Counting from the higher note.** You can, as long as you count all the letters between. The number is the same either way.

## In short

An interval is the distance between two notes, named by counting letters including both ends: unison, 2nd, 3rd, 4th, 5th, 6th, 7th, octave. Odd intervals go line-to-line or space-to-space; even ones line-to-space. In a five-finger position thumb to 5th finger spans a 5th. Melodic intervals are one after the other, harmonic intervals together, and each one has its own sound you can learn to hear.
