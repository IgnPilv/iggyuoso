## What a scale is

A **scale** is the set of notes a key uses, laid out in order from the home note up to the next home note an octave higher. The word comes from the Italian *scala*, a staircase or ladder, and that is a good picture: a scale climbs step by step, each note one letter higher than the last, using every letter exactly once.

Scales are not just finger exercises, though pianists do practise them every day. A scale is a summary of a key, the way an alphabet is a summary of a language. Tunes in C major are built almost entirely from the seven notes of the C major scale; chords in C major are built by stacking those notes. Knowing the scale of a key means knowing which notes belong.

## The pattern, again

You met the pattern in the chapter on keys: **tone, tone, semitone, tone, tone, tone, semitone** (T T S T T T S). Every major scale, starting on any of the twelve keys, follows it exactly. That is what makes them all sound "major": bright, stable, cheerful in a plain way.

## Two matching halves

Split the major scale in the middle and something neat appears. The bottom four notes, C D E F, go tone, tone, semitone. The top four notes, G A B C, also go tone, tone, semitone. And the two halves are joined by a tone (F to G).

Each four-note group is called a **tetrachord** (Greek for "four strings", from the ancient Greek lyre). So a major scale is two identical tetrachords, T T S, joined by a tone. This is a lovely shortcut for working out scales: the top half of one major scale is the bottom half of another. The upper tetrachord of C major, G A B C, is the lower tetrachord of G major, which then needs its own upper half, D E F♯ G, and there is your F sharp.

```figure

caption: Two halves with the same shape, T T S, joined by a tone in the middle.
```

> **Major scale = T T S + T + T T S.** Two matching four-note halves joined by a tone. The semitones are always between degrees 3–4 and 7–8.

## Degrees: every note has a number

Because every major scale has the same shape, it is useful to number the notes from the bottom: the home note is **1**, the next **2**, and so on up to **7**, with 8 being 1 again. These numbers are called **scale degrees** and are often written with a little hat: 1̂, 2̂, 3̂. In C major, 1 is C, 5 is G, 7 is B. In G major, 1 is G, 5 is D, 7 is F♯.

The numbers matter because each degree has the same **feeling** in every major key:

- **1** feels at rest, the place everything comes home to.
- **3** and **5** feel stable too; with 1 they make the tonic chord.
- **7** is a semitone below 1 and pulls up towards it very strongly. It is called the **leading note**, because it leads home.
- **4** sits a semitone above 3 and tends to fall to it.
- **2** and **6** are restless and tend to move by step to their neighbours.

Play C major up to B and stop. You can feel B leaning on the door of C. That pull is the reason the G scale needed F sharp: without a leading note a semitone below home, the key loses its grip. A later chapter gives each degree its traditional name (tonic, dominant and so on) and explores these pulls in detail.

## Fingering the scale

A scale has eight notes and you have five fingers, so the hand has to move. Pianists do this by passing the **thumb under** the hand (going up in the right hand) and **crossing a finger over the thumb** (coming down). For C, G, D, A and E major the standard right-hand fingering is **1 2 3 1 2 3 4 5**: thumb, two, three, thumb tucks under onto the fourth note, then up to five. The left hand going up is the mirror: **5 4 3 2 1 3 2 1**.

```figure
keys: { notes: "C4 D4 E4 F4 G4 A4 B4 C5", labels: fingers, fingers: "1 2 3 1 2 3 4 5" }
caption: Right hand, C major: the thumb passes under after finger 3, onto F.
```

Notice that the thumb passes under onto **F**, the note right after the semitone E–F. Fingering follows the shape of the scale. In keys with black keys the fingering changes so that the thumb never has to land on a black key; the technique branch teaches each key's fingering as it comes.

## Why practise scales?

Scales are the daily bread of piano practice for good reasons. They train evenness (every note the same length and loudness), the thumb movement that almost all real music needs, and the geography of each key, so that when a piece in E major comes along, your hand already knows where the four sharps are. And as you learn more theory, the scale becomes a map you can hang chords, intervals and melodies on.

## Common confusions

- **A scale must start on C.** Any note can be the start; the pattern is what makes it major.
- **Eight different notes.** Seven: the eighth is the first note again, an octave up.
- **Scales are only finger exercises.** They are maps of keys, and the source of nearly every tune and chord in that key.

## In short

A scale is a key's notes in order, from home to home. The major scale follows T T S T T T S and is made of two matching halves joined by a tone. Its degrees, 1 to 7, have the same feelings in every key: 1 rests, 7 leads home, 4 falls to 3. Pianists finger scales by passing the thumb under, and practise them to train evenness and know every key by hand.
