## Punctuation in music

Spoken language has punctuation: commas where you pause briefly, full stops where a thought ends. Music has the same thing, made of chords. At the end of a phrase, the harmony settles in a way that says "pause here" or "this is finished". These musical punctuation marks are called **cadences**, from the Latin *cadere*, "to fall", because the music seems to come down to rest.

A cadence is defined by its **last two chords**. There are four main kinds, and you will meet all of them in a later chapter. This chapter is about the most important one, the one that ends nearly every classical piece and most songs: the **perfect cadence**.

## V – I: the full stop

The **perfect cadence** (called the **authentic cadence** in American books) is the dominant chord moving to the tonic: **V – I**. In C major, G to C.

```figure

sound: "C4+E4+G4:h F4+A4+C5:h G3+B3+D4:h C3+C4+E4:w"
caption: I – IV – V – I, ending with the perfect cadence, V – I.
```

Why does it sound so final? Everything you learned in the last chapter comes together here:

- The **root** of the chord falls a 5th (or rises a 4th) from G to C, the strongest bass move there is.
- The **leading note** (B) rises a semitone to the tonic.
- The tension of V, the most unstable primary chord, is released into I, the most stable.

Adding the 7th to the dominant, **V7 – I**, makes it even stronger: the 7th (F in G7) falls by a semitone to the 3rd of the tonic chord (E). Two semitone pulls resolving at once.

> **Perfect cadence: V – I** (or V7 – I). Music's full stop. The bass falls a 5th from dominant to tonic and the leading note rises to the tonic.

## Making it sound really final

Not every V – I sounds equally final. The strongest, most conclusive version, sometimes called a **perfect authentic cadence**, has three features:

1. Both chords in **root position** (the roots G and C in the bass).
2. The **tonic** (C) as the **top note** of the final chord, in the melody.
3. The final chord on a **strong beat**.

If the melody ends on E or G instead, or the chords are inverted, the cadence still works but sounds a little less closed, like a full stop followed by "and yet...". Composers use the strongest version at the very end of a piece and lighter versions inside it.

## Voicing it smoothly

When you play a cadence with chords in the right hand and the bass in the left, use what you learned about inversions: keep common notes, move the rest by step. In C major, a smooth right-hand voicing of **I – IV – V7 – I** is:

| Chord | Left hand (bass) | Right hand |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I (C) | C | C E G |
| IV (F) | F | C F A |
| V7 (G7) | G | B F G (or B D F) |
| I (C) | C | C E G |

```figure

sound: "C3+C4+E4+G4:q F3+C4+F4+A4:q G3+B3+F4+G4:q C3+C4+E4+G4:w"
caption: I – IV – V7 – I with a smooth right hand. The hand barely moves; the bass jumps between roots.
```

This shape is one of the most useful things a pianist can know. It works in every key: just move the whole pattern. The technique branch drills it in key after key until your hands know it without thinking.

## Cadences in minor

In a minor key the perfect cadence is **V – i**, and here the raised 7th of the harmonic minor is essential. In A minor, the dominant chord must be **E major** (E G♯ B), not E minor. Without the G♯ there is no leading note, and the cadence loses its pull. That is the real reason the harmonic minor exists.


## Hearing cadences

A perfect cadence sounds like an ending, a door closing, a full stop. You have heard thousands of them: at the end of every hymn, most classical pieces, and the final chord of countless songs. When you listen to music, try to notice where the phrases end and whether the ending sounds final. That habit, hearing the punctuation, will help you understand the shape of any piece you play.

## Common confusions

- **"Perfect" means "without mistakes".** It is just the name of the V – I cadence (from the Latin for "complete").
- **Any ending on I is a perfect cadence.** It must come from V. IV – I is a different cadence (the plagal cadence, "Amen").
- **Minor V is minor.** For a cadence in minor, V needs the raised 7th: major.

## In short

Cadences are musical punctuation, defined by the last two chords of a phrase. The perfect cadence, V – I (or V7 – I), is the full stop: the bass falls a 5th and the leading note rises to the tonic. It is strongest in root position with the tonic on top on a strong beat. Voice it smoothly by keeping common notes. In minor it is V – i, with a major V thanks to the raised 7th.
