## Two more words for intervals

You already know four interval qualities: **perfect** (unisons, 4ths, 5ths, octaves), and **major** and **minor** (2nds, 3rds, 6ths, 7ths). Two more complete the set:

- **Augmented** means one semitone **larger** than major or perfect.
- **Diminished** means one semitone **smaller** than minor or perfect.

The letter distance (the number) stays the same; only the size in semitones changes. So C to G is a perfect 5th (7 semitones); C to G♯ is an **augmented 5th** (8 semitones); C to G♭ is a **diminished 5th** (6 semitones).

## The tritone

Look at the last two intervals in the picture. C to G♭ is a diminished 5th; C to F♯ is an augmented 4th. On the keyboard they are the same keys, six semitones apart: exactly half an octave. That interval is called the **tritone**, because it spans three whole tones (C–D–E–F♯).

The tritone is the most unstable interval in Western music. It divides the octave exactly in half, so it has no sense of which note is "home". Medieval theorists called it *diabolus in musica*, "the devil in music", and avoided it. Later composers made it the engine of harmony: you met it inside the dominant 7th, between the leading note and degree 4 (B and F in C major), where it resolves inward to the tonic chord. Heard on its own, the tritone sounds tense, mysterious, alarmed. Think of the start of *Maria* from *West Side Story* (an augmented 4th that resolves upward), or the siren-like opening of Jimi Hendrix's *Purple Haze*.

> **Augmented** = a semitone bigger than major or perfect. **Diminished** = a semitone smaller than minor or perfect. The **tritone** (augmented 4th = diminished 5th) is six semitones, half an octave, the most restless interval.

## Spelling matters

Why two names for one sound? Because the spelling shows where the interval is going. **C–F♯**, an augmented 4th, tends to **open outward**: F♯ rises to G, C falls to B (in G major). **C–G♭**, a diminished 5th, tends to **close inward**: G♭ falls to F, C rises to D♭. Same keys, opposite directions. Other enharmonic pairs work the same way: an augmented 2nd (C–D♯) sounds like a minor 3rd (C–E♭), but you count letters to tell them apart, and the augmented 2nd is the exotic leap in the harmonic minor scale (F–G♯ in A minor).

## The four triads

Stacking 3rds can give four kinds of triad, depending on which 3rds are used:

| Triad | Bottom 3rd | Top 3rd | Outer 5th | Example | Sound |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Major | major (4) | minor (3) | perfect | C E G | bright, stable |
| Minor | minor (3) | major (4) | perfect | C E♭ G | dark, stable |
| Diminished | minor (3) | minor (3) | diminished | C E♭ G♭ | tense, cramped |
| Augmented | major (4) | major (4) | augmented | C E G♯ | floating, unresolved |

Major and minor triads have a perfect 5th, which makes them stable: they can end a piece. Diminished and augmented triads don't, and they always sound as if they must move on.

### The diminished triad

It is two minor 3rds, and it occurs naturally in every major key on **degree 7** (B D F in C major, the **vii°** chord) and in minor keys on degree 2 (B D F in A minor, **ii°**). It contains the tritone, so it behaves like a dominant 7th without its root: B D F is G7 with no G. That is why vii° resolves to I just as V7 does. Stack one more minor 3rd and you get the **diminished 7th** (B D F A♭), a favourite of dramatic music, from Bach's organ toccatas to silent-film villains.

### The augmented triad

It is two major 3rds. It does not occur naturally in major keys (it appears on degree 3 of the harmonic minor: C E G♯ in A minor). Because its three notes divide the octave into equal parts, it has no root that sounds like home and seems to float. Composers use it as a passing colour, often by raising the 5th of a major chord (C – C+ – F, where G slides to G♯ and then to A). Debussy loved its dreamlike quality.

## Hearing the four triads

- **Major**: open and bright.
- **Minor**: darker, sad or serious, but at rest.
- **Diminished**: squeezed, nervous; it wants to collapse inward.
- **Augmented**: stretched, eerie, as if suspended in the air; it wants to expand.

A tip from ear training: listen to the top note. In major and minor it sits comfortably; in diminished it seems too low, in augmented too high.

## Common confusions

- **Diminished means minor.** Diminished is a step smaller still, a semitone below minor (or below perfect).
- **Augmented 4th and diminished 5th are different sounds.** They are the same sound; the spelling shows the direction.
- **Every triad can end a piece.** Only major and minor triads are stable.

## In short

Augmented intervals are a semitone larger than major or perfect; diminished ones a semitone smaller than minor or perfect. The tritone, half an octave, is both an augmented 4th and a diminished 5th, and drives the pull of the dominant. With these words we can name all four triads: major and minor (stable, with a perfect 5th), diminished (two minor 3rds, on degree 7, acts like V7) and augmented (two major 3rds, floating, used as a passing colour).
