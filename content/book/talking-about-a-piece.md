## From reading notes to reading music

By now you know a surprising amount of music theory: keys and scales, meters, chords and Roman numerals, cadences, form, texture, modulation, the eras. Each of these was a separate chapter. This chapter puts them together into one skill: **analysis**, describing a piece of music in a few clear strokes, the way a teacher, a programme note or a fellow musician would.

Analysis is not an academic extra. It is one of the best practice tools there is. A piece you understand, "it's in ternary form, the middle section goes to the relative minor, the last phrase is the first one with a different cadence", is a piece you can learn in half the time and remember for years, because you are remembering a handful of ideas instead of hundreds of separate notes. Experienced players analyse almost without thinking every time they open a new score.

## Eight questions

> **Key · meter · form · modulation · cadences · texture · era · character**

Ask them roughly in this order. The first ones you can answer from the first line; the later ones need you to look through the whole piece, or to listen.

### 1. Key

Look at the key signature, then at the first and last bass notes and chords. The signature gives two candidates (a major key and its relative minor); the opening and closing tonic decide. Watch for the raised 7th: G♯s in a piece with no signature mean A minor.

### 2. Meter

Read the time signature, and then feel it: is it simple (beats divide in two) or compound (in three)? Is there an upbeat? What is the main note value?

### 3. Form

Look for repeats, double bar lines, and for music that comes back. Label each section with a letter: a new idea gets a new letter, a return keeps its letter.

| Form | Plan | Typical in |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Binary | A B (each half often repeated) | Baroque dances, minuets |
| Ternary | A B A | songs, nocturnes, minuet and trio |
| Rondo | A B A C A | last movements of sonatas |
| Theme and variations | A A¹ A² A³ … | sets of variations |
| Strophic | the same music for each verse | folk songs, hymns |
| Sonata form | exposition – development – recapitulation | first movements of sonatas |

Also look at the phrase level: are the phrases four bars? Do they come in question-and-answer pairs? Is there a **coda** (a closing tag after the last section)?

### 4. Modulation

Scan for accidentals that keep returning and for cadences on new tonics. Where does the music go, and where does it come back home? In a binary-form Baroque dance, the music usually visits the dominant (or the relative major, if the piece is minor) around the middle, often right at the end of the first half, and the second half finds its way back.

### 5. Cadences

Mark the end of every phrase and name its cadence: perfect, plagal, imperfect, interrupted. The pattern of cadences is the skeleton of the piece.

### 6. Texture

Is it melody and accompaniment (homophony)? Several independent lines (polyphony)? Chords moving together (chordal)? Does the texture change between sections? Which hand has the melody?

### 7. Era and style

Using what you know of the four eras: what does the texture, the harmony, the rhythm suggest? Who wrote it, and when? Even when the composer is unknown, you can usually tell whether a piece belongs to the Baroque, Classical, Romantic or Modern world.

### 8. Character

What is the tempo marking and its meaning (*Allegro*, *Andante*, *Moderato*, *Largo*…)? What mood words are there (*dolce*, *grazioso*, *cantabile*, *con brio*)? What are the dynamics like? Then add your own description: graceful, stormy, playful, lonely. Character is what you are ultimately trying to communicate when you play.

## A worked example: the Minuet in G

You know this piece. Let's describe it.

- **Key**: G major (one sharp; starts and ends on G).
- **Meter**: 3/4, a dance in three, mainly quarter and eighth notes.
- **Form**: binary. Two halves of sixteen bars, each repeated. Phrases of four bars, paired as question and answer. (The app's lessons teach the first half.)
- **Modulation**: the first half stays in G. The second half moves to **D major**, the dominant (C♯s appear, leading up to D), closes there halfway through, and then returns to G.
- **Cadences**: an imperfect cadence at bar 8 (the melody rests on A, over a D chord), a perfect cadence in G at bar 16, a perfect cadence in D in the middle of the second half, and a perfect cadence in G at the end.
- **Texture**: two parts, melody in the right hand and a simple bass line in the left, with some independence between them: early counterpoint.
- **Era**: Baroque (by Christian Petzold, about 1725, long credited to J. S. Bach; it is in the notebook Bach kept for his wife Anna Magdalena).
- **Character**: *Moderato*, graceful and light, a dance: a clear pulse with a lilt on beat one.

## Using analysis in practice

1. **Before you play a note**, spend two minutes with the score answering the eight questions, even roughly.
2. **Mark the score** (or a copy): section letters, cadences, the places where it modulates.
3. **Practise by sections** and by phrases, not from the beginning every time. Notice which sections are repeats or near-repeats, and learn them once.
4. **Shape your playing** from the analysis: breathe at cadences, give the return of A a sense of homecoming, bring out the modulation, keep the texture's melody on top.
5. **Memorise by structure**: "A in G, B in E minor, A again with a coda" is much easier to recall under pressure than the notes alone.

## Common confusions

- **Analysis is only for composers and scholars.** It is a practice tool for every player.
- **You need to label every chord.** The big picture (form, keys, cadences) matters most; detail can come later.
- **There's one right answer.** Musicians often disagree on the finer points; what matters is that the description helps you understand and play.

## In short

To describe any piece, ask eight questions: key, meter, form, modulation, cadences, texture, era and character. The answers turn a page of notes into a few ideas, which makes the piece faster to learn, easier to memorise and clearer to perform. Practise by describing every new piece before you play it, as with the Minuet in G: G major, 3/4, binary, a trip to D and back, two-part Baroque texture, graceful.
