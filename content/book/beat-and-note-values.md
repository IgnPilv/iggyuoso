## The beat you already feel

Put on any song you like and you will find your foot tapping, or your head nodding, without deciding to. What you are tapping along to is the **beat**, also called the **pulse**: a steady, even "tick" underneath the music, like a heartbeat or a clock.

The beat is not the same as the tune, and it is not the same as the rhythm of the words. A singer may hold one word for a long time and then rush through five short ones, but the beat underneath stays steady the whole time. Being able to feel that steady pulse, and keep it going on your own, is the foundation of all rhythm. The exercises that ask you to tap along with the click are training exactly this.

> **The beat** is the steady pulse under the music. **Rhythm** is the pattern of long and short sounds laid over it.

How fast the beat goes is called the **tempo**. It is measured in **beats per minute** (bpm): 60 bpm is one beat a second, like a slow clock; 120 bpm is two a second, a brisk walk. The click in this app is a **metronome**, a device that ticks at a chosen tempo, invented in its familiar form about two hundred years ago and still used by every serious musician.

## Measuring notes in beats

Written music tells you how long to hold each note by its **shape**. The shapes are named after how they relate to each other, and in the most common kind of music each one lasts a set number of beats:

| Note | Looks like | Beats |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Whole note | an open oval, no stem | 4 |
| Half note | an open oval with a stem | 2 |
| Quarter note | a filled-in oval with a stem | 1 |

```figure
caption: One whole note, two half notes, four quarter notes. Each bar lasts the same four beats.
```

The names explain themselves: a half note is half of a whole note, and a quarter note is a quarter of one. Two halves make a whole; two quarters make a half; four quarters make a whole. Later you will meet eighth notes and sixteenth notes, which carry on halving in the same way.

British books use different names for the same shapes: a whole note is a **semibreve**, a half note a **minim**, and a quarter note a **crotchet**. You may see these in older books. They mean exactly the same thing.

## Holding notes for their full length

The most common rhythm mistake at every level is not playing too early or too late, but **letting go too soon**. When you see a half note, the sound must last through two full beats; a whole note, through four. Count while you hold: "one, two" for a half note, "one, two, three, four" for a whole note. Do not lift until the count tells you.

This app checks this for you: long notes released well before the end lose part of their score, and the feedback says "short holds" so you know which habit slipped.

## Silence counts too: rests

Music is not only sounds; it is also the gaps between them. A silence of a measured length is called a **rest**, and every note value has a matching rest:

| Rest | Looks like | Beats of silence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Whole rest | a small block hanging below a line | 4 (or a whole empty bar) |
| Half rest | a small block sitting on a line | 2 |
| Quarter rest | a squiggle, like a zig-zag | 1 |

```figure
staff: { notes: "C5:q r:q C5:q r:q | C5:h r:h | r:w", meter: 4/4 }
caption: Quarter rests, a half rest, and a whole rest filling a bar.
```

A trick for telling the whole and half rest apart: the whole rest is heavier, so it *hangs down* from the line, like a hole; the half rest is lighter and *sits* on the line like a hat.

A rest is not a pause where the music stops. The beat goes on through it, and you must keep counting. Many beginners treat a rest as a breather and come back in late. Count it exactly like a note: "one, two" for a half rest, and lift your hands off the keys so the silence is real.

## Rhythm grows from the beat

Everything later in rhythm, every dotted note, triplet, syncopation and odd meter, is built on top of the steady beat you are learning now. If the beat is shaky, nothing on top of it can be steady. That is why the first rhythm exercises can feel almost too simple. They are not wasted time; they are the foundation, and every professional musician still practises with a metronome.

## Common confusions

- **Speeding up on easy notes and slowing down on hard ones.** The beat must stay the same throughout. If a passage is hard, slow the whole thing down, which is what the tempo ladder in this app is for.
- **Counting the rest as nothing.** A rest has a length, just like a note.
- **Quarter means "a quarter of a second".** No: it means a quarter of a whole note. How long it lasts in seconds depends on the tempo.

## In short

The beat is the steady pulse under the music; tempo is how fast it goes. A whole note lasts four beats, a half note two, a quarter note one, and every note has a matching rest of the same length. Hold every note for its full value, keep counting through rests, and never let the beat wobble.
