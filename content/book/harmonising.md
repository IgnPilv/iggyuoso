## From tune to song

Being able to put chords under a melody, called **harmonising**, is one of the most practical skills a pianist can have. It lets you accompany a singer from a melody line, play songs from a lead sheet, and make your own arrangements. It sounds advanced, but with the primary chords and a simple method you can harmonise most folk tunes, carols and children's songs quickly.

## The basic principle: chord notes in the melody

A chord fits a melody note when the note **belongs to the chord**. In C major:

| Melody note | Belongs to |
| --- | --- |
| C | I (C E G) and IV (F A C) |
| D | V (G B D) |
| E | I |
| F | IV (and V7) |
| G | I and V |
| A | IV |
| B | V |

So the first question for each part of the melody is: which chord contains these notes? Because I, IV and V between them contain every note of the scale, there is always at least one answer.

## Strong beats matter most

You do not need a new chord for every note. Usually one chord lasts a whole bar or half a bar. Choose it by the notes on the **strong beats**, especially beat 1. Notes in between that do not belong to the chord, if they move by step, are **passing notes** or **neighbour notes** and are perfectly fine; they decorate the melody without needing their own chord.

For example, a bar of melody E F G E in C major: beats 1 and 3 have E and G, both in the C chord, while F is a passing note between them. So the whole bar takes **I**.

> **To harmonise**: for each bar (or half bar), look at the melody notes on the strong beats, and choose the chord that contains them. Notes moving by step between chord notes can be passing notes.

## Cadences decide the endings

The ends of phrases need special care, because that is where cadences go:

- The **last phrase** should end **V – I** (a perfect cadence), with the melody usually on the tonic.
- A phrase that sounds like a **question** often ends on **V** (a half cadence): the melody might rest on D, B or G.
- Most songs **start** on I.

Working from these anchors (start on I, end each phrase with a cadence) and filling in the middle by the strong-beat rule is how most people harmonise. If two chords would both fit, try each and listen; often one sounds clearly better, or a nice pattern appears (I – IV – V – I).

## From chords to accompaniment

Once you have chosen the chords, you need to decide **how** to play them. A block chord held for a whole bar is fine but can sound static. Pianists use **accompaniment patterns** that keep the harmony moving in rhythm. A few classic ones, for the left hand:

1. **Block chords**: the whole chord, struck together, once a bar or on each beat.
2. **Root and fifth**: just the root and fifth (C and G), which sounds open and strong. Great for folk and rock.
3. **Broken chord**: the chord notes one after another, for example C G E G, rocking up and down.
4. **Waltz (oom-pah-pah)**: in 3/4, the bass note on beat 1, then the rest of the chord on beats 2 and 3.
5. **Alberti bass**: low–high–middle–high (C G E G), named after Domenico Alberti, who used it constantly in the 1700s. It is everywhere in Mozart and Haydn.
6. **Stride or oom-pah**: bass note on the beat, chord on the off-beat.

Notice the last figure: the G chord is played with **B** at the bottom (first inversion), so the lowest notes go C → B → C, a smooth step instead of a jump. Using inversions in accompaniment patterns, as you learned in the chapter on inversions, keeps everything smooth.

## Matching the pattern to the music

The pattern should fit the style and mood. A gentle lullaby wants slow broken chords; a march wants root-and-fifth or oom-pah; a waltz wants oom-pah-pah; a classical sonatina wants Alberti bass; a ballad might want flowing arpeggios with the pedal. The accompaniment should support the melody, never drown it: play it a level softer than the tune.

## Common confusions

- **A chord for every note.** Usually one chord per bar or half bar; passing notes need no chord of their own.
- **Ignoring cadences.** Phrase endings should be V – I (finished) or end on V (question).
- **Accompaniment as loud as the tune.** Keep it a dynamic level below.

## In short

To harmonise a melody, choose for each bar the chord containing its strong-beat notes; step-wise notes in between are passing notes. Start on I and end phrases with cadences, V – I for full stops, V for questions. Then turn the chords into a pattern, block chords, root and fifth, broken chords, waltz or Alberti bass, using inversions to keep it smooth, and keep it softer than the tune.
