## Same tune, new height

Sing *Happy Birthday* at a party and someone always starts too high, and everyone strains at "dear so-and-so". The fix is to start lower. The tune is the same; you have just moved every note down by the same amount. Moving a piece of music up or down, keeping all its intervals and therefore its shape, is called **transposing** (from Latin, "to place across").

Musicians transpose all the time: to suit a singer's range, to suit an instrument, to make a passage easier to play, or simply to understand a tune as a pattern rather than as particular notes.

## Two ways to transpose

### By interval

Move **every note by the same interval**. To transpose *Frère Jacques* from C major up a major 2nd to D major, move every note up a major 2nd: C→D, D→E, E→F♯, G→A, and so on. The key signature changes from C major (none) to D major (two sharps).

This is precise, but it is easy to slip, because the *quality* of each interval must stay exactly the same. E up a major 2nd is F♯, not F. That is why it is usually easier to use the second method.

### By scale degree

Think of each note as a **scale degree** in the old key, and play the same degree in the new key. *Frère Jacques* starts 1 2 3 1. In D major, 1 2 3 1 is D E F♯ D. You do not need to work out any intervals: the new key's scale does it for you, sharps and flats included.

> **Transposing by degree**: turn the melody into scale-degree numbers (1 2 3 1...), then play the same numbers in the new key. The key signature takes care of the sharps and flats.

This method also explains why transposing is possible at all: every major key has the same pattern of steps, so the same degrees make the same tune.

## Transposing on paper

To write a transposed version:

1. **Find the new key**, by moving the old tonic by the chosen interval (C up a perfect 4th = F).
2. **Write the new key signature.**
3. **Move every note** by the same number of lines and spaces (a 4th = up three steps on the staff: line to space with one line and one space between).
4. **Check accidentals.** Notes that were inside the old key will automatically be right in the new key thanks to the signature. Accidentals in the original (chromatic notes) must be transposed by the same interval, carefully: a raised note stays raised relative to the new key. In C, an F♯ (raised 4th degree) becomes, in F, a B♮ (raised 4th degree of F, cancelling the B♭).

## Transposing chords

Chords transpose just as easily by Roman numerals. A song that goes C – Am – F – G in C major is **I – vi – IV – V**. In G major, I – vi – IV – V is G – Em – C – D. In E♭ major, E♭ – Cm – A♭ – B♭. If you know the numerals and the key, you can play any progression anywhere. This is how session musicians work: "It's a one-six-four-five in E flat."

```figure

  ask: "A tune in C major starts C E G E. Transpose it to F major. What are the first four notes?"
  choices: ["F A C A", "F A B♭ A", "F G A G"]
  correct: 0
  why: "C E G E is degrees **1 3 5 3**. In F major, 1 3 5 3 is **F A C A**."
```

## Transposing instruments

You may hear that some instruments are "in B♭" or "in F". Trumpets, clarinets, saxophones and horns are **transposing instruments**: their music is written in a different key from how it sounds, so that the same fingering always matches the same written note across the family. When a B♭ trumpet plays a written C, you hear a B♭. Pianists rarely deal with this, except when accompanying such players, but it is a good example of how basic transposition is in a musician's life.

## Transposing at the keyboard

Transposing as you play is a skill worth practising from the start. Begin with five-finger tunes: play a tune in C position, then move your hand to G position and play the same finger pattern. In a five-finger position, the fingers carry the scale degrees (thumb = 1), so the shape transfers directly, with any black keys the new key needs. The transposing lessons ask you to do exactly this, then gradually with longer tunes and both hands.

## Common confusions

- **Moving every note by the same number of keys** (semitones) works, but you must re-spell with the new key; by degree is easier.
- **Forgetting the new key signature.** Write or think it first.
- **Transposing accidentals carelessly.** Move them by the same interval as everything else.

## In short

Transposing moves music to a new key, keeping its intervals and shape. Do it by interval (every note moved the same amount) or, more easily, by scale degree (same numbers in the new key). On paper: find the new key, write its signature, move every note, and transpose accidentals by the same interval. Chord progressions transpose through Roman numerals.
