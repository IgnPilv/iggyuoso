## The notes are not the whole message

Two pianists can play exactly the same notes, at exactly the right times, and one performance is moving while the other is dull. Much of the difference lies in things the notes alone do not show: how fast, how smoothly or crisply, how loud, with what mood. Composers give these instructions with words and small signs written around the notes. Learning them is like learning the stage directions in a play.

Most of these words are **Italian**, because in the 1600s and 1700s, when the habit of writing them down took hold, Italy was the centre of European music. Musicians everywhere adopted the Italian terms, and they are still used worldwide, even by English, German and Russian composers.

## Tempo words

The speed of the beat is the **tempo**, and it is usually given by an Italian word at the start of a piece, above the staff. Often a metronome mark follows, like ♩ = 96, meaning 96 quarter-note beats per minute.

| Word | Meaning | Rough speed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Largo | broad, very slow | 40–60 |
| Adagio | slow, at ease | 55–75 |
| Andante | at a walking pace | 75–105 |
| Moderato | moderate | 95–120 |
| Allegretto | fairly quick | 105–125 |
| Allegro | quick, lively (literally "cheerful") | 115–150 |
| Presto | very fast | 165 and up |

These are not exact numbers: an *Allegro* by Mozart and an *Allegro* by a modern composer may differ a lot. Notice also that several words describe a **mood** as much as a speed: *allegro* means cheerful, *andante* means walking. That is a clue to how to play, not only how fast.

Some words change the tempo along the way:

- **ritardando** (*rit.*) or **rallentando** (*rall.*): gradually slowing down, very common at the end of a piece.
- **accelerando** (*accel.*): gradually speeding up.
- **a tempo**: back to the original speed after a change.
- A **fermata** (𝄐, a dot under an arch over a note): hold this note longer than written, as long as feels right, then carry on.

## Touch: how notes connect

**Articulation** is how notes begin, end and join together. It is to music what pronunciation is to speech: the same words can be said smoothly, crisply or with emphasis.

- **Legato** (Italian "tied together"): smooth and connected, with no gap between notes. On the piano, one finger lets go of its key exactly as the next finger goes down, like walking, where one foot leaves the ground as the other lands. Legato is shown by a curved line over or under a group of notes, called a **slur**.
- **Non legato**: notes slightly separated, each one clear, but not short. This is the normal "default" touch in much music when nothing is marked.
- **Staccato** (Italian "detached"): short and crisp, with silence after each note. Shown by a **dot above or below the note head**. The note is released quickly, roughly half its written value, but the *time* it takes up stays the same; the rest of the beat is silence.

A slur is different from a **tie**, though both are curved lines. A tie joins two notes of the **same pitch** into one held sound. A slur joins **different** notes and means "play these smoothly". If the notes under the curve are different, it is a slur.

> **Legato** = smooth (slur). **Staccato** = short (dot over or under the note). A **tie** joins the same note into one long sound; a **slur** joins different notes smoothly.

## Accents and other signs

- **Accent** (>, a little wedge above or below a note): play this note louder than those around it.
- **Tenuto** (a short line above or below a note): hold it for its full value, perhaps leaning on it slightly.
- **sforzando** (*sf* or *sfz*): a sudden strong accent on one note or chord.

## Character words

Composers often add words describing the mood, sometimes alongside the tempo: *Allegro con brio* (quick, with spirit), *Andante cantabile* (walking pace, singing).

| Word | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| cantabile | singing, the melody flowing like a voice |
| dolce | sweetly, gently |
| espressivo | expressively, with feeling |
| grazioso | gracefully |
| maestoso | majestically |
| leggiero | lightly |
| con moto | with movement, not dragging |
| molto / poco | very / a little (*molto rit.*, *poco cresc.*) |

## Why it matters at the piano

Expression is not decoration for later, once the notes are learned. It changes how you practise from the start. A staccato passage needs a light, bouncing hand; a legato tune needs fingers that connect without gaps; *dolce* asks for slower keys and a softer touch. If you learn the notes first without the articulation, you will have to relearn them. So read the words and signs as part of the music from the very first time you play a piece.

Release timing is not scored in every exercise yet, but your ear can judge it. Listen: is there a gap between legato notes? Do staccato notes really stop short?

## Common confusions

- **Staccato means faster.** No: shorter notes, same beat.
- **Allegro means loud.** No: quick and cheerful. Loudness has its own marks.
- **Slur or tie?** Same pitch: tie. Different pitches: slur.

## In short

Composers mark tempo, touch and mood with mostly Italian words and small signs. Tempo runs from Largo to Presto, with rit., accel., a tempo and the fermata to bend it. Legato (slurs) connects notes, staccato (dots) shortens them, accents stress them. Character words like dolce and cantabile describe the mood. Read them from the first time you play.
