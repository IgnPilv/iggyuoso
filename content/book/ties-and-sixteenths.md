## Ties: one sound, two written notes

Sometimes a note needs to last longer than any single note value can show, or it needs to carry on across a bar line, where one bar ends and the next begins. For this music uses a **tie**: a curved line joining two notes of **the same pitch**. Tied notes are played as **one** sound: strike the first note, and hold it through the value of the second without playing again.

```figure
staff: { notes: "E4:h G4:h~ | G4:q F4:q E4:h", meter: 4/4, labels: names }
sound: "E4:h G4:h. F4:q E4:h"
caption: The G is tied across the bar line: play it once and hold it for three beats.
```

The tie is necessary because a bar must add up to its time signature: you cannot write a three-beat note that starts on beat 3 of a 4/4 bar without spilling into the next bar. So you write a half note at the end of one bar, tied to a quarter note at the start of the next. On the page, the tie curves from one note head to the other, away from the stems.

> **A tie** joins two notes of the **same pitch** into one sound: play the first, hold through the second. A curve over **different** notes is a **slur** (play smoothly), not a tie.

Counting tied notes is straightforward: keep counting through the tied note exactly as if it were played, but do not play. The common mistake is to strike the second note again. The second mistake is to forget that the bar line has passed, and lose count. Count out loud across the bar line: "three, four, **one** (hold), two".

## Sixteenth notes

Halve an eighth note and you get a **sixteenth note** (British: **semiquaver**). Four of them fill one quarter-note beat. A sixteenth note has **two flags**, and groups of them are joined by **two beams**. The matching sixteenth rest has two little flags too.

To count sixteenths, each beat needs four syllables. The standard count is:

**1 e + a 2 e + a**, said "one-ee-and-uh, two-ee-and-uh".

The numbers are on the beat, "+" is halfway, and "e" and "a" fill the quarters in between.

```figure
sound: "C5:q C5:s C5:s C5:s C5:s C5:e C5:e C5:s C5:s C5:s C5:s C5:e C5:s C5:s C5:s C5:s C5:e C5:h"
caption: Quarters, eighths and sixteenths together. Count "1 (e + a) 2 e + a 3 (e) + (a) 4 e + a".
```

## Common sixteenth patterns

Four sixteenths in a row are easy to count. More often, sixteenths combine with eighths inside one beat, and these patterns are worth learning as shapes you recognise at a glance:

| Pattern (one beat) | Count | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| four sixteenths | 1 e + a | "gal-lo-ping-ly" |
| eighth + two sixteenths | 1 (e) + a | "gal-lop-ing" (long short short) |
| two sixteenths + eighth | 1 e + (a) | "run-ning-home" (short short long) |
| dotted eighth + sixteenth | 1 (e +) a | a skipping, jumpy rhythm |

```figure
staff: { notes: "G4:e G4:s G4:s G4:s G4:s G4:e G4:e. G4:s G4:q", meter: 2/4 }
sound: "G4:e G4:s G4:s G4:s G4:s G4:e G4:e. G4:s G4:q"
caption: "Gallop", then "running home", then the skipping dotted eighth and sixteenth, then a quarter.
```

The **dotted eighth and sixteenth** works just like the dotted quarter and eighth you learned before, only twice as fast: the dotted eighth takes three of the four sixteenth slots ("1 e +") and the sixteenth falls on "a", just before the next beat. Played well it skips; played lazily it turns into a limp triplet. Count it carefully at a slow tempo first.

## Cut time

You have seen 2/4, 3/4 and 4/4, where the quarter note is the beat. **Cut time**, written **2/2** or as a **C with a vertical line through it** (₵), has **two half-note beats** per bar. A bar of 2/2 holds the same notes as a bar of 4/4, but it is felt and counted in two big beats instead of four.

Why would a composer want that? Because some music moves quickly and feels wrong counted in four: marches, fast dances, Baroque *allegros*, many hymns. Counting "1 + 2 +" with the half note as the beat makes it flow; counting "1 2 3 4" makes it plod. In this app, a cut-time exercise's tempo and clicks count half notes.

```figure
staff: { notes: "C4:q E4:q G4:q E4:q | C5:h G4:h", meter: 2/2, labels: names }
sound: "C4:q E4:q G4:q E4:q C5:h G4:h"
caption: Cut time: two half-note beats in each bar. Count "1 + 2 + | 1 2".
check:
  ask: "How many sixteenth notes fit in one beat of 4/4?"
  choices: ["4", "2", "8"]
  correct: 0
  why: "A quarter note is 2 eighths, and each eighth is 2 sixteenths: **4** sixteenths to a beat, counted 1 e + a."
```

## Fast notes and the hand

Sixteenth notes are where finger evenness really shows. Each note of a run should be the same length and loudness, like beads on a string. Practise sixteenths slowly with the click, counting the syllables, and move up the tempo ladder only when every note is even. Speed comes from evenness, never the other way round. Grouping helps too: think of each group of four as one gesture leading to the next beat.

## Common confusions

- **Replaying a tied note.** The second note is held, not struck.
- **Tie or slur?** Same pitch: tie. Different pitches: slur.
- **Cut time is "twice as fast".** It is counted in half notes; whether it feels fast depends on the tempo.

## In short

A tie joins notes of the same pitch into one sound, often across a bar line; keep counting but do not replay. Sixteenth notes are a quarter of a beat in 4/4, counted "1 e + a". Learn common sixteenth patterns as shapes, and place the dotted eighth's sixteenth on the "a". Cut time (2/2) counts two half-note beats a bar.
