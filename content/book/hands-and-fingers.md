## Fingers have numbers

Piano music talks about your fingers by number, and the numbering is the same for both hands:

| Finger | Number |
| --- | --- |
| Thumb | 1 |
| Index finger | 2 |
| Middle finger | 3 |
| Ring finger | 4 |
| Little finger | 5 |

The thumb is always 1, in both hands. Because your hands are mirror images, that means the numbers run in opposite directions on the keyboard. In the **right hand**, 1 is on the left and 5 on the right. In the **left hand**, 5 is on the left and 1 on the right.

A quick way to learn them: hold up both hands with your palms facing you, wiggle both thumbs and say "one", both index fingers and say "two", and so on. Then press your fingertips together, hand to hand, in order. Ten seconds a day for a week and you will never have to think about it again.

## Why the numbers matter

In printed music you will often see small numbers above or below the notes. These are **fingerings**: suggestions for which finger plays which note. In this app they also appear on the falling notes and on the sheet music.

It is tempting to ignore fingering and use whatever finger is closest. For a single note, that is fine. But music moves, and a bad choice now means getting stuck three notes later with no finger left in the right place. Good fingering is like planning which foot to start on before going up a flight of stairs. Composers, teachers and editors work fingerings out so that the hand stays comfortable and the notes flow, and following them is one of the fastest ways to get better.

> **Follow the fingering.** It looks fussy at first, but a consistent fingering is what lets your hand learn a passage by feel, so you can stop thinking about it.

## A hand position: five fingers, five keys

The first way of playing that every pianist learns is the **five-finger position**: each finger rests over one white key, five neighbouring keys in all, and the hand stays still while the fingers do the work. Nothing moves except the finger that plays.

The most common first position is **middle C position**:

- The **right thumb** on middle C, the other fingers on D, E, F and G.
- The **left thumb** also on middle C, sharing it with the right thumb, and the other left fingers going down over B, A, G and F.

```figure
keys: { notes: "F3 G3 A3 B3 C4 D4 E4 F4 G4", labels: names }
caption: Middle C position. Both thumbs share middle C; the left hand reaches down to F, the right hand up to G.
```

A close cousin is **C position**, where the left hand moves down to the C below, little finger on C and thumb on G, so that both hands play the same five letters an octave apart. You will use both.

You will meet several positions named after their lowest note: **C position**, **G position** (right thumb on G), **F position** and others. A position is just a home base. While you are in it you never have to look for a key, because there is one finger waiting over each.

## How the hand should feel

There is a whole branch of technique about this, but four simple ideas cover most of what a beginner needs:

1. **Curved fingers.** Let your hand hang by your side, relaxed. Notice the soft curve your fingers make. Bring that same shape up to the keys, as if holding a small ball.
2. **Play on the fingertips**, a little behind the nail, not on the flat pad. The thumb plays on its side corner.
3. **Loose wrist and shoulders.** Tension is the enemy of speed and evenness. If your shoulders creep up, drop them.
4. **Sit at the right height and distance.** Forearms roughly level with the keys, elbows a little in front of your body, feet flat. Middle C should be in front of your middle.

## Two hands, two jobs

The right hand and the left hand often do different jobs. Very often the **right hand plays the tune** and the **left hand plays the bass or the chords** underneath. Most people's right hand is stronger and quicker at first, and the left hand needs extra attention. That is completely normal, and it is why this app scores each hand separately: so you can see exactly which one needs the work.

It is also why the first exercises in this app use both hands from the start, in simple ways: mirror images, hands taking turns, one hand holding while the other moves. The sooner your hands learn to work together, the more natural it feels.

## Common confusions

- **Numbering the left hand from the left.** In the left hand the little finger is 5 and it is on the left. Thumb is always 1.
- **A position is not a rule for ever.** Five-finger positions are where everyone starts, but real music soon leaves them, by crossing the thumb under or the hand moving to a new place. You will learn those moves in the technique branch.
- **Pressing too hard.** A piano key needs much less force than most beginners use. Let the weight of the finger do it.

## In short

Thumbs are 1 and little fingers are 5 in both hands. Fingerings in the music tell you which finger to use, and following them lets your hand learn by feel. A five-finger position puts one finger over each of five keys, so nothing needs to be searched for. Keep the hand curved, the wrist loose, and let each hand do its own job.
