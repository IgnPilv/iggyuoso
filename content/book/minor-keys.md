## A different home on the same notes

Play the white keys from C to C: C major. Now play the very same white keys, but from A up to A: A B C D E F G A. Nothing has changed except the starting note, yet the scale sounds completely different: darker, more serious, sometimes mysterious.

```figure

caption: A to A on white keys: the A natural minor scale.
```

That scale is **A minor**, and specifically the **natural minor** scale (the "plain" version; the next chapter adds two variations). It uses exactly the same seven notes as C major. What makes it minor is that the home note, the tonic, is now A, and the pattern of tones and semitones counted from A is different.

## The minor pattern

Count the steps from A: A–B tone, B–C **semitone**, C–D tone, D–E tone, E–F **semitone**, F–G tone, G–A tone. So the natural minor scale goes:

**T S T T S T T**

Compare the major scale, **T T S T T T S**. The semitones sit in different places, between degrees 2–3 and 5–6 instead of 3–4 and 7–8. The crucial difference is near the bottom: from A up to C is only a **minor 3rd** (three semitones), whereas from C up to E in the major scale is a **major 3rd** (four). As you learned in the last chapter, that 3rd decides major or minor.

> **Natural minor = T S T T S T T.** Its 3rd degree is a minor 3rd above the tonic. Compared with major, degrees 3, 6 and 7 are a semitone lower.

That last point gives you a second way to build a minor scale: take the major scale with the same home note, and lower its 3rd, 6th and 7th degrees. C major is C D E F G A B C; C natural minor is C D **E♭** F G **A♭ B♭** C.

## Relatives and parallels

Two kinds of family link major and minor keys, and it helps to have names for both:

- **Relative** keys share the **same notes and key signature** but have different home notes. A minor is the **relative minor** of C major; C major is the **relative major** of A minor.
- **Parallel** keys share the **same home note** but have different notes. C major and C minor are parallel keys.

Every major key has a relative minor, and you can always find it the same way:

> **The relative minor starts on the 6th degree of the major scale**, or, counting down, **three semitones (a minor 3rd) below** the major tonic.

| Major key | Relative minor | Key signature |
| --- | --- | --- |
| C major | A minor | none |
| G major | E minor | one sharp (F♯) |
| F major | D minor | one flat (B♭) |
| D major | B minor | two sharps |
| B♭ major | G minor | two flats |

## One signature, two keys: which is it?

Because relative keys share a key signature, a key signature alone cannot tell you whether a piece is in the major or the minor. One sharp might mean G major or E minor. To decide, look for the home note:

1. **The last note**, especially the last bass note, is very often the tonic.
2. **The first note or chord** often is too.
3. **Raised 7th degrees**: in minor pieces, the 7th degree is often sharpened with an accidental (you will see why in the next chapter). A piece with one sharp and lots of D♯s is almost certainly in E minor.

## The character of minor

Minor keys have been used for laments, ghost stories and storms, but also for dances, folk songs and fiery showpieces. Much folk music around the world, and a great deal of rock and film music, lives in minor. What minor reliably gives is a feeling of depth and shadow compared with major. A good exercise is to play a familiar major tune in the parallel minor, lowering every 3rd, 6th and 7th degree. *Twinkle Twinkle* in C minor sounds like a mournful lullaby.

At the keyboard, minor five-finger positions are just major positions with the middle finger moved down a semitone: C D E♭ F G for C minor, A B C D E for A minor. That moved finger is the 3rd, the note that makes it minor.

## Common confusions

- **Minor means black keys.** A minor uses only white keys.
- **Relative vs parallel.** Relative: same notes, different home (C major / A minor). Parallel: same home, different notes (C major / C minor).
- **Reading the key from the signature alone.** Check the last bass note and look for raised 7ths.

## In short

The natural minor scale runs T S T T S T T and has a minor 3rd above its tonic. It is the major scale with degrees 3, 6 and 7 lowered. Every major key has a relative minor on its 6th degree (a minor 3rd below), sharing its key signature; parallel keys share a tonic instead. To tell relatives apart, look at the last bass note and for raised 7ths.
