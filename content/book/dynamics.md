## The instrument named after loud and soft

The full name of your instrument is the **pianoforte**, Italian for "soft-loud". When it was invented, around 1700 in Florence by Bartolomeo Cristofori, its great novelty was exactly that: unlike the harpsichord before it, whose strings are plucked at the same strength however you press, the piano's hammers hit harder when you press faster. For the first time a keyboard player could play softly and loudly, and everything in between, just by touch. The name was later shortened to *piano*, which ironically means only "soft".

The loudness of music is called its **dynamics**. Dynamics are one of the most powerful tools you have for making music sound alive rather than mechanical. A tune played at one flat loudness is like a sentence read in a monotone. The same notes with a gentle beginning, a fuller middle and a soft ending suddenly sound like someone speaking.

## The dynamic marks

Dynamics are written below the staff (between the staves in piano music) in bold italic letters, abbreviations of Italian words:

| Mark | Italian | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *pp* | pianissimo | very soft |
| *p* | piano | soft |
| *mp* | mezzo piano | moderately soft |
| *mf* | mezzo forte | moderately loud |
| *f* | forte | loud |
| *ff* | fortissimo | very loud |

*Mezzo* means "half" or "middle". *Mf* is the everyday, comfortable middle loudness, and if a piece has no marking at the start, that is roughly where to play. Composers sometimes use *ppp* and *fff* for extremes.

A mark applies from where it appears until the next mark. You do not have to keep being reminded: if the music says *p*, play softly until you see something else.

> **p = soft, f = loud**, and *m* (mezzo) softens either one towards the middle: *pp p mp mf f ff*. A mark lasts until the next one.

## Loud and soft are relative

There is no exact volume for *forte*. It depends on the piano, the room, the piece and the other marks around it. What matters is the **difference**: *f* must be clearly louder than the *p* before it. On a quiet digital piano at night, your *ff* might be a real piano's *mf*, and that is fine as long as the contrast is there. This app judges dynamics the same way: it listens for a clear change between sections, not a fixed number.

## How a pianist plays louder

This is the surprising part for most beginners. Loudness on the piano does **not** come from pressing harder into the key once it is down. It comes from **how fast the key goes down**. A fast key throws the hammer at the string faster, and the note is louder. A slow key gives a soft note.

- For **forte**, let more of the arm's weight drop into the key, so it goes down quickly. Stay loose. Banging with a stiff hand gives a harsh sound and tires you out.
- For **piano**, start with the finger already touching the key and press it down gently and slowly, but all the way, so the note still speaks.

After the key is down, pressing harder changes nothing. Once the hammer has struck, the note's loudness is decided. That is why holding a key "harder" does not make the note grow.

Your MIDI keyboard measures key speed too. In MIDI it is called **velocity**, a number from 1 to 127 sent with every note. That is how this app can hear your dynamics.

## Echoes, and getting gradually louder

The simplest use of dynamics is the **echo**: a phrase played loudly and then repeated softly, as if bouncing back from a hill. Baroque composers loved this effect, which is sometimes called **terraced dynamics**: sudden steps between loud and soft, like terraces on a hillside.

The other kind of change is gradual. A **crescendo** (Italian for "growing") means getting louder bit by bit; a **diminuendo** or **decrescendo** means getting softer. They are written as the words, abbreviated *cresc.* and *dim.*, or as long thin wedges called **hairpins**: opening out for a crescendo, closing up for a diminuendo. You will practise these in the technique branch.

## Where dynamics come from

Dynamics are not decoration added after the "real" notes. They usually follow the shape of the music. A rising tune tends to grow louder; a falling one, softer. The climax of a phrase, its highest or most intense moment, is usually its loudest point, and endings are often soft. When there are no marks at all, as in much music written before about 1750, players shape the dynamics themselves from the music. Learning to hear where a phrase is going is part of becoming musical, and it starts here.

## Common confusions

- **Louder by pressing harder after the note sounds.** Only key speed matters.
- **Soft meaning timid.** A soft note still needs a clear, even sound. Press all the way down.
- **Getting faster when getting louder.** Crescendos tempt you to speed up, diminuendos to slow down. Keep the tempo and change only the loudness.

## In short

The pianoforte was named for its ability to play soft and loud. Dynamics are marked *pp p mp mf f ff*, each lasting until the next, and judged relative to each other. Loudness comes from how fast the key goes down, not how hard you push afterwards. Echoes jump between levels; crescendo and diminuendo change gradually. Good dynamics follow the shape of the music.
