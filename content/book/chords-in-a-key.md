## The family of chords in a key

A key is a family of seven notes. Take each note of the scale in turn as a root and stack two 3rds on it, using only notes of the scale, and you get seven triads: the **diatonic chords** of the key (*diatonic* means "belonging to the scale"). In C major:

| Degree | Root | Triad | Quality | Numeral |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | C | C E G | major | I |
| 2 | D | D F A | minor | ii |
| 3 | E | E G B | minor | iii |
| 4 | F | F A C | major | IV |
| 5 | G | G B D | major | V |
| 6 | A | A C E | minor | vi |
| 7 | B | B D F | diminished | vii° |

Because every major scale has the same pattern of tones and semitones, every major key has the same pattern of chord qualities: **major, minor, minor, major, major, minor, diminished**. In G major the chords are G, Am, Bm, C, D, Em, F♯°. In F major: F, Gm, Am, B♭, C, Dm, E°. Learn the pattern once and you know the chords of every major key.

> **Diatonic triads in a major key: I ii iii IV V vi vii°.** Upper case = major, lower case = minor, ° = diminished. The pattern is the same in every major key.

## The three primary chords

Of the seven, three do most of the work in simple music: **I, IV and V**, the **primary triads**, built on the tonic, subdominant and dominant. You met them in an early chapter. Now you can see why they are so useful:

- All three are **major**: strong and clear.
- Together they contain **every note of the scale**. I has C E G, IV has F A C, V has G B D. That covers C D E F G A B. So any note of a melody in C major belongs to at least one of them.
- They stand in a strong relationship to each other: IV and V are a 5th below and a 5th above the tonic, the two pillars of the key.

That is why thousands of folk songs, hymns, blues and early rock-and-roll songs use nothing but I, IV and V. With these three you can harmonise almost any simple tune.

## The jobs: home, departure, tension

Musicians often describe chords by their **function**, the job they do in the key. In the simplest picture there are three jobs:

- **Tonic** function (**I**): home, rest, stability.
- **Subdominant** function (**IV**): moving away from home, a gentle departure.
- **Dominant** function (**V**): tension, the furthest point from home, which wants to resolve back to I.

A huge amount of music is just these three jobs in a cycle: home, away, tension, home. **I – IV – V – I**.

```figure

caption: I – IV – V – I. Home, departure, tension, home.
```

The secondary chords join in by sharing the jobs. **vi** shares two notes with I (A C E and C E G) and can stand in for it; **ii** shares two notes with IV (D F A and F A C) and often does IV's job, especially before V; **iii** and **vii°** lean towards I and V. A later chapter explores ii, iii and vi properly.

## Why V pulls to I

Look inside the V chord in C: G, B, D. It contains **B**, the leading note, a semitone below C, which pulls up strongly to C. It also has D, which tends to fall to C. And its root, G, falls a 5th to C, the strongest bass movement in music. So V carries three different pulls towards the tonic at once. When V moves to I, all that tension is released, and we hear a satisfying arrival. Adding a 7th to V (G B D **F**) adds a fourth pull: F wants to fall to E. That is the **dominant 7th**, and it gets its own chapter.

## Chords in minor keys

In a minor key the chord pattern is different, and it depends on which version of the scale you use. Using the **harmonic minor** (with the raised 7th), which is what most harmony in minor does for the important chords, A minor gives:

**i** (Am), **ii°** (B°), **III** (C, often with the natural 7th), **iv** (Dm), **V** (E major, thanks to G♯), **VI** (F), **vii°** (G♯°).

The crucial point is the **V**: with the raised 7th, the dominant chord becomes **major** (E G♯ B), so it pulls home to i as strongly as in a major key. The primary triads in minor are **i, iv and V**: minor, minor and major.

## Hearing function

The ear branch asks you to tell I, IV and V apart after a cadence sets the key. A few clues: I sounds settled; V sounds like a question, open and expectant, wanting an answer; IV sounds warm and spacious, like a door opening, not as urgent as V. Listen to the bass: in simple accompaniments it plays the root, and the root's distance from the tonic is a strong clue.

## Common confusions

- **Diatonic chords are only major.** They include minor and diminished chords; the scale decides.
- **"V" means a G chord.** Only in C major. In D major, V is A.
- **The minor V is minor.** In harmonic minor, V is major, because the 7th is raised.

## In short

A triad on each degree gives the seven diatonic chords: I ii iii IV V vi vii° in every major key. The primary triads I, IV and V are major, contain every note of the scale and do the three main jobs: home, departure and tension. V pulls to I through the leading note and its falling root. In minor, the primary triads are i, iv and a major V.
