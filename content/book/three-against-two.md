## Two rhythms at once

So far, when your two hands played together, their rhythms fitted on a shared grid: quarters against eighths, eighths against sixteenths, every note of the slower hand lining up with a note of the faster hand. **Three against two** (written 3:2, also called a **polyrhythm** or cross-rhythm) is different. In the same span of time, one hand plays **three** evenly spaced notes and the other plays **two** evenly spaced notes. Only the first notes coincide. The others fall in between, in a lilting interlocking pattern.

On the page, you will usually see it as **triplet eighths in one hand against ordinary eighths in the other**, or a triplet quarter group against two quarters.

## The exact pattern

Divide one beat into **six** tiny equal slices (the smallest common grid, since 6 can be split into 2s and 3s). The three-note hand plays on slices 1, 3 and 5. The two-note hand plays on slices 1 and 4.

| Slice | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Three-hand | ● | | ● | | ● | |
| Two-hand | ● | | | ● | | |
| What you hear | both | | 3 | 2 | 3 | |

So the combined rhythm is: **together – (pause) – three – two – three – (pause)**, or as durations: long, short, short, long. That's the whole secret.

> **3 against 2**: both hands together, then the three-hand alone, then the two-hand, then the three-hand, then back to "together". Combined rhythm: long – short – short – long.

## Say it before you play it

Musicians use a phrase whose syllables fall exactly on that pattern:

**"Nice cup of tea"**, or **"Not dif-fi-cult"**.

- "**Nice**" = both hands together.
- "**cup**" = the three-hand alone.
- "**of**" = the two-hand alone.
- "**tea**" = the three-hand alone.

Then start again on "Nice". Say it steadily, in a loop, then tap: both hands on "Nice", the right on "cup" and "tea", the left on "of". Once that feels natural, swap hands (left plays three, right plays two), and then put it on the piano with real notes.

## How to practise it

1. **Tap the composite rhythm** on a table, both hands, saying "Nice cup of tea". Slowly.
2. **Hands separately at the same tempo**: the three-hand alone, thinking of the beat; the two-hand alone. Each hand must feel *even* on its own.
3. **Hands together, very slow**, counting the six slices (1 2 3 4 5 6) if you need to.
4. **Speed up gradually**, and as it gets faster stop thinking of slices: feel each hand's own even pulse, the two pulses floating against each other. At speed, 3:2 is a feeling, not a calculation.
5. **Listen** for the giveaway mistakes: the three-hand turning into "long short short" (a lopsided triplet), or the two-hand drifting into the triplet grid ("dotted" instead of straight).

## Where you will meet it

- **Romantic piano music**: Chopin's *Fantaisie-Impromptu* (four against three!), many of his nocturnes, and Brahms, who loved two against three in the hands. Debussy's *Arabesque No. 1* opens with triplets against eighths.
- **Classical**: accompaniments in triplets under a melody that includes plain eighths, or dotted rhythms that must be fitted to triplets.
- **Jazz**: a swing feel lives between straight and triplet eighths, and pianists often play triplet figures against a straight left hand.
- **African and Afro-Cuban music**: three-against-two (and the related six-against-four) is the foundation of many drumming traditions, where it is felt bodily as a dance, never counted.
- **Hemiola**: a close relative in which the rhythm shifts between groupings of three and two over a longer span (a later chapter).

## Common confusions

- **Three against two means three notes per beat in both hands.** The hands have different numbers of notes in the same time.
- **It's random, just play roughly.** It has an exact, repeatable pattern: 1–3–4–5 on a grid of six.
- **Count it forever.** Count at first; at speed, feel two independent pulses.

## In short

Three against two means one hand plays three even notes while the other plays two in the same span. On a grid of six slices, the three-hand plays on 1, 3, 5 and the two-hand on 1, 4, giving the composite "Nice cup of tea". Practise it tapped, then hands separately, then together slowly, then faster until each hand feels its own even pulse. It appears in Romantic piano music, jazz and African drumming.
