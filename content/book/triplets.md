## Splitting the beat in three

So far every beat has been split into **twos**: a quarter into two eighths, an eighth into two sixteenths. But a beat can also be split into **three** equal parts. Three eighth notes played in the time of one quarter note (that is, in the time normally taken by two eighths) are called a **triplet**, or more exactly an **eighth-note triplet**.

A triplet is written as three eighth notes beamed together with a small **3** over or under the beam (sometimes with a bracket). The 3 means "three in the time of two".

## Counting triplets

Because the beat now has three parts, it needs three syllables. Common choices are:

- **1 - trip - let, 2 - trip - let**
- **1 - la - li, 2 - la - li**
- or simply the word **"tri-po-let"** or **"straw-ber-ry"** on each beat.

Whatever you choose, the first syllable lands on the beat and the three are perfectly **even**. The danger with triplets is turning them into something else: two quick notes and a long one, or a long one and two quick ones, which sound like dotted rhythms or eighth-and-two-sixteenths. A good test is to tap the beat with your foot and say "straw-ber-ry" evenly against it.

> **A triplet** = three equal notes in the time of two, marked with a 3. Count "**1** trip-let **2** trip-let", with the beat on the first note of each group.

## Twos and threes side by side

The interesting part is switching between them. Play two eighths on one beat, then a triplet on the next: "1 and, 2 trip-let". The beat stays exactly the same; only its division changes. Musicians practise this switching a lot, because it is easy to let the triplet drag or the eighths rush. Later you will even play twos in one hand against threes in the other ("three against two"), a famous challenge with its own chapter.

## Triplets and character

Triplets give music a rolling, flowing feel: think of the gentle rocking of a barcarolle, or the famous triplet accompaniment of Beethoven's *Moonlight Sonata*, which moves in triplets from the first bar to the last. Many blues and rock ballads are built on a steady triplet pulse in the background. A whole piece that divides every beat into threes is better written in a compound meter like 6/8 or 12/8, which you will meet in a later chapter.

There are also **quarter-note triplets** (three quarter notes in the time of two, spanning two beats) and **sixteenth-note triplets**. They follow exactly the same principle: three in the time of two.

## Off-beat eighths

Another way to give rhythm life is to put notes **between** the beats. You count "1 + 2 + 3 + 4 +"; the numbers are **on** the beat, the "+"s are **off** the beat. A rhythm that starts notes on the "+" and rests on the beat, like *rest–note, rest–note*, creates a bouncy, off-balance feel. It is the typical rhythm of reggae guitar, and of many accompaniment patterns where the left hand plays the bass on the beat and the right hand answers on the off-beat ("oom-pah").

The hard part is not rushing: when nothing sounds on the beat, there is a temptation to come in early. Keep the beat going inside (tap your foot on the numbers) and place each note exactly halfway.

## Dotted eighths again

You met the **dotted eighth and sixteenth** with the sixteenth notes: three-quarters of a beat and one quarter. It sounds like a skip: "**long**-a **long**-a". It is very different from a triplet's long-short ("1 – let" with the middle note left out), though the two are easily confused. The dotted rhythm is sharper and more jagged: the short note is a quarter of the beat. In a triplet-based long-short, the short note is a third of the beat, gentler and rounder. That rounder, triplet-based long-short is the basis of **swing**, which you will meet later.

## Common confusions

- **Triplets are faster eighths.** They are a different division of the same beat: three where there were two.
- **Uneven triplets.** All three notes are equal; check with "straw-ber-ry".
- **Coming in early on off-beats.** Feel the silent beat before each off-beat note.

## In short

A triplet puts three equal notes in the time of two, marked with a 3; count "1 trip-let". Twos and threes can alternate while the beat stays steady. Off-beat eighths fall on the "and" between beats and give rhythm bounce. Dotted eighths make a sharp skip, distinct from the rounder triplet long-short that leads to swing.
