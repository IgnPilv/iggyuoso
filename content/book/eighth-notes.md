## Halving the beat again

You already know the family: a whole note splits into two halves, a half note into two quarters. Split a quarter note in two and you get two **eighth notes**. In 4/4, 3/4 and 2/4, where the quarter note is the beat, an eighth note lasts **half a beat**. (British books call it a **quaver**.)

An eighth note looks like a quarter note with a little **flag** on its stem. When two or more eighth notes come together, the flags are usually replaced by a thick line joining their stems, called a **beam**. Beams make rhythms much easier to read, because they show at a glance which notes belong to the same beat.

The matching silence is the **eighth rest**, which looks like a small 7 with a dot on its upper stroke. It lasts half a beat.

## Counting with "and"

If each beat has two eighth notes, counting only the numbers is not enough: you need a word for the second half of each beat. Musicians use **"and"**, often written **+**. A bar of eight eighth notes in 4/4 is counted:

**1 + 2 + 3 + 4 +**, said "one and two and three and four and".

The numbers fall on the beats, where your foot taps; the "and"s fall exactly halfway between, as your foot comes up. That is why the second half of the beat is called the **offbeat** or **upbeat**. Every rhythm in eighth notes can be placed with this count: a note on "1", a note on "+", a quarter note covering "2 +", and so on.

```figure
staff: { notes: "C5:q C5:e C5:e C5:q C5:e C5:e | C5:e C5:e C5:e C5:e C5:h", meter: 4/4 }
sound: "C5:q C5:e C5:e C5:q C5:e C5:e C5:e C5:e C5:e C5:e C5:h"
caption: Count "1 (+) 2 + 3 (+) 4 + | 1 + 2 + 3 (+ 4 +)". Say every syllable; play where a note starts.
```

> **Eighth note = half a beat** (in 2/4, 3/4 and 4/4). Count "**1 + 2 + 3 + 4 +**": numbers on the beat, "and" exactly halfway between.

The secret is to keep saying *every* syllable, even the ones where nothing new starts. When a quarter note sits on "3", you still say "three and" while it sounds. That way the "and"s keep the pulse subdivided in your head, and the eighth notes cannot rush or drag.

## Even eighths

In most classical and folk music, two eighth notes in a beat are exactly **equal**, like a clock ticking twice as fast. This is called **straight** or **even** eighths. The most common fault is to make them uneven without meaning to, long-short or short-long, especially when the fingers find one note easier than the other. Practising with the click, counting out loud, and listening back are the cures. In jazz and some pop, eighth notes are deliberately played long-short, called **swing**; you will meet that much later, and it is written the same way but played differently.

## The big idea: subdivide

Here is the method that makes any rhythm countable, now and later:

1. Find the **shortest note value** in the passage.
2. **Count in that value**: give every one of them a syllable.
3. Longer notes simply last for several syllables.

With quarters and eighths, count "1 + 2 +". When sixteenth notes arrive (a quarter of a beat), you will add two more syllables per beat: "1 e + a". When triplets arrive, "1 trip-let". The principle never changes: chop the beat into equal parts small enough that every note starts on one of them.

## Eighth notes in other meters

In 2/4 and 3/4 eighth notes work exactly the same way: "1 + 2 +" and "1 + 2 + 3 +". Their beams group them by beat, so in 3/4 you will usually see three groups of two. In time signatures with 8 on the bottom, like 3/8 and 6/8, the eighth note itself is the counted unit; those have a chapter of their own later.

## Eighths and your hands

When both hands play, the eighth notes of one hand often sit against quarter or half notes in the other. Count the eighths out loud and you will find that each long note in one hand lines up with a number, while the eighths fill in the "and"s. The most important thing is that notes written in the same column start together. If your two hands drift apart, slow down until they line up, then build the tempo back up with the tempo ladder.

## Common confusions

- **Skipping the "and" when nothing happens on it.** Say it anyway; it is what keeps the timing exact.
- **Rushing eighth notes.** Fast notes feel exciting and tend to speed up. Count and lean on the click.
- **Reading a beam as a tie.** A beam joins separate notes, each played. A tie (a curved line) joins two notes into one held sound.

## In short

An eighth note is half a quarter note: half a beat in 2/4, 3/4 and 4/4. Flags and beams mark them; beams group them by beat. Count "1 + 2 + 3 + 4 +", saying every syllable. To count any rhythm, subdivide into the shortest value and give each part a syllable. Play straight eighths evenly unless the music swings.
